\section{Commodity indices}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Commodity indices and their record: the CRB, S\&P GSCI and Bloomberg indices, commodity ETFs and their energy weight, and the 1970--2006 risk, return and correlation figures Gliner reproduces]\label{def:commodity-indices-risk-return}
\qffield{Statement} The Commodity Research Bureau (CRB) Index ``is composed of 22 commodities and dates back to the 1950s''; the Deutsche Bank ETFs DBA (agriculture: corn, soybeans, sugar, cattle, coffee, cocoa and more) and DBC (energy, precious metals, agriculture and industrial metals) give basket exposure, the caution with DBC being that ``more than 50 percent of the index is energy weighted'' \citep[chap.~10, sec.\ ``CRB Index'', PDF p.~299]{s7-gliner}. ``Commodities have a higher volatility than foreign exchange, equities, and fixed income'', with ``an attractive Sharpe ratio as a relative comparison'' and, before 2008, negative correlation with equities; Table~10.1, from Goldman Sachs's \emph{Commodities as an Asset Class} (July 2007), gives annualised monthly continuously compounded returns, volatilities and correlations of equities, bonds, cash and the S\&P GSCI for 1970--2006, with an equity--GSCI correlation of $-0.04$ (sec.\ ``Return and Volatility'', p.~300).

\qffield{Notation} A production-weighted index (S\&P GSCI) weights commodities by world production value; the Bloomberg Commodity Index (BCOM, formerly DJ-UBS) uses liquidity and production with caps (legacy descriptions, not checked against methodologies).

\qffield{Conditions} The 1970--2006 statistics are one sample from a bank's 2007 note. \emph{Live and back-calculated history} (corrected in the slice-37 follow-up). The investable index ``arguably began in 1991 with the creation of the S\&P GSCI (originally the Goldman Sachs Commodity Index)'', and later indices were launched ``with backfilled history'' \citep[p.~36]{dunsby-nelson-2010}; S\&P Dow Jones Indices, whose pages refused automated access, is reported in search listings to give the headline index's launch as April 1991 (the 11 April 1991 date supplied for this follow-up could not be confirmed from an S\&P page) and to treat values before an index's launch date as hypothetical back-tested performance. On either date, the 1970--1990 part of Gliner's Table~10.1 is back-calculated and 1991--2006 is live; the slice-37 wording ``pre-2000 index history is back-filled'' was wrong. \emph{Variants.} A \emph{spot} index tracks futures prices only; an \emph{excess-return} index adds the roll from rolling futures; a \emph{total-return} index adds interest on fully collateralising the futures (S\&P's description as reported in the same listings). Gliner's table labels the returns ``Annualized Monthly Returns (Continuously Compounded)'' and does not say which GSCI variant Goldman Sachs used, so its 1970--2006 figures should not be read as total-return (or as spot) without the 2007 note, which was not read. ``Post-2008, correlations with risk assets increased significantly'' (legacy) is the financialisation question, not checked here.

\qffield{Practice} Read an index's rulebook for weights, roll schedule and rebalancing before using it as a benchmark; ETF returns differ from spot prices by the roll and fees, which the legacy calls ``contango and roll costs'' (\hyperref[def:contango-backwardation-stocks]{contango and backwardation}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy ``S\&P GSCI \dots\ heavily energy-exposed ($>50\%$)'' moves Gliner's DBC caution to the GSCI; the GSCI's energy weight was not checked. Commodities ``particularly useful as inflation hedges'' is the \texttt{commodity-allocation-inflation} pathway's question, to be measured by horizon and regime.
