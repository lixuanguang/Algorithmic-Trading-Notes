\section{Commodity indices}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Commodity indices and their record: the CRB, S\&P GSCI and Bloomberg indices, commodity ETFs and their energy weight, and the 1970--2006 risk, return and correlation figures Gliner reproduces]\label{def:commodity-indices-risk-return}
\qffield{Statement} The Commodity Research Bureau (CRB) Index ``is composed of 22 commodities and dates back to the 1950s''; the Deutsche Bank ETFs DBA (agriculture: corn, soybeans, sugar, cattle, coffee, cocoa and more) and DBC (energy, precious metals, agriculture and industrial metals) give basket exposure, the caution with DBC being that ``more than 50 percent of the index is energy weighted'' \citep[chap.~10, sec.\ ``CRB Index'', PDF p.~299]{s7-gliner}. ``Commodities have a higher volatility than foreign exchange, equities, and fixed income'', with ``an attractive Sharpe ratio as a relative comparison'' and, before 2008, negative correlation with equities; Table~10.1, from Goldman Sachs's \emph{Commodities as an Asset Class} (July 2007), gives annualised monthly continuously compounded returns, volatilities and correlations of equities, bonds, cash and the S\&P GSCI for 1970--2006, with an equity--GSCI correlation of $-0.04$ (sec.\ ``Return and Volatility'', p.~300).

\qffield{Notation} A production-weighted index (S\&P GSCI) weights commodities by world production value; the Bloomberg Commodity Index (BCOM, formerly DJ-UBS) uses liquidity and production with caps. \emph{BCOM, checked}: target weights combine two-thirds liquidity and one-third production percentages, using five-year averages, then apply annual diversification limits (no commodity above 15 percent, no commodity with its derivatives above 25 percent, no group such as energy above 33 percent, and at least 2 percent per commodity ``as liquidity allows''); the 2026 target weights are energy 29.44, grains 21.15, precious metals 18.84, industrial metals 15.76, softs 9.17 and livestock 5.64 percent, across 23 commodities; BCOM itself is an excess-return index, BCOMTR adds the return on Treasury-bill collateral (the 13-week bill auction high rate) and BCOMSP is a spot version; the roll shifts 20 percent a day over the sixth to tenth business days of the month; values before the launch on 14 July 1998 are ``historical estimations'' \citep[secs.~1.2 with Table~1, 2.2, 2.6, 3.1--3.2 and app.~B, pp.~4--6, 11, 23--25, 31--33 and~40]{bcom-methodology-2026}.

\emph{S\&P GSCI, checked}: weights are contract production weights built from five-year world production averages, subject to total-dollar-value trading and reference-percentage-dollar-weight thresholds (at least 1.00 percent to enter, 0.10 percent to stay); the excess-return index (S\&P GSCI ER) ``is \dots\ calculated based on the contract daily return'' and the total-return index ``reflects the performance of a total return investment in commodities---contract daily return plus the daily interest on the funds hypothetically committed to the investment''; the roll runs from the fifth to the ninth business day of the month at 20 percent a day; the headline index has base date 31 December 1969 (value 100) and launch date 11 April 1991 \citep[PDF pp.~7, 9, 13 and~18, printed pp.~6, 8, 12 and~17; user-supplied browser export]{spdji-gsci-methodology-2026}.

\qffield{Conditions} Figures to 2006; ETF details as of 2013. The 1970--2006 statistics are one sample from a bank's 2007 note. \emph{Live and back-tested history}. S\&P's education note states: ``The S\&P GSCI was launched April 11, 1991. \dots\ All data prior to such date is back-tested hypothetical data'' \citep[PDF p.~6, notes to the exhibits; user-supplied browser export]{spdji-why-gsci-2024}, and the methodology's disclosure says that information before a launch date ``is hypothetical back-tested performance, not actual performance, and is based on the index methodology in effect on the launch date'' \citep[PDF p.~39]{spdji-gsci-methodology-2026}. The investable index ``arguably began in 1991'' and later indices were launched ``with backfilled history'' \citep[p.~36]{dunsby-nelson-2010}.

So Gliner's Table~10.1 (1970--2006) combines back-tested values from January 1970 to 10 April 1991 with live values from 11 April 1991; the boundary falls inside calendar 1991, which is neither wholly back-tested nor wholly live, and the monthly return for April 1991 straddles it. \emph{Variants.} A \emph{spot} index tracks futures prices only; an \emph{excess-return} index adds the roll from rolling futures; a \emph{total-return} index adds interest on fully collateralising the futures (S\&P's ER and TR definitions above; the education note separates the ``S\&P GSCI'' and the ``S\&P GSCI Excess Return (ER)'' by the roll, PDF p.~9).

Gliner's table labels the returns ``Annualized Monthly Returns (Continuously Compounded)'' and does not say which GSCI variant Goldman Sachs used, so its 1970--2006 figures should not be read as total-return (or as spot) without the 2007 note, which was not read. ``Post-2008, correlations with risk assets increased significantly'' is the financialisation question, not checked here.

\qffield{Practice} Read an index's rulebook for weights, roll schedule and rebalancing before using it as a benchmark; ETF returns differ from spot prices by the roll and fees, which one account calls ``contango and roll costs'' (\hyperref[def:contango-backwardation-stocks]{contango and backwardation}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The ``S\&P GSCI \dots\ heavily energy-exposed ($>50\%$)'' moves Gliner's DBC caution to the GSCI; the GSCI's current energy weight was not read as a number (the education note shows it only as a chart, Exhibit~8, PDF p.~8, declining since 2008), while BCOM's 33 percent group cap holds its 2026 energy target at 29.44 percent. Commodities ``particularly useful as inflation hedges'' is the \nolinkurl{commodity-allocation-inflation} pathway's question, to be measured by horizon and regime.
