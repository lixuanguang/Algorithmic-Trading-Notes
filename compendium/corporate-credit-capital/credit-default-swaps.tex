\section{Credit default swaps}

\begin{qfmechanism}[The credit default swap: premium leg paid quarterly, physical settlement against par and its cash equivalent par minus recovery, credit events for sovereigns and corporates, counterparty risk, and what has changed since Gliner's account]\label{mech:cds-contract-credit-events}
\qffield{Statement} A CDS works like insurance on a reference entity's debt: the protection buyer pays a premium, ``quoted at an annual spread, but paid quarterly'' (100~bp a year is 25~bp a quarter, ``usually in March, June, September, and December''); being long credit risk means selling protection and receiving the spread \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``Credit Default Swaps (CDS)'', PDF pp.~272--273]{s7-gliner}. The basic terms are the reference obligation, notional, spread and maturity (p.~273). On a credit event, in Gliner's description, ``the protection buyer stops paying the premium and delivers the reference bond to the protection seller. Then the protection seller makes the protection buyer whole by transferring par (100)'' (p.~273, Figure~9.9).

His two characteristic credit events are \emph{failure to pay} and \emph{restructuring} (postponing, reducing or deferring obligations); for sovereigns, he writes, ``CDS is not automatically triggered during a restructuring event and both protection buyer and seller have a right (but not an obligation) to trigger the CDS'' (p.~273). More than one party can buy protection on the same debt, so CDS notional can exceed the reference obligations (p.~272), and the seller's own default is a counterparty risk (p.~273).

\qffield{Notation} $s$ annual spread; $N$ notional; $R$ recovery as a fraction of par; protection payout $N(1-R)$.

\qffield{Conditions} Gliner's account as of 2013, largely pre-2009 practice. \emph{An illustration under one assumption, not an identity of real settlements}. If the delivered bond is worth $R$ per unit of par and cash settlement pays par minus that same $R$, the two methods give the same payout, $1-R$ (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/macro-pricing-risk-checks.py}, section~12). In practice the values need not coincide: the buyer chooses which deliverable obligation to deliver (the cheapest), cash settlement uses a price fixed by an auction or poll at a particular time, and accrued premium and timing differ; so the payout under one method is not proof of what the other would have paid.

With a delivered bond worth 38 and an auction final price of 40, physical delivery nets 62 and cash settlement 60 per 100. buyer who ``receives par if credit event occurs'' does so only on delivery of the bond; its seller ``pays par minus recovery'' describes the cash equivalent.

\qffield{Practice} Gliner's argument for buying protection, that on \$10 million at 100~bp the investor pays \$100,000 a year ``with almost unlimited upside (up to \$10,000,000)'' (p.~279), overstates the payout: the gain is $N(1-R)$, \$6 million at 40 percent recovery, less premiums paid. He notes that CDS are more often bought to hedge a bond position, the difference between the bond and CDS spreads being the \emph{basis} (p.~279).

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Practice has moved on, by contract coverage}. Gliner's account follows earlier contract practice. ISDA's Big Bang Protocol, open from 12 March to 7 April 2009, supplemented the 2003 Credit Derivatives Definitions with the 2009 Determinations Committees and Auction Settlement Supplement \citep[protocol page and FAQ, retrieved 2026-10-16]{isda-big-bang-2009}. What changed depends on which contracts incorporate it. \emph{Coverage}: adhering parties amended their existing ``Protocol Covered Transactions'' (DC provisions effective from 8 April 2009; the 60- and 90-day backstop dates fully from 20 June 2009); the Supplement applies to later trades that incorporate it, and DTCC applied it to covered trades whether or not a party had adhered; ``Excluded Transactions like LCDS and CDS on ABS'' were outside it, and auction settlement did not apply to ``Covered Non-Auction Transactions''.

\emph{Triggering a covered contract}: a party may ask the DC to determine a credit event, with Publicly Available Information, or ``Alternatively, the party could send a Credit Event Notice and Notice of Publicly Available Information directly to its counterparty'', either within 60 days of the event; if the DC accepts the question and then declines to decide it, the 60-day limit is tolled while it deliberates, and if no member accepts it within two business days it is dismissed and the limit keeps running. So bilateral notices did not end in 2009; DC resolutions bind covered trades, and bilateral notice remains a route, subject to the backstop dates.

\emph{Settlement}: if the DC holds an auction, covered parties ``will not have the option to physically settle prior to the auction, unless agreed bilaterally'', but can submit physical settlement requests into it; in the 2009 FAQ the DC ``will not hold auctions for Restructuring Credit Events'' and may skip illiquid names, in which case the confirmation's fallback settlement method applies (restructuring auctions came with the later Small Bang protocol, whose terms were not read; the Greek restructuring of 2012 was auctioned, below).

\emph{Standard North American contracts}: the same FAQ describes the ``100/500'' Standard North American Corporate transaction, with investment-grade names on a fixed 100~bp coupon and high-yield names on 500~bp quoted as points upfront, quarterly roll dates of 20 March, June, September and December, No Restructuring as the standard (Modified Restructuring electable), and auction hardwiring. ``both buyer and seller have right (not obligation) to trigger'' therefore still describes the notice route for contracts that use it; it is not simply pre-2009 practice.

\emph{Greece.} On 9 March 2012 the ISDA EMEA Determinations Committee ``resolved unanimously'' that a Restructuring Credit Event had occurred with respect to the Hellenic Republic under Section 4.7(a) of the 2003 Definitions (as amended by the July 2009 Supplement), following the exercise of collective action clauses on Greek-law bonds, and an auction was held on 19 March 2012 \citep[releases of 9 and 19 March 2012]{isda-greece-2012}; the auction's final price (21.5, per a republished copy of the results) was not read in an ISDA or administrator document.

The 2014 ISDA Credit Derivatives Definitions \citep[catalogue entry only, not read]{add-cds-contract} are sold by ISDA and were not read; ISDA's release announcing them (read 2026-10-16) describes, without the operative wording, a new credit event ``triggered by a government-initiated bail-in'' for financial reference entities, delivery of the assets into which sovereign debt is converted for sovereign CDS, a standard reference obligation per entity and seniority, and amendments on successors, guarantees, contingent obligations, currency redenomination and restructuring settlement; the Definitions ``do not apply automatically'', the implementation date was the September 2014 roll, and they apply only if referenced in new trades or adopted for existing ones by protocol \citep[pp.~1--2]{isda-2014-release}. A press release is not the contract terms, so how these provisions are worded, and later standard terms outside North America, remain unread.

The adoption protocol itself, published by ISDA on 21 August 2014, amends adhering parties' Protocol Covered Transactions to incorporate the 2014 Definitions from an Implementation Date defined as ``the later of (a) September 22, 2014 and (b) the ISDA NY Business Day after any Cut-off Date'' \citep[PDF pp.~1 and~13]{isda-2014-protocol}, and ISDA's protocol page records that all eight Reference Banks had adhered by 17 September 2014, satisfying the Protocol Effectiveness Condition (retrieved 2026-10-17). That dates the switch; it still does not give the Definitions' operative wording on credit events or deliverable obligations. Bankruptcy as a credit event applies to corporates, not sovereigns, which cannot file for bankruptcy.
