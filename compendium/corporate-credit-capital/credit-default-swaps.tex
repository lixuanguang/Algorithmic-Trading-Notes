\section{Credit default swaps}

\begin{qfmechanism}[The credit default swap: premium leg paid quarterly, physical settlement against par and its cash equivalent par minus recovery, credit events for sovereigns and corporates, counterparty risk, and what has changed since Gliner's account]\label{mech:cds-contract-credit-events}
\qffield{Statement} A CDS works like insurance on a reference entity's debt: the protection buyer pays a premium, ``quoted at an annual spread, but paid quarterly'' (100~bp a year is 25~bp a quarter, ``usually in March, June, September, and December''); being long credit risk means selling protection and receiving the spread \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``Credit Default Swaps (CDS)'', PDF pp.~272--273]{s7-gliner}. The basic terms are the reference obligation, notional, spread and maturity (p.~273). On a credit event, in Gliner's description, ``the protection buyer stops paying the premium and delivers the reference bond to the protection seller. Then the protection seller makes the protection buyer whole by transferring par (100)'' (p.~273, Figure~9.9). His two characteristic credit events are \emph{failure to pay} and \emph{restructuring} (postponing, reducing or deferring obligations); for sovereigns, he writes, ``CDS is not automatically triggered during a restructuring event and both protection buyer and seller have a right (but not an obligation) to trigger the CDS'' (p.~273). More than one party can buy protection on the same debt, so CDS notional can exceed the reference obligations (p.~272), and the seller's own default is a counterparty risk (p.~273).

\qffield{Notation} $s$ annual spread; $N$ notional; $R$ recovery as a fraction of par; protection payout $N(1-R)$.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{An illustration under one assumption, not an identity of real settlements} (qualified in the slice-37 follow-up). If the delivered bond is worth $R$ per unit of par and cash settlement pays par minus that same $R$, the two methods give the same payout, $1-R$ (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/slice37-checks.py}, section~12). In practice the values need not coincide: the buyer chooses which deliverable obligation to deliver (the cheapest), cash settlement uses a price fixed by an auction or poll at a particular time, and accrued premium and timing differ; so the payout under one method is not proof of what the other would have paid. With a delivered bond worth 38 and an auction final price of 40, physical delivery nets 62 and cash settlement 60 per 100 (slice-37 follow-up check script, section~6). The legacy's buyer who ``receives par if credit event occurs'' does so only on delivery of the bond; its seller ``pays par minus recovery'' describes the cash equivalent.

\qffield{Practice} Gliner's argument for buying protection, that on \$10 million at 100~bp the investor pays \$100,000 a year ``with almost unlimited upside (up to \$10,000,000)'' (p.~279), overstates the payout: the gain is $N(1-R)$, \$6 million at 40 percent recovery, less premiums paid. He notes that CDS are more often bought to hedge a bond position, the difference between the bond and CDS spreads being the \emph{basis} (p.~279).

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Practice has moved on} (updated in the slice-37 follow-up). Gliner's account follows earlier contract practice. ISDA's Big Bang Protocol, open from 12 March to 7 April 2009, supplemented the 2003 Credit Derivatives Definitions with the 2009 Determinations Committees and Auction Settlement Supplement: after 8 April 2009, parties wishing to settle by reference to a CDS auction incorporate the Supplement and elect auction settlement, and ``If the DC decides to hold an auction, parties will not have the option to physically settle prior to the auction, unless agreed bilaterally'', though physical settlement requests can be submitted into the auction \citep[protocol page, retrieved 2026-10-16]{isda-big-bang-2009}. So the legacy's ``both buyer and seller have right (not obligation) to trigger'' describes the pre-2009 notice regime; a Determinations Committee now resolves whether a credit event occurred. \emph{Greece.} On 9 March 2012 the ISDA EMEA Determinations Committee ``resolved unanimously'' that a Restructuring Credit Event had occurred with respect to the Hellenic Republic under Section 4.7(a) of the 2003 Definitions (as amended by the July 2009 Supplement), following the exercise of collective action clauses on Greek-law bonds, and an auction was held on 19 March 2012 \citep[releases of 9 and 19 March 2012]{isda-greece-2012}; the auction's final price (21.5, per a republished copy of the results) was not read in an ISDA or administrator document. The fixed-coupon standardisation of contracts and the 2014 ISDA Credit Derivatives Definitions \citep[catalogue entry only, not read]{add-cds-contract} were not checked. Bankruptcy as a credit event applies to corporates, not sovereigns, which cannot file for bankruptcy (legacy; consistent with Gliner's sovereign list, which omits it).
