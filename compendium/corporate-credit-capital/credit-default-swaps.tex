\subsection{Credit default swaps}

\begin{qfmechanism}[The credit default swap: premium leg paid quarterly, physical settlement against par and its cash equivalent par minus recovery, credit events for sovereigns and corporates, counterparty risk, and what has changed since Gliner's account]\label{mech:cds-contract-credit-events}
\qffield{Statement} A CDS works like insurance on a reference entity's debt: the protection buyer pays a premium, ``quoted at an annual spread, but paid quarterly'' (100~bp a year is 25~bp a quarter, ``usually in March, June, September, and December''); being long credit risk means selling protection and receiving the spread \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``Credit Default Swaps (CDS)'', PDF pp.~272--273]{s7-gliner}. The basic terms are the reference obligation, notional, spread and maturity (p.~273). On a credit event, in Gliner's description, ``the protection buyer stops paying the premium and delivers the reference bond to the protection seller. Then the protection seller makes the protection buyer whole by transferring par (100)'' (p.~273, Figure~9.9).

His two characteristic credit events are \emph{failure to pay} and \emph{restructuring} (postponing, reducing or deferring obligations); for sovereigns, he writes, ``CDS is not automatically triggered during a restructuring event and both protection buyer and seller have a right (but not an obligation) to trigger the CDS'' (p.~273). More than one party can buy protection on the same debt, so CDS notional can exceed the reference obligations (p.~272), and the seller's own default is a counterparty risk (p.~273).

\qffield{Notation} $s$ annual spread; $N$ notional; $R$ recovery as a fraction of par; protection payout $N(1-R)$.

\qffield{Conditions} Gliner's account as of 2013, largely pre-2009 practice. \emph{An illustration under one assumption, not an identity of real settlements}. If the delivered bond is worth $R$ per unit of par and cash settlement pays par minus that same $R$, the two methods give the same payout, $1-R$. In practice the values need not coincide: the buyer chooses which deliverable obligation to deliver (the cheapest), cash settlement uses a price fixed by an auction or poll at a particular time, and accrued premium and timing differ; so the payout under one method is not proof of what the other would have paid.

With a delivered bond worth 38 and an auction final price of 40, physical delivery nets 62 and cash settlement 60 per 100. A buyer of protection receives par only on delivery of the bond; ``par minus recovery'' paid by the seller is the cash equivalent.

\qffield{Practice} Gliner's argument for buying protection, that on \$10 million at 100~bp the investor pays \$100,000 a year ``with almost unlimited upside (up to \$10,000,000)'' (p.~279), overstates the payout: the gain is $N(1-R)$, \$6 million at 40 percent recovery, less premiums paid. He notes that CDS are more often bought to hedge a bond position, the difference between the bond and CDS spreads being the \emph{basis} (p.~279).

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Practice has moved on, by contract coverage}. Gliner's account follows earlier contract practice. ISDA's Big Bang Protocol, open from 12 March to 7 April 2009, supplemented the 2003 Credit Derivatives Definitions with the 2009 Determinations Committees and Auction Settlement Supplement \citep[protocol page and FAQ]{isda-big-bang-2009}. What changed depends on which contracts incorporate it. \emph{Coverage}: adhering parties amended their existing ``Protocol Covered Transactions'' (DC provisions effective from 8 April 2009; the 60- and 90-day backstop dates fully from 20 June 2009); the Supplement applies to later trades that incorporate it, and DTCC applied it to covered trades whether or not a party had adhered; ``Excluded Transactions like LCDS and CDS on ABS'' were outside it, and auction settlement did not apply to ``Covered Non-Auction Transactions''.

\emph{Triggering a covered contract}: a party may ask the DC to determine a credit event, with Publicly Available Information, or ``Alternatively, the party could send a Credit Event Notice and Notice of Publicly Available Information directly to its counterparty'', either within 60 days of the event; if the DC accepts the question and then declines to decide it, the 60-day limit is tolled while it deliberates, and if no member accepts it within two business days it is dismissed and the limit keeps running. So bilateral notices did not end in 2009; DC resolutions bind covered trades, and bilateral notice remains a route, subject to the backstop dates.

\emph{Settlement}: if the DC holds an auction, covered parties ``will not have the option to physically settle prior to the auction, unless agreed bilaterally'', but can submit physical settlement requests into it; in the 2009 FAQ the DC ``will not hold auctions for Restructuring Credit Events'' and may skip illiquid names, in which case the confirmation's fallback settlement method applies (restructuring auctions came with the later Small Bang protocol; the Greek restructuring of 2012 was auctioned, below).

\emph{Standard North American contracts}: the same FAQ describes the ``100/500'' Standard North American Corporate transaction, with investment-grade names on a fixed 100~bp coupon and high-yield names on 500~bp quoted as points upfront, quarterly roll dates of 20 March, June, September and December, No Restructuring as the standard (Modified Restructuring electable), and auction hardwiring. The notice route, under which both buyer and seller have the right but not the obligation to trigger, therefore still applies to contracts that use it; it is not simply pre-2009 practice.

\emph{Greece.} On 9 March 2012 the ISDA EMEA Determinations Committee ``resolved unanimously'' that a Restructuring Credit Event had occurred with respect to the Hellenic Republic under Section 4.7(a) of the 2003 Definitions (as amended by the July 2009 Supplement), following the exercise of collective action clauses on Greek-law bonds, and an auction was held on 19 March 2012 \citep[releases of 9 and 19 March 2012]{isda-greece-2012}; a republished copy of the results gives a final price of 21.5.

The 2014 ISDA Credit Derivatives Definitions \citep[catalogue entry]{add-cds-contract}, as summarised in ISDA's release, add a new credit event ``triggered by a government-initiated bail-in'' for financial reference entities, delivery of the assets into which sovereign debt is converted for sovereign CDS, a standard reference obligation per entity and seniority, and amendments on successors, guarantees, contingent obligations, currency redenomination and restructuring settlement; the Definitions ``do not apply automatically'', the implementation date was the September 2014 roll, and they apply only if referenced in new trades or adopted for existing ones by protocol \citep[pp.~1--2]{isda-2014-release}. A press release is not the contract terms, so the provisions' wording, and later standard terms outside North America, are not covered here.

The adoption protocol itself, published by ISDA on 21 August 2014, amends adhering parties' Protocol Covered Transactions to incorporate the 2014 Definitions from an Implementation Date defined as ``the later of (a) September 22, 2014 and (b) the ISDA NY Business Day after any Cut-off Date'' \citep[PDF pp.~1 and~13]{isda-2014-protocol}, and ISDA's protocol page records that all eight Reference Banks had adhered by 17 September 2014, satisfying the Protocol Effectiveness Condition. That dates the switch; it still does not give the Definitions' operative wording on credit events or deliverable obligations. Bankruptcy as a credit event applies to corporates, not sovereigns, which cannot file for bankruptcy.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:sovereign-credit-defaults]{sovereign credit}; \hyperref[mech:otc-clearing-collateral]{OTC clearing and collateral}; \hyperref[meth:equity-macro-leading-indicators]{CDX high yield as an equity indicator}.

\qffield{Further reading} The ISDA CDS standard model \citep{add-cds-model}.

\end{qfmechanism}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Single-name and index CDS: reference entity, notional, spread, maturity and standard dates; fixed coupons and upfront payments; credit events; cheapest-to-deliver and auction settlement; index composition and rolls]\label{def:cds-contract-features}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Single-name CDS.} The buyer of protection pays a spread on the notional, usually quarterly in arrears, until maturity or a credit event; the seller then compensates the buyer for the loss on the reference entity's debt \citep[pp.~372--374]{risk-management-financial-institutions-2023}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Parameters}: reference entity, notional, spread, maturity, the credit events, and the seniority of deliverable obligations.
\item \emph{Dates}: contracts usually mature on 20 March, June, September or December, and five years is the most common maturity (p.~375).
\item \emph{Credit events}: usually failure to pay, restructuring and bankruptcy; restructuring is sometimes excluded in North American contracts (p.~376).
\item \emph{Settlement}: physical delivery of bonds for their face value, or, as is now usual, cash equal to par less the price of the cheapest deliverable bond set in a two-stage auction. After a default the buyer pays the premium accrued since the last payment date (p.~374).
\item \emph{Cheapest to deliver}: several bonds of the same seniority are deliverable but may trade at different prices after default, because claims include accrued interest and reorganisations treat bondholders differently. The buyer delivers the cheapest (p.~376).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Index CDS.} CDX NA IG and iTraxx Europe each reference 125 investment-grade companies and are updated each March and September, dropping names no longer investment grade. Buying index protection is roughly buying protection on every name at the index spread. When a name defaults its notional is paid out and removed, and the annual payment falls in proportion. The index is slightly below the average single-name spread, because high spreads are paid for a shorter expected time \citep[pp.~376--377]{risk-management-financial-institutions-2023}.

\emph{Fixed coupons.} Standard contracts and indices trade like bonds. A fixed coupon and recovery rate are specified, the market quotes a spread, and the present value of the difference between them changes hands at the trade date: the buyer pays if the spread exceeds the coupon, the seller if not (p.~377). Hull gives the price as $P=100-100\,D\,(s-c)$, with $D$ the risky duration \citep[p.~596]{s7-hulloptions}. The 2009 protocols and the 2014 Definitions that standardised these terms are covered with their sources in \hyperref[mech:cds-contract-credit-events]{the CDS mechanism entry}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Hull's index example reproduced} (\texttt{trading\_notes.numerical.credit\_risk\_analysis.credit\_cds\_securitisation\_checks}). Protection of 800,000 on each of 125 names at 166 basis points costs 1,660,000 a year. Each default cuts the payment by 13,280 and triggers the usual payout on that name.

\emph{Upfront on a standard index trade} \citep[Example~25.1]{s7-hulloptions}. iTraxx is quoted at 0.345 percent against a coupon of 0.406 percent, with quarterly payments, 4 percent rates and 40 percent recovery. The risky duration is 4.447 and the price 100.27. Since the spread is below the coupon, the seller of protection pays the buyer 0.27 per 100 at the start, and the buyer then pays the 0.406 percent coupon.

\qffield{Conditions} Hull's descriptions are of standard terms as of 2021--2023; regional standard terms and the current Definitions' wording were not read.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating the quoted spread as the running payment on a fixed-coupon contract. Forgetting the accrued premium on default. Assuming every bond of the reference entity is deliverable.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mech:cds-contract-credit-events]{CDS mechanism and credit events}; \hyperref[meth:cds-pricing]{pricing}; \hyperref[meth:cds-strategies]{strategies}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Pricing a CDS: the premium and protection legs, risk-neutral hazard rates, the par spread, upfront and mark-to-market values, and the role of recovery]\label{meth:cds-pricing}
\qffield{Statement} A CDS is priced by equating the present values of its two legs under risk-neutral default probabilities \citep[pp.~689--691]{risk-management-financial-institutions-2023}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{premium leg} is the spread times the survival-weighted, discounted payment dates, plus accrued premium paid on default. Per unit of spread this is the \emph{risky duration} (or risky annuity);
\item the \emph{protection leg} is the discounted expected payout $1-R$ at each possible default time.
\end{itemize}
The par spread is protection divided by risky duration. A contract struck at another spread is worth the difference times the risky duration (p.~691). Factors: the hazard rate and its term structure, recovery, the risk-free curve, and the payment and default timing. Approximately, $s\approx\lambda(1-R)$ (p.~380).

\emph{Implied hazard and recovery.} Hazard rates are implied from quoted spreads as volatilities are from option prices. Provided the same recovery is used to imply the hazard and to value the contract, the value is not very sensitive to recovery, since implied default probabilities scale with $1/(1-R)$ and payoffs with $1-R$. A binary CDS, which pays a fixed amount, is sensitive \citep[pp.~594--595]{s7-hulloptions}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Hull's Appendix~K reproduced}: hazard 2 percent, rates 5 percent, recovery 40 percent, annual payments, defaults at mid-year.
\begin{itemize}
\item Survival probabilities are 0.9802, 0.9608, 0.9418, 0.9231 and 0.9048. Table~K.1 prints year 4 as 0.9321; Table~K.2 has 0.9231, which is $e^{-0.08}$.
\item The premium leg is $4.0728s$, plus accrual of $0.0422s$: $4.1150s$. The protection leg is 0.0506.
\item The par spread is $0.0506/4.1150=123$ basis points, close to the rule-of-thumb 120.
\item A contract struck at 150 basis points is worth $4.1150\times0.015-0.0506=0.0111$ to the seller.
\item A binary CDS paying 1 has a protection leg of 0.0844 and a spread of 205 basis points.
\item A quoted spread of 100 basis points implies a hazard of 1.63 percent \citep[p.~594]{s7-hulloptions}.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Recovery} (original extension). Implying the hazard from 123 basis points at 30 percent recovery gives 1.71 percent instead of 2 percent. The 150 basis point contract is then worth 0.0112 rather than 0.0111: recovery barely matters, provided it is used consistently.

\qffield{Conditions} The simple model treats default as independent of rates and of the seller's own credit; counterparty risk and the CDS--bond basis are outside it.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Implying hazards with one recovery and valuing with another. Ignoring the accrued premium. Using the quoted spread, not the coupon, as the running payment on a standard contract.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:cds-contract-features]{contract features}; \hyperref[meth:risky-bond-cva]{risky bonds}; \hyperref[meth:cds-strategies]{strategies}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[CDS strategies: hedging and taking credit exposure, the CDS--bond basis, single-name against index trades, and curve trades sized by Credit01 with their jump-to-default risk]\label{meth:cds-strategies}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Exposure.} Buying protection is short credit: it hedges a bond or loan, or expresses a view that spreads will widen. Selling protection is long credit without funding a bond. A bond hedged with a CDS earns roughly the risk-free rate, so the CDS spread should be close to the bond's par yield spread \citep[p.~378]{risk-management-financial-institutions-2023}.

\emph{Basis.} The CDS--bond basis is the CDS spread less the bond yield spread. It is not an exact arbitrage. The cheapest-to-deliver option and restructuring clauses push it up; counterparty risk in the CDS and the exclusion of accrued interest from the payout push it down; bonds above par tend to give a negative basis. Before 2007 it was positive, about 16 basis points in 2004--2005; in the crisis it was at times very negative but hard to arbitrage for lack of liquidity; since then it has usually been small and negative (pp.~379--380). A negative-basis trade buys the bond and buys protection.

\emph{Index against single names.} An index trade takes diversified exposure to the market; long the index and short a single name, or the reverse, isolates one company's spread relative to the market (Interpretation).

\emph{Curve trades.} Comparing the CDS curve with a curve implied by the issuer's bonds can suggest the CDS curve is too flat \citep[p.~13]{berd-mashal-wang-2004}:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{bearish steepener} sells short-dated protection and buys long-dated protection in equal notionals; it gains if spreads widen or steepen;
\item a \emph{bullish steepener} uses notionals that make the trade Credit01-neutral, so parallel moves matter little and it gains if the front end tightens more.
\end{itemize}
Credit01 is the change in value for a one basis point spread change, approximately notional times risky duration (Interpretation).

\emph{Other markets} (Interpretation). The same credit appears in loans, equity and convertibles. A structural model turns the equity price and volatility into an implied spread (\hyperref[mod:structural-reduced-form-credit]{structural models}); if the CDS trades well above it, selling protection and selling equity bets that the two will converge (capital-structure arbitrage). Loan CDS and convertibles allow the same comparison against loans and equity-linked bonds. Each trade depends on a model linking the markets, and the link can break.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Larkspur} (original): flat hazard 2 percent, recovery 40 percent, rates 5 percent; risky durations 4.11 at 5 years and 7.01 at 10 years.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Credit01-neutral steepener}: buy 10m of 10-year protection and sell $10\times7.01/4.11=17.05$m of 5-year. To first order a parallel 50 basis point widening nets zero. The 10-year widening by 50 basis points alone gains 0.351m; the 5-year alone loses 0.351m.
\item \emph{The cost}: the trade is net short 7.05m of protection, so an immediate default loses $7.05\times0.6=4.23$m. Credit01 neutrality is not default neutrality.
\item \emph{Equal notionals} of 10m: the trade is net long 29.0k of risky duration per unit of spread, gaining 0.145m on a parallel 50 basis point widening, with no jump-to-default exposure.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Equity against CDS} (original, using Hull's Example~17.4 firm). The Merton-implied spread is 124 basis points. A market CDS at 200 basis points is 76 basis points rich relative to the equity, but the gap is only as reliable as the model's asset volatility.

\emph{Basis} (original). With the CDS at 200 basis points and the bond spread at 240, buying the bond and protection locks in about 40 basis points a year if the bond can be financed at the risk-free rate. If funding costs 40 basis points more, the trade earns nothing.

\emph{Choice.} A view that the issuer's near-term risk is overpriced, with no view on default itself, suits the equal-notional trade. A view only on the shape, with no view on direction, suits the Credit01-neutral trade, accepting the jump-to-default loss.

\qffield{Conditions} Durations are first-order measures; large spread moves change them. Funding, collateral and the cheapest-to-deliver option decide whether a basis trade earns its apparent spread.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Calling a Credit01-neutral trade hedged. Treating a negative basis as free money without financing costs. Ignoring index rolls and composition changes.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:cds-pricing]{CDS pricing}; \hyperref[meth:credit-spread-term-structure]{credit curves}; \hyperref[mech:cds-contract-credit-events]{contract mechanics}.

\end{qfmethod}
