\section{Exchange-rate mechanics, parity and determination}

These entries cover the foreign exchange market and its arithmetic (quotes, real rates, cross and forward rates, bid--offer spreads, arbitrage and the value of a forward), then what determines exchange rates: regimes and capital flows, the parity conditions, fair value and forecasts, the carry trade, and payment flows, policy and crises. \emph{Notation}: following EIDM, A/B is the price of one unit of B (the \emph{base} currency) in units of A (the \emph{price} currency), so USD/EUR 1.30 means 1.30 dollars per euro; the market code for the same quote is EURUSD. Numbers are checked in \texttt{notes/artefacts/fx-mechanics-determination/fx-checks.py}.

\begin{qfdefinition}[The foreign exchange market and real exchange rates: functions and participants, size, quotes and their direction, percentage changes, and nominal against real exchange rates]\label{def:fx-market-real-exchange-rates}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Functions} \citep[chap.~9, sec.~2, pp.~473--484]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}: the FX market converts currencies for trade and investment, lets participants hedge future currency flows with forwards, swaps and options, and lets speculators take positions. \emph{Participants} (pp.~478--482): the \emph{sell side} (dealers, mostly large banks) and the \emph{buy side}: corporations, real-money investors (pension funds, insurers, mutual funds), leveraged accounts (hedge funds, CTAs), retail, governments, central banks (intervention and reserve management) and sovereign wealth funds. \emph{Size}: global turnover was \$9.6 trillion a day in April 2025 \citep[preliminary results]{bis-triennial-2025}.

\emph{Quotes} (pp.~484--488). An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. A \emph{direct} quote uses the domestic currency as the price currency, an \emph{indirect} quote the foreign currency; both are in common use, so the base and price currencies must be read off each quote. A two-sided quote is a bid and an offer for the base currency; most rates are quoted to four decimals, yen rates to two. A rise in USD/EUR is a euro appreciation; the dollar's depreciation in percentage terms is different, because $1/(1+x)-1\neq-x$.

\emph{Real exchange rates} (pp.~468--470). The real rate measures the relative price of foreign goods:
\[
q_{d/f}=S_{d/f}\,\frac{P_f}{P_d},
\]
rising when the nominal rate or foreign prices rise and falling when domestic prices rise. A higher real rate (with the domestic currency as price currency) means foreign goods cost more in real terms. Real rates are indexes, not traded prices; they can drift for years, which is why they matter for trade and long-run value.

\qffield{Notation} $S_{d/f}$ nominal spot, domestic currency per unit of foreign; $P_d,P_f$ price levels (CPIs); $q_{d/f}$ real rate.

\qffield{Conditions} Participant descriptions follow EIDM's 2013 text; market shares and turnover are from the 2025 survey and change between surveys.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced}:
\begin{itemize}
\item USD/EUR rising from 1.2500 to 1.3000 is a 4.00 percent euro appreciation but a 3.85 percent dollar depreciation (p.~487);
\item a bid--offer of 12.4020/12.4060 MXN/USD inverts to 0.08061/0.08063 USD/MXN, the new bid being the inverse of the old offer (Example~9-4, pp.~487--488);
\item a 10 percent rise in GBP/EUR with euro-area prices up 5 percent and U.K.\ prices up 2 percent raises the U.K.\ buyer's real rate by about 13 percent (13.24 percent exactly) (p.~469).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Reading a quote without identifying its base currency. Treating an appreciation and a depreciation of the same size in percent as symmetric. Treating a real exchange rate as a tradable price.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:cross-forward-rates]{cross and forward rates}; \hyperref[def:fx-market-structure]{FX market structure}; \hyperref[def:us-dollar-dxy-sdr]{the dollar in global macro}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Cross rates and forward rates: computing a cross rate from two quotes, forward points and percentage premiums, covered interest arbitrage and the forward rate, and money-market day counts]\label{meth:cross-forward-rates}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Cross rates} \citep[chap.~9, sec.~3.2, pp.~488--492]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}. Given A/B and B/C, the cross rate is $A/C=(A/B)\times(B/C)$; a quote may need inverting first so that the common currency cancels. A cross quote inconsistent with its components offers \emph{triangular arbitrage}.

\emph{Forward points} (sec.~3.3, pp.~492--494) are the forward rate minus spot, scaled to the last decimal of the spot quote ($\times10{,}000$, or $\times100$ for yen). Positive points mean the base currency is at a \emph{forward premium}, negative at a \emph{discount}. The forward rate is spot plus points$/10{,}000$, or spot times $(1+\text{percentage premium})$. Points grow with maturity and with the interest differential.

\emph{Covered interest parity} (pp.~494--496). A domestic deposit and a foreign deposit hedged with a forward are both riskless, so they must earn the same:
\[
(1+i_d)=S_{f/d}(1+i_f)\frac{1}{F_{f/d}},\qquad F_{f/d}=S_{f/d}\frac{1+i_f}{1+i_d}.
\]
Regardless of quoting convention, the higher-interest currency trades at a forward discount and the lower-interest currency at a premium (p.~496). A forward that breaks the relation is an arbitrage: borrow in the currency whose hedged return is lower, convert, deposit and hedge. With money-market rates the interest factors are $1+i\tau$, each with its own day count (act/360 or act/365) for the same calendar period (\hyperref[mod:fx-carry-trade]{carry conventions}).

\qffield{Notation} $S_{f/d}$, $F_{f/d}$ spot and forward, foreign currency per domestic unit (EIDM's derivation); $i_d,i_f$ rates for the period; $\tau$ accrual fraction.

\qffield{Conditions} CIP holds closely among liquid currencies with free capital flows but not exactly: since 2008 the cross-currency basis has been non-zero, especially across quarter-ends (\hyperref[ev:cip-deviations]{CIP deviations}). Forward points quoted by dealers have their own bid--offer.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced}:
\begin{itemize}
\item CAD/EUR $=1.0460\times1.2880=1.3472$; JPY/CAD $=85.50/1.0460=81.74$; a dealer quoting 82.00 offers 0.26 yen per Canadian dollar of arbitrage (pp.~488--489);
\item GBP/EUR $=1.3960/1.5850=0.8808$ (Example~9-5, p.~492);
\item 12-month points of $-26.5$ on spot 1.2875 give 1.28485; 3-month points of $-5.5$ give 1.28695, a discount of 0.043 percent (pp.~492--494);
\item with spot 1.6535 (f/d), a 3.50 percent domestic and 5.00 percent foreign rate, the forward is 1.6775; at a quoted 1.6900 the hedged foreign deposit earns 2.73 percent, so borrowing abroad and depositing at home earns 77 basis points with no capital (p.~495).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Original.} For 90 days with the dollar at 4.30 percent act/360 and sterling at 3.90 percent act/365, a spot of 1.2700 dollars per pound gives a forward of 1.27143 (+14.3 points); the hedged sterling deposit then earns exactly the dollar rate.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Multiplying quotes whose common currency does not cancel. Adding points without the sign or the right scale. Treating the forward premium as a forecast (\hyperref[res:international-parity-conditions]{parity conditions}). Mixing day counts.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:fx-bid-offer-triangular-arbitrage]{bid--offer and triangular arbitrage}; \hyperref[meth:currency-forward-mark-to-market]{forward valuation}; \hyperref[meth:forward-price-value-lifecycle]{forward price and value}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Bid--offer spreads and triangular arbitrage: what drives FX spreads, the two arbitrage constraints on quotes, implied cross bid--offers, and identifying and computing a triangular arbitrage]\label{meth:fx-bid-offer-triangular-arbitrage}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Spreads} \citep[chap.~10, sec.~2, pp.~531--533]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}. A dealer quotes clients a bid and offer for the base currency and lays off the risk in the interbank market. The client spread depends on the interbank spread, which is wider for less traded pairs, outside the overlap of the main trading centres and in volatile markets; on the size of the trade; and on the client relationship and credit. Retail spreads can be hundreds of pips against one or two interbank.

\emph{Arbitrage constraints} (sec.~2.1, pp.~533--535):
\begin{enumerate}
\item a dealer's bid cannot exceed the interbank offer, nor its offer be below the interbank bid;
\item a cross bid cannot exceed the implied cross offer from the component quotes, nor the cross offer be below the implied bid.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Implied cross bid--offers.} Write the trade (``sell JPY, buy EUR'') as a chain of trades in the component pairs and use, for each leg, the side the trader faces: buying a base currency pays the offer, selling it hits the bid. When a component must be inverted, its bid becomes the inverse of its offer. Thus $\text{JPY/EUR}_{\text{offer}}=\text{JPY/USD}_{\text{offer}}\times\text{USD/EUR}_{\text{offer}}$, while $\text{GBP/EUR}_{\text{bid}}=(1/\text{USD/GBP}_{\text{offer}})\times\text{USD/EUR}_{\text{bid}}$.

\emph{Identifying arbitrage}. If a dealer's cross offer is below the implied bid, buy from the dealer and sell through the components; if its bid is above the implied offer, do the reverse. Profit per unit is the gap.

\qffield{Notation} Bid $<$ offer always; a pip is the last quoted decimal (0.0001, or 0.01 for yen).

\qffield{Conditions} Arbitrage requires simultaneous execution at the quoted prices and wholesale access; in practice violations are eliminated almost at once by traders and algorithms (p.~535).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced}:
\begin{itemize}
\item USD/EUR 1.3649/1.3651 and JPY/USD 76.64/76.66 imply JPY/EUR 104.61/104.65 (p.~534);
\item USD/GBP 1.5644/1.5646 inverts to GBP/USD 0.63914/0.63922, implying GBP/EUR 0.8724/0.8726 (p.~535);
\item a dealer's 1.3652/1.3654 against an interbank 1.3649/1.3651 lets anyone buy euros at 1.3651 and sell to the dealer at 1.3652, one pip or \$100 per euro million (p.~533);
\item Example~10-1 (pp.~536--537): SEK/EUR 10.0077/10.0094 and JPY/CAD 85.76/85.80; a dealer quoting JPY/CAD 85.73/85.75 sells Canadian dollars at 85.75, below the implied bid of 85.76: buy from the dealer, sell interbank, 0.01 yen per dollar; a quote of 85.74/85.81 brackets the implied rates and offers none.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Using mid rates to test for arbitrage. Forgetting that inverting swaps bid and offer. Ignoring the size and time of day at which a quoted spread applies.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:cross-forward-rates]{cross rates}; \hyperref[def:fx-market-structure]{FX market structure}; \hyperref[def:arbitrage]{arbitrage}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Currency forwards: spot, forward and forward premium, all-in rates, closing out with an offsetting forward, the cash flow at settlement and its present value]\label{meth:currency-forward-mark-to-market}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Forward premium} \citep[chap.~10, sec.~2.2, pp.~538--542]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}: the forward rate differs from spot by points that reflect the interest differential over the term, from covered interest parity; the \emph{all-in} forward rate is spot plus scaled points, taken on the bid or offer side as the trade requires. At inception a forward is worth zero; afterwards its value changes with the spot rate and with interest rates in both currencies.

\emph{Marking to market} (pp.~542--543), in four steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item create an equal and offsetting forward for the same settlement date (sell what was bought);
\item find its all-in rate for the remaining term: use the bid if the base currency is being sold, the offer if it is being bought;
\item compute the settlement cash flow, the contract size times the difference between the original and offsetting rates, which arises in the price currency because the base-currency amounts net to zero; a gain if the currency one was long has appreciated;
\item discount that cash flow to today at the interest rate of its currency for the remaining term.
\end{enumerate}

\qffield{Notation} Contract size $N$ in the base currency; original rate $F_0$, offsetting all-in rate $F_t$; remaining days $D$; the value of a long base-currency forward is $N(F_t-F_0)/(1+i_{\text{price}}D/360)$.

\qffield{Conditions} Money-market discounting with the stated basis; credit and collateral effects ignored. Libor was the discount rate in EIDM's examples; current practice uses overnight-rate curves.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced}:
\begin{itemize}
\item long GBP 10 million against AUD at 1.6100, closed with three months left when spot is 1.6210/1.6215 and three-month points 130/140: the offsetting sale is at the bid, 1.6340; the settlement cash flow is AUD 240{,}000, worth AUD 237{,}154 today at 4.80 percent (pp.~542--543);
\item Example~10-3 (pp.~544--545): short NZD 10 million at 0.7900 USD/NZD, closed by buying NZD three months forward at the offer, $0.7830-0.0010=0.7820$: USD 80{,}000 at settlement, USD 79{,}938 today at 0.31 percent, discounted in dollars because the cash flow is in dollars.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Using the wrong side of the quote. Discounting at the rate of the wrong currency. Treating the forward's value as the undiscounted settlement difference.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:cross-forward-rates]{forward rates}; \hyperref[meth:currency-hedging-programme]{hedge ratios and rolling}; \hyperref[meth:forward-price-value-lifecycle]{forward price and value}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Exchange-rate regimes and their effects: the ideal regime, the spectrum from dollarisation to free floating, effects on trade through elasticities and absorption, capital flows, and the objectives of capital restrictions]\label{def:exchange-rate-regimes-capital-flows}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{The ideal regime} would combine a credibly fixed rate, full convertibility and an independent monetary policy, which the impossible trinity rules out \citep[chap.~9, sec.~4.1, p.~500]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}. \emph{The spectrum} (sec.~4.3, pp.~503--511), from least to most flexible:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{arrangements with no separate legal tender}: dollarisation, or a monetary union, which gives up independent policy;
\item a \emph{currency board}: domestic currency fully backed by reserves of the anchor at a fixed rate, with monetary policy surrendered;
\item a \emph{fixed parity} (conventional peg) within narrow margins, defended by intervention;
\item \emph{target zones} (wider bands) and \emph{crawling pegs} adjusted periodically, often for inflation differentials, and crawling bands;
\item a \emph{managed float}, intervening without a stated target;
\item an \emph{independent float}, the rate set by the market.
\end{itemize}
More fixed regimes import credibility but leave the economy exposed to speculative attack if reserves or commitment are doubted; more flexible regimes absorb shocks but add volatility.

\emph{Effects on trade} (sec.~5, pp.~512--520). In the \emph{elasticities approach} a depreciation improves the trade balance if the generalised Marshall--Lerner condition holds,
\[
\omega_X\varepsilon_X+\omega_M(\varepsilon_M-1)>0,
\]
reducing to $\varepsilon_X+\varepsilon_M>1$ when trade is balanced; demand responds with lags, so the balance may first worsen (the J-curve). The \emph{absorption approach} adds that the balance improves only if national income rises relative to spending, so at full employment a depreciation must be accompanied by lower absorption. \emph{Capital flows}: exchange-rate expectations and interest differentials drive flows, which in floating regimes often dominate short-run moves.

\emph{Capital restrictions} (chap.~8, sec.~3.5, pp.~435--437) aim to reduce volatility from hot money, defend a peg or allow independent monetary policy, keep domestic saving at home, and protect strategic industries from foreign ownership; they may also reduce efficiency and invite evasion.

\qffield{Notation} $\omega_X,\omega_M$ shares of exports and imports in total trade; $\varepsilon_X,\varepsilon_M$ price elasticities of foreign demand for exports and domestic demand for imports.

\qffield{Conditions} EIDM's taxonomy follows the IMF's de jure classification as described in 2013; de facto classifications differ (\hyperref[def:currency-regimes-intervention]{currency regimes}). Marshall--Lerner assumes exports priced in domestic and imports in foreign currency.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced} (Exhibit~9-9, p.~514): a 10 percent euro depreciation raises import spending on EUR 600 million by 3.5 percent ($\varepsilon_M=0.65$), EUR 21 million, and export receipts on EUR 400 million by 7.5 percent ($\varepsilon_X=0.75$), EUR 30 million: the balance improves by EUR 9 million, and $0.4\times0.75+0.6\times(0.65-1)=0.09>0$. The book's printed working reads ``0.65 -- 4'' for $0.65-1$; the result 0.09 is correct.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Expecting a depreciation to improve the balance immediately. Treating a peg as a free option for investors: when it breaks, the move is large. Assuming capital controls work equally against persistent and temporary flows.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:currency-regimes-intervention]{currency regimes and intervention}; \hyperref[def:flows-policy-exchange-rates]{payment flows and policy}; \hyperref[meth:trade-benefits-restrictions-blocs]{trade restrictions}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfresult}[International parity conditions: covered and uncovered interest parity, forward rates as forecasts, absolute, relative and ex ante purchasing power parity, the Fisher effect, real interest rate parity and the international Fisher effect, and how they connect]\label{res:international-parity-conditions}
\qffield{Statement} \citep[chap.~10, sec.~3.1, pp.~549--565]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Covered interest parity}: the forward premium equals the interest differential, enforced by arbitrage (\hyperref[meth:cross-forward-rates]{CIP}).
\item \emph{Uncovered interest parity}: the expected change in the spot rate equals the interest differential, $\%\Delta S^e_{f/d}\approx i_f-i_d$; it requires risk neutrality and has no arbitrage to enforce it.
\item \emph{Forward rate parity}: if both CIP and UIP hold, the forward is an unbiased forecast of the future spot; asking whether it is unbiased ``is the same as asking whether uncovered interest rate parity holds'' (p.~553).
\item \emph{Purchasing power parity}: absolute (one price level across countries, which does not hold); relative (exchange-rate changes offset inflation differentials, $\%\Delta S_{f/d}\approx\pi_f-\pi_d$); ex ante (the same with expected inflation).
\item \emph{Fisher effect} and \emph{real interest rate parity}: nominal rate $\approx$ real rate $+$ expected inflation; if UIP and ex ante PPP hold, real rates converge across countries.
\item \emph{International Fisher effect}: the nominal interest differential equals the expected inflation differential, which follows from real interest rate parity and the Fisher effect (p.~561).
\end{itemize}
If all hold, the expected percentage change in the spot rate equals the forward premium, the nominal yield spread and the expected inflation spread, and the real yield spread is zero (p.~565).

\emph{Evidence}. CIP holds closely (with the post-2008 basis); UIP fails over short and medium horizons, so forwards are poor forecasts and high-yield currencies have not depreciated as predicted (pp.~553--556, 569). Relative PPP fails over one year but holds better over six years and more; ``Estimates place the half-life of PPP deviations at around three to five years'' (p.~560).

\qffield{Notation} $\pi$ inflation; $r$ real rate; f/d convention for the exchange rate.

\qffield{Conditions} The approximate forms drop cross products; exact forms use ratios of $(1+i)$ and $(1+\pi)$. The conditions relate expected changes and forwards to differentials; none pins down the level of the spot rate.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.} With a 1.5 percent real rate everywhere and inflation of 3 percent at home and 1 percent abroad, the Fisher equation gives nominal rates of 4.545 and 2.515 percent; relative PPP and UIP then both predict the foreign currency appreciating 1.980 percent (d/f), equal to the forward premium under CIP.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating UIP as an arbitrage condition. Using PPP to time currencies over months. Confusing the forward discount of a high-yield currency with a forecast of depreciation.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:exchange-rate-forecasting-fair-value]{forecasting and fair value}; \hyperref[meth:carry-trade-uip]{carry trade}; \hyperref[ev:cip-deviations]{CIP deviations}.

\end{qfresult}

\begin{qfmethod}[Forecasting exchange rates and long-run fair value: forward, PPP, UIP and random-walk forecasts, real-interest-differential and risk-premium models, the IMF's equilibrium approaches, and shorter-term tools]\label{meth:exchange-rate-forecasting-fair-value}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Evaluating forecasts} \citep[chap.~10, secs.~3.1--3.3 and~9, pp.~553--569 and~605--610]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{forward rate} and \emph{UIP} forecasts build in the interest differential, but because UIP fails over short horizons they are poor predictors; their errors are large relative to the predicted change;
\item \emph{relative PPP} is a weak guide over one year but an anchor over longer horizons, with deviations decaying slowly (half-life three to five years);
\item the \emph{random walk} (no change) is a hard benchmark to beat at short horizons;
\item fundamentals-based models explain long-run movements better than short-run ones, so shorter-term forecasting relies on technical analysis, order flow, sentiment and positioning (pp.~605--610).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Long-run fair value} (pp.~565--569). PPP is a fair-value framework over long horizons, but large, persistent departures make it a poor real-time gauge. The IMF's Consultative Group on Exchange Rate Issues used three approaches: the \emph{macroeconomic balance} approach (how far the exchange rate must move to close the gap between a country's medium-term current account and its sustainable level), the \emph{external sustainability} approach (the move needed to stabilise net foreign assets or debt at a chosen level), and a reduced-form econometric equilibrium real exchange rate. A simple model links the real rate to its long-run level, the real interest differential and relative risk premiums:
\[
q_{f/d}=\bar q_{f/d}+\big[(r_d-r_f)-(\phi_d-\phi_f)\big]\qquad\text{(eq.~10-12, p.~569)},
\]
so a currency's real value rises with an upward revision to its equilibrium, a higher relative real rate or a lower relative risk premium.

\qffield{Notation} $q$ real exchange rate (f/d), $\bar q$ its long-run equilibrium; $r$ real rates; $\phi$ risk premiums.

\qffield{Conditions} Equation (10-12) is EIDM's simplified form, whose two sides have different units unless the bracket is multiplied by $q$ (footnote~15, p.~569); fair-value estimates depend on the chosen price index, base year and model.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Fair value from the model.} With $\bar q=1.00$, a real-rate gap of 1.5 points and a risk-premium gap of 0.8 points, $q=1.007$: the domestic currency sits 0.7 percent above its long-run value, supported by its real-rate advantage.
\item \emph{PPP fair value.} A base-year rate of 1.10 (d/f) with domestic prices up 10 percent and foreign up 5 percent gives 1.1524; a spot of 1.20 leaves the foreign currency 4.1 percent above PPP, a gap that would halve over three to five years on EIDM's evidence.
\item \emph{Forward against random walk.} On 600 simulated months in which a 3-point interest differential is not offset by depreciation, the random walk forecasts slightly better than the forward (root mean squared error 0.02543 against 0.02560): the forward's bias is small against monthly volatility of 2.5 percent, which is why tests need long samples.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating a fair-value gap as a short-term signal. Choosing the PPP base year that gives the desired answer. Judging forecasting skill on a few observations.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:international-parity-conditions]{parity conditions}; \hyperref[meth:fx-valuation-signals]{FX valuation signals}; \hyperref[meth:fx-volatility-forecasts]{exchange-rate and volatility forecasts}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[The carry trade and uncovered interest parity: the trade's construction and profit, why it profits when UIP fails, crash risk, negative skew and leverage, and safe-haven funding currencies]\label{meth:carry-trade-uip}
\qffield{Statement} Under UIP high-yield currencies should depreciate by the interest differential, so borrowing low-yield and investing high-yield currencies would earn nothing on average. ``Overwhelmingly, the evidence suggests that uncovered interest rate parity does not hold, at least over short- and medium-run time periods'': high-yield currencies have not depreciated, and low-yield currencies have not appreciated, as predicted \citep[chap.~10, sec.~4, pp.~569--572]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}. The \emph{carry trade} is long high-yield and short low-yield (\emph{funding}) currencies. Its reward is ``the gradual accrual of the interest rate differential''; its risk is an adverse exchange-rate move that ``can occur virtually instantaneously'' (p.~570). Returns are more peaked than normal, with fat tails and negative skew: many small gains and occasional large losses. Crashes come because the trade is leveraged and crowded in calm periods, unwinds trigger stop-losses and further liquidation, and in turmoil investors flee to low-yield havens such as the yen and Swiss franc (pp.~570--571). One explanation is that the yield is a risk premium for less stable economies; Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and Schrimpf find that ``high interest rate currencies are negatively related to innovations in global FX volatility and thus deliver low returns in times of unexpected high volatility, when low interest rate currencies provide a hedge'', and that their volatility proxy captures more than 90 percent of the cross-sectional excess returns of five carry portfolios \citep[abstract]{msss-2012}.

\qffield{Notation} Funded P\&L per unit of funding: $\Pi=(S_T/S_0)(1+i_f\tau_f)-(1+i_d\tau_d)$, with $S$ in funding currency per unit of target (\hyperref[mod:fx-carry-trade]{FX carry}).

\qffield{Conditions} The profit is an expected return in normal periods, not an arbitrage; it is earned by bearing crash risk. Transaction costs and the forward points actually traded (including any basis) reduce it.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Borrowing yen at 0.1 percent and investing in Australian dollars at 4.5 percent for a year earns 1.265 percent of the funding if the Australian dollar falls 3 percent: the 4.4-point carry less the depreciation and a 0.135-point cross term. UIP would have predicted a 4.21 percent fall, which would have left nothing.
\item A simulated monthly stream earning 0.6 percent in normal months but losing 8 percent in 2 percent of months has a positive mean (0.45 percent) and a skewness of $-1.9$.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Judging the trade by its Sharpe ratio in calm years. Ignoring that funding currencies rally in crises, when other risky assets fall. Assuming diversification across target currencies removes the crash risk, which is common to them.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:fx-carry-trade]{FX carry model}; \hyperref[res:international-parity-conditions]{UIP}; \hyperref[meth:active-currency-strategies]{active currency strategies}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Payment flows, policy and exchange rates: current and capital accounts, the Mundell--Fleming and monetary models, the policy mix, intervention and capital controls, and the warning signs of currency crises]\label{def:flows-policy-exchange-rates}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Balance of payments} \citep[chap.~10, sec.~5, pp.~573--584]{economics-investment-decision-makers-2013}. The current account equals national saving less investment, private and public: $CA=(S_p-I)+(T-G)$. Persistent deficits tend to weaken a currency over the long run, through the need to attract capital and through the accumulation of external debt, while capital flows, being larger and faster, dominate in the short run; surges of inflows into emerging markets often end in sharp reversals.

\emph{Policy} (sec.~6, pp.~585--596). In the \emph{Mundell--Fleming} model with flexible rates and mobile capital, expansionary monetary policy lowers rates and depreciates the currency; expansionary fiscal policy raises rates and appreciates it if capital is mobile, but depreciates it if capital is immobile and the trade balance worsens. With fixed rates the central bank must offset monetary expansion (which becomes ineffective) and accommodate fiscal expansion. With high mobility, expansionary fiscal plus tight monetary policy is ``extremely bullish'' for a currency and the reverse bearish; with low mobility, policies act through trade flows, so uniformly tight policy is bullish (pp.~586--587, Exhibits~10-7 and~10-8). \emph{Monetary models} relate the rate to money supplies and expected inflation (with overshooting when prices are sticky); the \emph{portfolio balance} view holds that sustained deficits raise the supply of a country's bonds, requiring a weaker currency or higher yields unless policy reverses.

\emph{Intervention and controls} (sec.~7, pp.~597--601). Sterilised intervention leaves the money base unchanged; most studies find its effect in developed markets ``statistically insignificant or quantitatively unimportant'' because reserves are small relative to daily turnover, while emerging-market central banks, whose reserves are large relative to turnover in their currencies, are better placed (pp.~600--601). Capital controls are more effective against temporary than persistent flows and can preserve monetary independence while managing the exchange rate.

\emph{Crises} (sec.~8, pp.~602--605). Interest differentials, consensus forecasts and ratings failed to warn before the ERM, Mexican and Asian crises. An IMF study of ten variables around currency crises in 50 countries over 1975--1997, which EIDM summarises, found that before a crisis the real exchange rate is well above its tranquil-period mean, reserves fall sharply, the terms of trade deteriorate somewhat, inflation is higher, the ratio of M2 to reserves and broad money growth rise (money growth peaking about 18 months before), private credit grows quickly and equity prices boom and bust; the trade balance and real activity show no distinct pattern beforehand (pp.~603--604). One school holds that crises follow deteriorating fundamentals; another that sentiment shifts or contagion can strike sound economies, which limits any early-warning system (p.~603).

\qffield{Notation} $S_p$ private saving, $I$ investment, $T-G$ government balance, as shares of GDP.

\qffield{Conditions} The policy results assume sticky prices and given expectations; the crisis list is a set of stylised facts from studies EIDM summarises, and early-warning systems issue false signals.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Private saving of 18 percent of GDP, investment of 22 percent and a budget deficit of 3 percent imply a current-account deficit of 7 percent, which must be financed by capital inflows of the same size.
\item A currency whose reserves fall 30 percent in five months while its real exchange rate rises 23 percent shows two of EIDM's warning signs; EIDM cautions that markets rarely price such crises in advance.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Reading a current-account deficit as a short-term sell signal. Expecting the same policy to move the currency the same way under high and low capital mobility. Relying on spreads or ratings for crisis warnings.
