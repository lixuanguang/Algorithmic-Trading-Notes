\section{Currencies in global macro}

\begin{qfdefinition}[The dollar in global macro: FX as shock absorber, transmission channel and expression; the dollar's share of turnover and reserves; the dollar index and its weights; and the SDR]\label{def:us-dollar-dxy-sdr}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner: ``The U.S.\ dollar accounts for 85 percent of all foreign exchange (FX) transactions'', the shares summing to 200 percent because each trade involves two currencies (Figure~7.1, BIS Triennial Survey, April 2013), and more than 60 percent of central banks' reserves, declining gradually \citep[chap.~7, sec.\ ``The Role of the U.S.\ Dollar'', PDF pp.~180--181]{s7-gliner}. The U.S.\ Dollar Index is the dollar against a basket changed once, in 1999, with weights EUR 57.6, JPY 13.6, GBP 11.9, CAD 9.1, SEK 4.2 and CHF 3.6 percent, traded as the DX future and the UUP ETF (p.~181). The SDR was created by the IMF in 1969, initially defined as 0.888671 grams of fine gold, later as a basket of currencies; it ``is neither a currency, nor a claim on the IMF'' and serves as the IMF's unit of account (sec.\ ``Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)'', pp.~187--188). The IMF's current factsheet gives the basket as the U.S.\ dollar, euro, Chinese renminbi, Japanese yen and pound sterling, with the valuation reviewed every five years and the current weights effective 1 August 2022 \citep[factsheet dated 7 October 2023]{imf-sdr-factsheet}.

\qffield{Notation} DXY the ICE U.S.\ Dollar Index, a geometric average of six exchange rates; SDR Special Drawing Right.

\qffield{Conditions} Gliner's 85 percent means the dollar is on one side of 85 percent of trades; he sources it to the BIS 2013 survey (Figure~7.1). In April 2025 the dollar ``was on one side of 89.2\% of all trades'', up from 88.4 percent in 2022, with the euro at 28.9, the yen at 16.8, sterling at 10.2 and the renminbi at 8.5 percent, out of a total of 200 \citep[preliminary results, sec.~2]{bis-triennial-2025}. \emph{Index methodology} (slice-37 follow-up). ICE describes the index as ``a geometrically-averaged calculation of six currencies'', computed as $\text{USDX}=50.14348112\times\text{EURUSD}^{-0.576}\times\text{USDJPY}^{0.136}\times\text{GBPUSD}^{-0.119}\times\text{USDCAD}^{0.091}\times\text{USDSEK}^{0.042}\times\text{USDCHF}^{0.036}$, the exponent negative where the dollar is the quote currency; it ``originally was developed by the U.S.\ Federal Reserve in 1973'' as a bilateral trade-weighted average, futures have traded since 1985, and the weights match Gliner's \citep[FAQ, June 2015, p.~2]{ice-dxy-faq}. The FAQ is dated 2015; later changes, if any, were not checked. The weights are fixed, changed only once, in 1999 (Gliner); the index is therefore not a trade-weighted index against today's partners: it gives the euro over half the weight and omits the renminbi and the Mexican peso (legacy, consistent with the weights).

\qffield{Practice} The legacy's three roles of FX in macro (shock absorber, transmission channel, expression instrument) and its claims that global macro is ``de facto USD-centric'', that many local trades are ``USD liquidity trades in disguise'', and that stress appears first in the dollar index, the cross-currency basis and EM currencies are the legacy author's framing; dollar funding and the basis are the subject of \texttt{fx-cip-dollar-funding}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The SDR is not a reserve currency anyone trades; ``insight into official reserve diversification trends'' (legacy) comes from the IMF's COFER data on reserve composition (Gliner's Figure~7.2), not from the SDR basket. Weights in percent (57.6 for the euro) are exponents of a geometric average, not shares of a basket of currency amounts; a 1 percent rise in EUR/USD lowers the index by about 0.576 percent.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:fx-market-structure]{spot FX market structure}; \hyperref[def:currency-regimes-intervention]{currency regimes}; \hyperref[mod:fx-carry-trade]{FX carry}; \hyperref[meth:fx-valuation-signals]{FX valuation signals}.

\qffield{Further reading} The dollar's role and safe-asset demand \citep{wide-jkl-safe-asset}.

\qffield{Index} U.S.\ dollar; reserve currency; FX turnover by currency; BIS Triennial Survey; U.S.\ Dollar Index; DXY; dollar index weights; Special Drawing Right; SDR basket; COFER; roles of FX in macro

\begin{qfdefinition}[Currency regimes and intervention: fixed, managed and floating regimes in Gliner's grouping, sterilised and unsterilised intervention, the impossible trinity, and reserve depletion]\label{def:currency-regimes-intervention}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner groups currencies by the central bank's intervention: \emph{fixed} (HKD, DKK, LVL, AED, TRL in his 2013 table), \emph{floating with frequent intervention} (JPY, BRL, CNY, CHF with the EUR/CHF floor) and \emph{floating with no intervention} (USD, EUR, GBP, AUD, NZD, MXN, CAD, SEK, NOK, INR), stressing that ``no model will be of help if, for example, a currency is pegged'' \citep[chap.~7, sec.\ ``Currency Regimes'', PDF pp.~185--194]{s7-gliner}. His examples include the Hong Kong dollar's peg at 7.8 (p.~190) and Brazil's repeated interventions (Table~7.2, pp.~191--192). Intervention is \emph{sterilised} when the central bank offsets its effect on the monetary base and \emph{unsterilised} when it lets the base change (chap.~11, pp.~360--361). The \emph{impossible trinity}, from Mundell's work, says a country can have only two of a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and an independent monetary policy (chap.~11, sec.\ ``The Impossible Trinity'', p.~363).

\qffield{Notation} Peg, crawling peg, band and currency board are forms of a fixed regime; a floor (EUR/CHF 1.20 from 6 September 2011) limits movement in one direction.

\qffield{Conditions} Gliner's grouping is de jure and as of 2013; de facto classifications, which the pathway uses, can differ, and several of his currencies have since changed regime (the legacy dates the end of the EUR/CHF floor to 2015). \emph{A de facto check} (second slice-37 follow-up), on the Ilzetzki--Reinhart--Rogoff monthly fine classification (codes 1 no separate legal tender, 2 pre-announced peg or currency board, 3 pre-announced horizontal band within $\pm2\%$, 11 moving band within $\pm2\%$, 12 de facto moving band within $\pm5\%$ or managed floating, 13 freely floating) for December 2013 and December 2025 \citep[sheets ``Classification'' and ``Fine'', last update 5 October 2026]{irr-era-2025}: HKD, DKK and AED are 2 in both months, and Latvia moves from 2 to 1 in January 2014, when it adopted the euro; but Gliner's ``fixed'' TRL is 12 in both, and his intervening floats differ: CNY is 3 (a band) and CHF 2 in 2013 (the floor), 11 in 2025; JPY moves from 13 to 12 in August 2024. Of his ``no intervention'' floats only USD and AUD are 13 in both months; GBP, CAD and NZD are 11 in 2025 (since January 2020), SEK and INR 11 in both, MXN and NOK reach 13 only in 2016 and 2023. So de jure ``free float'' and de facto ``freely floating'' are different lists (extract in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/irr-fine-codes-gliner-currencies.csv}). The IMF's own de facto table (AREAER) was not read: its 2023 overview refused access (HTTP 403) and the database requires a login.

\qffield{Practice} The legacy's points (in a free float, price discovery happens in FX; in a managed regime, pressure appears in rates, reserves or capital controls; a hard peg looks stable until it breaks; ``cheap'' can stay cheap if the regime prevents adjustment) and its list of intervention motives and conditions for success (reserve adequacy, credibility, consistency with monetary policy, liquidity; intervention against fundamentals ``typically fails''; reserve depletion as a leading indicator) are standard arguments, not shown in Gliner's chapter; testing them is the pathway's outcome. The trinity explains the ERM crisis of 1992 and the Asian crisis of 1997 in the legacy's telling; Gliner gives no such cases in that section.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The trinity is about full capital mobility and a fixed rate; intermediate regimes trade off partial independence against partial stability, so ``choose two of three'' is a corner-solution statement.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:central-bank-policy-framework]{central-bank tools}; \hyperref[rule:major-central-banks-current]{major central banks}; \hyperref[def:us-dollar-dxy-sdr]{the dollar and the SDR}.

\qffield{Further reading} On whether intervention works, \citep{cur-fratzscher-et-al-2019}; for a survey, \citep{cur-menkhoff-2013}.

\qffield{Index} currency regime; fixed exchange rate; peg; currency board; managed float; free float; FX intervention; sterilised intervention; unsterilised intervention; impossible trinity; trilemma; Mundell; Hong Kong dollar peg; EUR/CHF floor; reserve depletion

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Gliner's FX valuation signals: equity performance, sovereign credit, risk reversals, trade and the trade-weighted index, export-partner growth, debt and the current account, reserves, PPP and GDP per capita, and policy-rate and LIBOR differentials]\label{meth:fx-valuation-signals}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner lists factors for a currency model \citep[chap.~7, sec.\ ``Valuation Techniques for Foreign Exchange'', PDF pp.~195--209]{s7-gliner}: relative \emph{equity index performance}, through flows (pp.~195--196); \emph{credit}, five-year sovereign CDS (Australia against Mexico, p.~197); \emph{sentiment}, ``call-put skew on three-month risk reversals'', to be ``back-tested on a currency-by-currency basis'' and correlating better in distress than in calm periods (p.~197); the \emph{trade} balance and current account (pp.~198--199); the \emph{trade-weighted index} (pp.~199--200); \emph{economic activity} (p.~200); \emph{export-partner growth} weighted by export shares, with Australia's exports to China, Japan, Korea, India and Taiwan (pp.~200--201); \emph{debt to GDP} (p.~202); \emph{absolute and relative PPP}, with the Big Mac index, and Meese and Rogoff's finding that it does not forecast in the short run (pp.~203--205); \emph{GDP per capita} (p.~205); and \emph{carry} indicators, the policy-rate (OIS) differential and the LIBOR differential, decomposed as LIBOR $=$ fed funds $+$ (LIBOR $-$ fed funds) (pp.~206--208), with risk-adjusted carry (\hyperref[mod:fx-carry-trade]{FX carry}).

\qffield{Notation} Risk reversal: implied volatility of an out-of-the-money call minus that of an equally out-of-the-money put (usually 25-delta); TWI: a trade-weighted index of the currency against its partners.

\qffield{Conditions} The list is a set of candidate factors with charts, not a tested model: Gliner gives no out-of-sample results, and the pathway's question is precisely whether such factors beat a random walk. Several cut both ways, as the legacy notes: strong activity can attract inflows and also worsen the trade balance.

\qffield{Practice} Build each factor from data available at the time and test against a random walk with economic as well as statistical criteria. The legacy grouped the factors into risk-appetite, trade, sustainability, long-term anchors and carry, and added FX reserve adequacy (months of import cover, short-term debt coverage), which Gliner treats under reserves elsewhere.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Gliner's account of the \emph{Balassa--Samuelson effect}, that as a country becomes richer ``foreign investment flows into the country'' and strengthens the currency (p.~205), is not the effect: Balassa--Samuelson says faster productivity growth in traded goods raises the relative price of non-traded goods and so the real exchange rate. GDP per capita adjusted for PPP corrects for price levels, not for inflation as he puts it. Meese and Rogoff's 1983 result concerned structural exchange-rate models against a random walk at short horizons, a wider statement than ``PPP is not an accurate forecasting tool''. LIBOR differentials are historical (\hyperref[def:money-market-reference-rates]{reference rates}). \emph{Own out-of-sample test of one factor, relative PPP} (batch of 2026-10-17, corrected rerun v2 the same day). The hypothesis and pass rules were written in the script before its first run, by the batch record's account, and committed together with the results, so this is not a preregistration.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Design.} Nine currencies against the dollar (JPY, GBP, CHF, CAD, AUD, NZD, SEK, NOK, DKK), on FRED's monthly H.10 \emph{averages}. The signal $z_t$ is the deviation of the log real rate from its expanding mean.
\item \emph{Timing.} A month is eligible as a predictor only if its price levels were known at $t$: monthly CPI exactly two months old, or quarterly CPI four to six months past its quarter end, never older. Outcomes need only spot rates, not future CPI.
\item \emph{Forecasts.} Expanding regressions of the next 1- and 12-month spot change on $z_t$, using only outcomes already observed, were compared with a random walk from January 1985. The effective sample ends are August 2021 (JPY), May--June 2025 (the monthly-CPI currencies) and September 2025 (AUD, NZD).
\item \emph{Results.} Full-sample slopes are negative for all nine at 12 months, as PPP predicts. The forecasts lose to the random walk out of sample for every currency: pooled $R^2=-2.0$ percent at 1 month and $-14.9$ percent at 12 (Clark--West $t=-2.1$ and $-0.3$).
\item \emph{Portfolio.} A monthly portfolio long the three cheapest and short the three dearest currencies returned $+0.09$ percent a year in spot terms, 1985 to May 2025 (Sharpe 0.01). Adding three-month interbank differentials gives $-0.17$ percent a year (2002--2025), before an assumed 5~bp per unit of turnover. Because it is computed from monthly averages, this is a statistical portfolio, not an executable trading result, and interbank rates are not the trader's funding or forward points.
\end{itemize}
Neither pass rule is met. The superseded v1 run (kept in \nolinkurl{v1-2026-10-17/}) required future CPI to observe an outcome and carried 2025Q1 CPI for AUD and NZD to September 2026. It reported $-15.3$ percent at 12 months ($t=-0.4$); the changes move no conclusion. Script, protocol and outputs: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/fx-determination-forecasting/fx_ppp_oos_test.py}, \nolinkurl{fx_ppp_results.txt} and the derived panel \nolinkurl{fx_ppp_monthly.csv}, which records each month's eligibility and CPI age. \emph{A second factor: carry} (third batch of 2026-10-17). Gliner's carry indicator is the short-rate differential (pp.~206--208). It was tested on the same FRED files with a protocol written before any carry result was computed (\nolinkurl{batch60-fx-carry-protocol.md}). The protocol is not blind to the data, which the PPP test had used, and it was committed with the results. The predictor is the previous month's three-month interbank differential against the dollar. The model is a pooled expanding regression of 1- and 12-month dollar exchange-rate changes on it, forecasting from January 1995.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Forecasts: not supported.} The pooled out-of-sample $R^2$ against a random walk is $-0.4$ percent at 1 month (Clark--West $t=-0.85$) and $-6.7$ percent at 12 (Clark--West $t=-4.4$), negative for every currency at 12 months. The full-sample pooled slope is $+0.0017$ per percentage point at 12 months, against $-0.01$ under uncovered interest parity: high-rate currencies did not depreciate by the differential, on average. That is the forward-premium pattern, though it does not help forecast spot rates.
\item \emph{Portfolio: supported, narrowly.} Long the currencies above the cross-sectional median differential and short those below, earning spot change plus the interest accrual, net of 5~bp per unit of turnover. January 1985 to August 2026, the portfolio returned $+2.07$ percent a year (volatility 5.6 percent, Sharpe 0.37, Newey--West $t=2.04$, against the threshold of~2). Its three worst months were March 1985, September 2008 and February 2020.
\end{itemize}
So carry pays through the accrued differential, not through forecastable exchange-rate moves. The portfolio is a statistical return on monthly-average prices and interbank rates, not an executable trade; its $t$ barely clears the rule. Script and outputs: \nolinkurl{batch60_fx_carry_test.py}, \nolinkurl{batch60_fx_carry_results.txt} and \nolinkurl{batch60_fx_carry_monthly.csv}. Limits: monthly averages rather than month-end rates; revised CPI, not vintages; dollar bases only; interbank rates in place of policy-rate or OIS differentials; two of Gliner's twelve factors, since the others need data (CDS, risk reversals, export shares) not used here.

\qffield{Further reading} Order flow and fundamentals in exchange-rate determination, and currency value \citep{wide-menkhoff-value}.

\begin{qfmodel}[The FX carry trade: carry as the rate differential at a constant exchange rate, funding and target currencies, crowding and crash risk, and carry-to-risk ratios]\label{mod:fx-carry-trade}
\qffield{Statement} ``In currencies, carry is the return one makes on holding a currency, assuming that the exchange rate is held constant''; carry trades borrow in low-yielding currencies (yen, dollar, sterling in Gliner's time) and buy high-yielding ones (Australian and New Zealand dollars, Brazilian real, Turkish lira) \citep[chap.~7, sec.\ ``Carry Indicators for Currency Valuation'', PDF pp.~206--207]{s7-gliner}. ``The carry trade is a very crowded trade, which means that in large risk off markets such as 2008, the strategy suffers big losses'' (p.~207). \emph{Risk-adjusted carry} divides three-month carry by 30-day volatility; Gliner's Table~7.6 (September 2013) ranks pairs within groups, NZD/EUR at 0.26 being the most attractive New Zealand cross, and he says risk-adjusted carry ``tends to outperform standard carry trades'' and needs frequent adjustment (pp.~208--209).

\qffield{Notation} \emph{Conventions} (slice-37 follow-ups; the second replaces the first's single accrual fraction). $S_t$ is the spot rate quoted as domestic currency per unit of foreign currency; $i_d$ and $i_f$ are simple money-market rates, each quoted on its own day-count basis, and $\tau_d$ and $\tau_f$ are the accrual fractions for the same calendar period under those bases (for 90 days, $90/360$ for an ACT/360 rate and $90/365$ for an ACT/365 rate); $F$ is the forward for the period. \emph{Exact relations}: covered interest parity,
\[ \frac{F}{S_0}=\frac{1+i_d\tau_d}{1+i_f\tau_f}; \]
and the funded long-foreign position (borrow one unit of domestic currency at $i_d$, buy $1/S_0$ of foreign currency, deposit it at $i_f$, convert back at $S_T$) earns, per initial domestic unit, the arithmetic P\&L
\[ \Pi=\frac{S_T}{S_0}(1+i_f\tau_f)-(1+i_d\tau_d). \]
Carry is $\Pi$ at an unchanged rate, $S_T=S_0$: exactly $i_f\tau_f-i_d\tau_d$, which equals $(i_f-i_d)\tau$ only when both rates share one basis. Hedging at $F$ gives $\Pi=(F/S_0)(1+i_f\tau_f)-(1+i_d\tau_d)=0$ exactly, on either basis. \emph{First-order forms}: with $s=S_T/S_0-1$, $\Pi=(i_f\tau_f-i_d\tau_d)+s+s\,i_f\tau_f$, so $\Pi\approx i_f\tau_f-i_d\tau_d+s$ after dropping the cross term; the forward premium $(F-S_0)/S_0=(i_d\tau_d-i_f\tau_f)/(1+i_f\tau_f)\approx i_d\tau_d-i_f\tau_f$. \emph{A different quantity}: the continuously compounded log excess return $x=\ln(S_T/S_0)+\ln(1+i_f\tau_f)-\ln(1+i_d\tau_d)$, equivalently $\ln(S_T/S_0)+(r_f-r_d)\tau$ with continuously compounded rates on a common $\tau$; it satisfies $e^x-1=\Pi/(1+i_d\tau_d)$, a return per unit of domestic funding repaid, not the arithmetic P\&L $\Pi$. \emph{Example} (90 days, $i_d=5\%$ ACT/360, $i_f=2\%$ ACT/365): $\tau_d=0.25$, $\tau_f=0.246575$, $F/S_0=1.0075314$; carry $-0.75685\%$, not the $-0.75\%$ a common basis would give; a 1 percent rise in $S$ gives $\Pi=+0.24808\%$ against $+0.24315\%$ to first order, and $x=0.24472\%$ (second follow-up check script, section~1). Carry-to-risk $=$ annualised carry $/$ annualised volatility.

\qffield{Conditions} The realised arithmetic P\&L is carry plus the exchange-rate change, to first order (above); uncovered interest parity predicts that the change offsets carry on average, and carry's profitability is the forward-premium puzzle. Carry-to-risk uses volatility as the risk measure and so ignores skewness and crash risk; the ranking by ratio can differ from the ranking by carry (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/slice37-checks.py}, section~17).

\qffield{Practice} The legacy's risk list (crowding, weekend gaps in EM currencies, regime shifts in policy, correlated sell-offs of carry currencies against funders, illiquidity when exits are needed) is consistent with Gliner's 2008 remark but goes beyond it; its statements that exchange-rate moves ``often dominate carry returns'' and that carry has ``positive returns on average but with significant negative skewness'' are claims from the literature the pathway reads. The abstract of Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen's working paper states that ``exchange rate movements between high-interest-rate and low-interest-rate currencies are negatively skewed'', attributes the skewness to ``sudden unwinding of carry trades'' when risk appetite and funding liquidity fall, and reports that carry losses reduce future crash risk but raise its price \citep[abstract, NBER Working Paper 14473, November 2008, revised December 2013]{catalogue-0241}; \emph{The paper's body} (second slice-37 follow-up, the November 2008 working-paper PDF): 3-month interbank rates and exchange rates against the dollar for eight developed currencies (AUD, CAD, JPY, NZD, NOK, CHF, GBP, EUR) from Datastream, 1986--2006 (PDF p.~8); equal-weighted portfolios long the $k$ highest-rate and short the $k$ lowest-rate currencies, rebalanced weekly (1992--2006) or quarterly (1986--2006), have skewness of $-0.717$ and $-0.700$ for $k=1$, $-0.537$ and $-0.748$ for $k=2$ and $-0.695$ and $-0.977$ for $k=3$ (weekly and quarterly), with annualised Sharpe ratios between 0.59 and 0.78 and average quarterly returns of 1.6 to 2.2 percent (Table~2, PDF p.~12); the CRSP value-weighted U.S.\ stock index has skewness of $-0.41$ weekly and $-0.88$ quarterly over the same samples (p.~13), so carry skewness is ``roughly comparable'' to the stock market's, and it is not diversified away by adding currencies \citep[working paper of November 2008, PDF pp.~8 and 12--13]{catalogue-0241}. These are developed-market G10 samples to 2006, before the 2008 unwind. \emph{A broader sample through the crisis} (read 2026-10-16 in the authors' accepted version, February 2011). Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and Schrimpf sort up to 48 currencies against the dollar (and a subsample of 15 developed ones, ten after the euro) into five portfolios by one-month forward discount each month, November 1983 to August 2009, net of bid--ask costs charged on entry and exit with about 30 percent monthly turnover \citep[sec.~II, pp.~8--9]{msss-2012}. The high-minus-low carry portfolio earned 7.23 percent a year net with a standard deviation of 9.81 percent, skewness $-1.03$, kurtosis 4.79 and a Sharpe ratio of 0.74 for all countries, and 5.72 percent, 10.24, $-0.92$ and 0.56 for developed countries; skewness falls from portfolio~1 to~5 for all countries but not monotonically for developed ones (Table~I, p.~45). Their pricing result is about volatility, not skewness: carry returns load negatively on innovations in global FX volatility, which price the five portfolios, while they ``do not find that the skewness of a portfolio is robustly related to average excess returns'' and aggregate-skewness betas are insignificant with low cross-sectional fit (sec.~V.C, pp.~28--29). So negative skewness of carry is confirmed in this sample, including 2008, but it is not what explains the cross-section there. The Sharpe ratios are consistent with the means and standard deviations (7.23/9.81 = 0.74, 5.72/10.24 = 0.56). ``Measuring the standard error of the sample and finding the most attractive pairs'' is Gliner's (p.~209), without method.

\qffield{Pitfalls} ``Outperforms standard carry trades'' is asserted without evidence in the passage. Gliner's Table~7.6 labels the carry ``3M Carry (LIBOR)'' and the volatility ``30D Volatility''; the legacy's ``30D Realized Volatility'' adds a word the table does not have.
