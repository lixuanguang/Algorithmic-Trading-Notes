\section{Crypto venues, perpetuals and trading risk}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Crypto venue and instrument structure: fragmented executable prices, reference prices and best execution, and spot, dated futures, perpetuals (linear, inverse, quanto), options and pools]\label{def:crypto-venue-instrument-structure}
\qffield{Statement} Crypto assets trade on many independently owned venues. Makarov and Schoar, using tick data from 34 exchanges in 19 countries, find that individual exchanges mostly run centralised order books like equity markets, but that, unlike regulated equity markets, the crypto market ``lacks any provisions to ensure that investors receive the best price'', contrasting the U.S. NBBO requirement \citep[discussion-paper version of December 2018, pp.~1--2 and footnote~1]{s6-makarov}. Kept apart here:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{executable prices}: the bids and offers on each venue, in its own quote currency, custody and fee terms;
\item \emph{reference prices}: indices a venue or benchmark administrator computes from several venues, against which derivatives are marked or settled (Deribit marks positions with a Mark Price and computes funding against a Deribit Index, \hyperref[mech:perpetual-funding]{perpetual funding});
\item \emph{fair-value models}: a trader's own estimate of value, which need not equal either;
\item \emph{legal best-execution obligations}: duties under a jurisdiction's rules (\hyperref[def:best-execution]{best execution}), which Makarov and Schoar found absent across crypto venues as a market-wide price-protection rule (p.~1).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Instruments.} \emph{Spot} trades exchange one asset for another; on Deribit spot trades are brokerage services settled and delivered immediately \citep[Rulebook v2.0, Rules 1.2(a) and 11.3(a)]{gapclose-deribit-rules}. \emph{Dated futures} expire, and convergence to spot at expiry limits the basis \citep[arXiv v2, p.~2]{roadmap-perpetual-pricing}. \emph{Perpetual futures} have no expiry and are anchored by periodic funding payments (\hyperref[mech:perpetual-funding]{perpetual funding}). Ackerer, Hugonnier and Jermann distinguish three contract types (pp.~2--3):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{linear}: contract size in units of the base asset (1 BTC), margined and settled in the quote currency (USD, or in practice a stablecoin such as USDT, p.~7);
\item \emph{inverse}: the base asset itself (BTC) is the margin and settlement asset, and the contract size is in the quote currency (for example 10{,}000 USD);
\item \emph{quanto}: a third currency (for example ETH) is used for margining and settlement.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Options}: Deribit lists European-style cash-settled options and linear options; its European options are priced in BTC or ETH and cannot be exercised before expiry (Rulebook, Appendix~A, 2(d)). \emph{Listing snapshot, 17 October 2026} (Deribit public API, one venue on one day; open interest as the API reports it, in units of the underlying, the contract size being one coin for both BTC listings; units not confirmed from the API documentation). BTC and ETH options are listed as inverse (``reversed'', premium and settlement in the coin): 938 BTC instruments over 12 expiries, a median of 42 strikes per expiry. USDC-settled linear options are listed on BTC, ETH, SOL, XRP, AVAX, HYPE and TRX. Inverse BTC options carry 390{,}301 of open interest against 2{,}781 for USDC-settled BTC options (0.7\%).

Open interest is concentrated: the top 10\% of inverse BTC instruments hold 67\% of it, 202 of 938 hold none, and two expiries (30~October and 25~December 2026) hold 72\% \citep[\nolinkurl{get_instruments} and \nolinkurl{get_book_summary_by_currency}, 17 October 2026]{deribit-api-2026}. \emph{On-chain pools} (automated market makers) are outside this entry's scope.

\qffield{Conditions} The fragmentation evidence is historical. Over December 2017 to February 2018 the daily average price ratio between the United States and Korea exceeded 15 percent and reached 40 percent on several days; Japan--U.S. differences were about 10 percent and U.S.--Europe about 3 percent, while differences within a country typically did not exceed one percent on average (pp.~1--2). The authors estimate at least \$2 billion of potential arbitrage profit over that period from executed trades (p.~2, footnote~2). Spreads co-moved across countries and opened during rapid appreciation; between cryptocurrencies on the same exchanges spreads were an order of magnitude smaller, and pairwise capital-control tightness was positively related to the correlation of spreads, which the authors read as evidence that capital controls contribute (pp.~2--3). These are 2017--2018 findings; current spreads were not measured.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: a crypto price is a price on a venue, in a quote currency, with that venue's custody, collateral and fees. Quote-currency differences (USD, USDT, USDC, KRW) and settlement currency (linear against inverse) change the units of P\&L and margin. Cross-references: fragmentation without a consolidated tape in spot FX is in \hyperref[def:fx-market-structure]{spot FX market structure}, routing across venues in \hyperref[def:smart-order-routing]{smart order routing}, limits to arbitrage in \hyperref[res:arbitrage-risks]{arbitrage risks}.

\emph{A second venue on the same day}. On OKX's public API at about 11:52 UTC, OKX lists 1{,}466 BTC options: 786 inverse (settled in BTC) over 14 expiries and 680 linear USD-settled (``\_UM'') over 10, each with a 0.01~BTC multiplier. Of \$2.55 billion of open interest, 99.2 percent is in the inverse contracts; the three largest expiries hold 64.7 percent (25~December and 30~October 2026 and 26~March 2027) and the ten largest instruments 21.1 percent \citep[\nolinkurl{public/instruments} and \nolinkurl{public/open-interest}, BTC-USD options, 17 October 2026]{okx-api-2026}. So the Deribit pattern, inverse contracts dominant and open interest concentrated in the quarterly and year-end expiries, is repeated at a second venue on the same day; two venues on one day are not a time series.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Crypto has no universal consolidated tape, but that does not make fair value venue-dependent: venue prices differ, fair value does not. The sources support the absence of a market-wide best-price rule in 2018 (Makarov and Schoar) and say nothing on consolidated tapes. Funding keeps perpetual futures near spot only loosely (\hyperref[mech:perpetual-funding]{perpetual funding}). Option exposure is concentrated in a few strikes and expiries on the one venue sampled above; concentration across venues is not shown. Deribit's terms are one venue's.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mech:perpetual-funding]{perpetual funding}; \hyperref[def:crypto-trading-risk-layers]{crypto trading risk layers}; \hyperref[def:best-execution]{best execution}; \hyperref[def:fx-market-structure]{spot FX market structure}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmechanism}[Perpetual funding: premium and interest terms, sign convention, accrual and settlement, damper and caps (Deribit as one venue), and why convergence is not guaranteed]\label{mech:perpetual-funding}
\qffield{Statement} A perpetual future has no expiry, so it cannot rely on convergence at maturity; instead, longs and shorts exchange periodic \emph{funding} payments. Ackerer, Hugonnier and Jermann describe the payment as generally the sum of a \emph{premium} part $\kappa_t(f_t-x_t)$, paid by longs when the perpetual price $f_t$ is above spot $x_t$, and an \emph{interest} part $\iota_t x_t$ set by the exchange to reflect the interest-rate differential between the two currencies; for a linear contract the long's cash flow over one period is
\[ (f_{t+1}-f_t)-\kappa_t(f_t-x_t)-\iota_t x_t \]
in the quote currency \citep[arXiv v2, sec.~3, pp.~7--8, eqs.~(4)--(6)]{roadmap-perpetual-pricing}. Funding is commonly computed every 8 hours (p.~2). In their frictionless no-arbitrage model the exchange can choose the interest factor so that the perpetual price equals spot for any sufficiently large premium rate (p.~4; Corollary~1, p.~12). That is a model result. He, Manela, Ross and von Wachter state that perpetuals ``are not guaranteed to converge to the spot price'', derive bounds with trading costs, and find empirical deviations larger than in traditional currency markets, co-moving across currencies and diminishing over time \citep[arXiv v7 of 17 September 2026, abstract]{roadmap-perpetual-fundamentals}.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{One venue's rule: Deribit, Funding Specifications page edited 27 July 2026} \citep[Funding Specifications]{gapclose-deribit-rules}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{sign}: when the funding rate is positive, longs pay shorts; when negative, shorts pay longs; Deribit charges no fee on funding, so payments net to zero between holders;
\item \emph{rate}: premium rate $P=(\text{Mark Price}-\text{Deribit Index})/\text{Deribit Index}$; a damper sets funding to zero for $|P|\le0.025\%$ and otherwise moves $P$ towards zero by $0.025\%$; a cap then bounds the rate, $\pm0.5\%$ for BTC, $\pm1.0\%$ for ETH and $\pm5.0\%$ for the USDC- and USDT-margined perpetuals:
\[ \text{rate}=\min\bigl(c,\ \max\bigl(-c,\ \max(0.025\%,P)+\min(-0.025\%,P)\bigr)\bigr); \]
\item \emph{units and cadence}: the rate is expressed per 8 hours but accrues continuously; the payment is rate $\times$ position size $\times$ (period / 8 hours), in the settlement currency of the contract: BTC in Deribit's BTC example, where a USD 100{,}000 position is 1 BTC, an inverse contract in Ackerer, Hugonnier and Jermann's sense (Interpretation), and USDC for a USDC-margined linear one;
\item \emph{settlement}: accrued funding shows at once in account equity (realised session P\&L) and can be used for trading, but moves to the cash balance only at the daily settlement at 08:00 UTC, after which it can be withdrawn (also Rulebook v2.0, Rule 11.4).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Practice} Worked example (Deribit's): BTC perpetual mark USD 100{,}075 against index USD 100{,}000 gives $P=0.075\%$ and a rate of $0.05\%$ per 8 hours; a long of USD 100{,}000 (1 BTC) pays $0.05\%\times1\text{ BTC}\times1/480=0.000001041667$ BTC per minute and $0.0005$ BTC over 8 hours. A USDC-margined SOL perpetual at mark 100.10 against index 100 gives $0.075\%$, so a USDC 100{,}000 long pays 0.15625 USDC per minute and 75 USDC over 8 hours. The printed max/min formula agrees with the prose rule on a grid of premia and in the other examples (damper, caps, offsetting minutes). Interpretation: funding is a carry cost or income of holding the perpetual against spot, in the contract's settlement currency.

\emph{What the funding gap measures}. He, Manela, Ross and von Wachter derive no-arbitrage perpetual prices in frictionless markets and bounds with trading costs; empirically ``The mean absolute deviation is about 52\% to 90\% per year across different cryptocurrencies'', larger than covered-interest-parity deviations, comoving across coins and shrinking by about 22 percentage points a year; trading the deviation earns a Bitcoin Sharpe ratio of 3.35 under retail-level costs (3.27 after effective spreads) (sec.~1, printed pp.~3--4). They explain the gap by end users who ``demand perpetual futures for leveraged exposure'' against arbitrageurs with limited balance-sheet capacity, and find deviations compressed after Binance introduced portfolio margining in April 2022 (printed pp.~4--5) \citep[arXiv v7, sec.~1, printed pp.~3--5]{roadmap-perpetual-fundamentals}. On that account the funding gap measures leverage demand meeting scarce arbitrage capital; the paper tests capacity, not positioning concentration.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Funding is the periodic payment that ties perpetual futures to spot, with longs paying when it is positive; the sign convention matches Deribit's. The tie to spot holds as a design intent and a model result, not a guarantee. Funding is a carry term; that it is also a crowding signal is not supported. The dated-futures evidence in \hyperref[ev:crypto-dated-futures-carry]{crypto carry} ties the basis to small traders' demand for leverage and to limited arbitrage capital, which supports a leverage-demand reading.

Venue rules differ: other venues may set cadence, interest term, clamps and index construction differently, and Deribit's own page contains slips in its ETH examples (an index ``at USD 10,000'' and ``a 1 BTC long position'' in the ETH column of Example~3; 7.5\% inside the ETH cap formula of Example~5), whose stated results are nevertheless correct.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:crypto-venue-instrument-structure]{crypto venue and instrument structure}; \hyperref[def:crypto-trading-risk-layers]{crypto trading risk layers}; \hyperref[mech:futures-margin-settlement]{futures margin and settlement}; \hyperref[def:securities-lending-fee-rebate]{lending fees and rebates}.

\end{qfmechanism}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Crypto trading risk layers: liquidation, insurance fund, socialised loss and auto-deleveraging at one venue, kept apart from custody, stablecoin and network-settlement risk]\label{def:crypto-trading-risk-layers}
\qffield{Statement} The losses a crypto trader can bear beyond market risk come in layers, governed by different documents and failing differently:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{liquidation}: forced closing of positions when margin falls short. Under Deribit's rulebook, if a member fails to meet maintenance margin, Deribit may close positions, cancel orders, delta-hedge with futures and perpetuals, apply auto-deleveraging where applicable, or close the whole account, together the Liquidation Process, and may charge a liquidation fee \citep[Rulebook v2.0, Rules 10.1 and~10.5]{gapclose-deribit-rules};
\item \emph{insurance fund}: if an account's margin balance in a currency is negative after liquidation, Deribit transfers assets from its insurance fund, funded mainly by liquidation fees and excess collateral from liquidations, to bring it to zero; if the fund in that currency is depleted, Deribit may, in its absolute discretion, convert another currency of the fund (Rules 10.6--10.7; definition of Insurance Fund);
\item \emph{socialised loss}: Deribit may, in its absolute discretion, trigger the Socialised Loss Mechanism if the insurance fund is depleted in all currencies, or if it is depleted in the bankrupt account's currency and Deribit decides not to convert other currencies (Rule 10.8), so the mechanism can be triggered while other currencies remain in the fund, and need not be triggered when the fund is exhausted. Once it is, Deribit may deduct from all members with positive profit and loss in the same day's session, in any currency, their pro-rata share of the negative balance, halt trading, cancel orders and close positions at the mark price; members agree to this and may dispute only a manifest error in their share (Rules 10.9--10.11);
\item \emph{auto-deleveraging}: a step in the liquidation waterfall in which, once liquidity-support providers' capacity is used, eligible positions of a distressed account are transferred to a deleveraged counterparty at a preferential price and closed against that member's existing positions (definition of Auto-Deleveraging; the full waterfall is in the Exchange Membership Terms, clause~12);
\item \emph{custody}: Deribit holds members' assets in its own wallets or through a third-party custodian of its choice, or acts as limited agent over a member-chosen custodian for margining, liquidation and settlement (Rule~2, guidance);
\item \emph{stablecoin, network and bridge risk}: redemption and depeg of stablecoin collateral, chain congestion, reorganisations and transfer delays. \emph{One depeg documented}. In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank entered receivership on Friday 10 March at 11:37~a.m.\ ET. Circle announced around 10~p.m.\ that it could not withdraw \$3.3 billion of USDC reserves held there, about 8 percent of the total. Redemptions had already surged before the announcement. Primary issuance and redemption, ``constrained by working hours of the U.S. banking systems'', largely stopped over the weekend, and USDC fell to 86 cents on secondary markets. Dai's one-to-one Peg Stability Modules against USDC were drained, and stablecoins with no SVB exposure also lost their pegs. The depegs eased only after the FDIC, Treasury and Federal Reserve announced full protection of SVB depositors \citep[FEDS Notes, 17 December 2025, summary and sec.~1]{feds-2025-svb-stablecoins}.

So a fully backed stablecoin used as collateral can lose its peg when its primary market is closed and its reserves are frozen, and smart-contract links can pass the loss to other coins.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} All rules above are Deribit FZE's, a virtual-asset service provider regulated by Dubai's VARA, under Rulebook version 2.0 applicable 13 August 2026 (Rule 1.1); other venues' waterfalls may differ. Venue insolvency is a separate legal process from a venue's socialised-loss mechanism.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: keep apart a trader's own liquidation (its positions closed), a loss to the venue's backstop (insurance fund), a loss charged to other members (socialised loss), a position closed against a counterparty's will (auto-deleveraging) and a loss of assets held at the venue (custody, insolvency). Cross-venue strategies add transfer and settlement paths between venues, during which capital cannot respond. Margin and default waterfalls in a regulated futures clearing house are in \hyperref[mech:futures-margin-settlement]{futures margin and settlement}.

\emph{Settlement finality and reorganisations}. For a systemically important stablecoin arrangement, CPMI and IOSCO apply Principle~8: clearly define ``the point at which a transfer of a stablecoin through the operational method used becomes irrevocable and unconditional'', give it a clear legal basis, and prevent ``any misalignment between the state of the ledger and legal finality''. They note that with probabilistic settlement finality ``can never be achieved with absolute certainty'' and forks can revoke transactions, the risk growing with the number of transactions added (secs.~2 and~3.4, PDF pp.~5 and~16) \citep[July 2022, secs.~2 and~3.4]{cd-cpmi-iosco-stablecoin-pfmi-2022}. Under Principle~9 a stablecoin used for money settlements ``should have little or no credit or liquidity risk'', judged by the holder's legal claim, the reserves and timely conversion at par. This frames chain reorganisations and transfer delay as settlement-finality questions.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Venue risk (outage, withdrawal halt, auto-deleveraging or insolvency), liquidation risk, stablecoin risk, bridge and settlement risk, and fragmentation are distinct layers. Auto-deleveraging and liquidation are described for Deribit only; fragmentation is a market-structure fact (\hyperref[def:crypto-venue-instrument-structure]{crypto venue and instrument structure}), linked by Makarov and Schoar to capital controls rather than to slow crypto transfers.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mech:perpetual-funding]{perpetual funding}; \hyperref[def:crypto-venue-instrument-structure]{crypto venue and instrument structure}; \hyperref[mech:futures-margin-settlement]{futures margin and settlement}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfevidence}[Crypto carry on dated futures: size, drivers (small traders' demand for leverage) and limits to arbitrage (spot access, margin and cross-margining), 2019--2024]\label{ev:crypto-dated-futures-carry}
\qffield{Statement} Schmeling, Schrimpf and Todorov measure the annualised basis of one- and three-month constant-maturity bitcoin and ether futures over spot, using data from Binance, OKEx, FTX, Huobi, BitMEX, Deribit and the CME (the CME from August 2020) \citep[revision of 1 October 2025, abstract, sec.~1 and data section, PDF pp.~3--9]{roadmap-carry}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Size.} Carry ``can reach exceptionally high levels, sometimes exceeding 40\% per annum''. It averaged about 7 percent a year across exchanges from April 2019 to July 2024 (pp.~3 and~5).
\item \emph{Not fundamentals.} In $F_t=S_te^{(r_t+u_t-y_t)(T-t)}$, interest rates bear ``little to no relation'' to the carry and storage costs are negligible. That implies a large negative convenience yield: investors prefer being long futures to holding spot (p.~5).
\item \emph{Demand.} Higher carry goes with larger net long positions of smaller traders in the CFTC's Commitments of Traders data, who add positions in strong trends and periods of attention. Dealers and leveraged funds take the short side. Introducing CME micro bitcoin futures raised CME carry relative to other venues in a difference-in-differences test (pp.~6--7).
\item \emph{Limits to arbitrage.} Regulation long kept many professionals from holding spot, and margin frictions bind. With no cross-margining on traditional exchanges, a long spot position cannot collateralise a short futures position. The futures leg suffers severe drawdowns, and carry predicts liquidations of short futures. \emph{Scale, from Table~7}: in OKEx data, a monthly regression of next-month sell liquidations, ``scaled by open interest'', on the standardised one-month basis, with implied volatility, realised skew and open interest as controls, has a basis coefficient of 2.22 (standard error 0.79) and an intercept of 10.84 (1,619 observations, adjusted $R^2$ 0.29; Table~7, col.~2, printed p.~41, PDF p.~44).

Since the text expresses liquidations ``as a percentage of open interest'' (PDF p.~25), a one-standard-deviation rise in carry goes with sell liquidations about 2.2 percentage points of open interest higher, against a level of about 10.8 percent when the regressors are at their means; buy liquidations are not predicted (0.41, s.e.\ 0.45). In the subsample built to exclude profit-taking (basis widening on at least 70 percent of days in the two weeks before expiry), the coefficient is 2.02 on an intercept of 9.33 (211 observations).

The prose states the effect in two ways: ``an increase in the standardized carry by 10\% predicts an increase in liquidations of short futures positions by 22\% of total open interest'' (PDF p.~7) and ``a rise in standardized carry by 10\% predicts a 22\% increase in total sell liquidations (relative to total open interest)'' (PDF p.~25). Neither maps cleanly onto the table: a 22 percent rise relative to the intercept is close to $2.22/10.84\approx20$ percent, while ``22\% of total open interest'' is ten times the coefficient, and what ``10\%'' of a standardised variable means is not stated. The earlier ``liquidations of 22 percent of open interest'' in this entry is withdrawn.

The liquidation measure ``capture[s] both forced liquidations by the exchange due to missed margin calls and voluntary liquidations by the trader'' (PDF p.~25), so it is contract closing, not forced liquidation alone. After spot bitcoin ETFs were introduced in 2024, carry fell by about 3 percentage points across exchanges and 5 more on the CME, 36 and 97 percent of the mean (p.~8).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} The evidence is for fixed-date futures, which converge to spot at expiry; perpetual futures are discussed only through other papers (p.~8). The causal claims rest on two difference-in-differences designs around specific events. The exchange sample includes FTX, which failed in November 2022.

\qffield{Practice} A cash-and-carry trade that ``appears risk-free'' in the absence of frictions (p.~5) carries funding and liquidation risk before convergence. Size it against the margin path and the venue's liquidation rules, not against the annualised basis alone (\hyperref[def:crypto-trading-risk-layers]{crypto trading risk layers}). For perpetuals, the paper restates other work: He et al.\ find that perpetuals ``typically trade at a positive funding rate, that is, the long side pays the short side'', and Christin et al.\ that a carry trade short perpetual futures earns high Sharpe ratios (p.~8).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Table~5 (printed p.~39, PDF p.~42) regresses the one-month basis on treatment and post dummies in two-month windows: around the spot-ETF launch, $Post=-0.03$ and $Treat\times Post=-0.05$ (both $s.e.$ 0.01; intercept 0.13; 291 observations), and around CME's micro futures $Treat\times Post=+0.11$ (intercept 0.28; 292 observations). The basis is in decimals of an annual rate, so the coefficients are the text's ``around 3\%'' and ``5\%'' in annualised percentage points of basis, matching the Size bullet's statement.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mech:perpetual-funding]{perpetual funding}; \hyperref[res:cost-of-carry-forward-price]{cost-of-carry forward price} (the pricing equation); \hyperref[def:contango-backwardation-stocks]{convenience yield in commodities} (opposite sign).

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{roadmap-perpetual-fundamentals} for funding rates; \nolinkurl{s11-r15}.

\end{qfevidence}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Automated market maker pools: the constant-product rule and its fee, price impact and the cost of providing liquidity, and concentrated liquidity in Uniswap v3]\label{def:amm-pool-mechanics}
\qffield{Statement} A Uniswap pair pools reserves $x$ and $y$ of two tokens. Uniswap v1 and v2 maintain ``the invariant that the product of the reserves cannot decrease''; ``traders pay a 30-basis-point fee on trades, which goes to liquidity providers'' \citep[sec.~1, p.~1]{uniswap-v2-2020}. Version~2 enforces the fee through $(x_1-0.003\,x_{in})(y_1-0.003\,y_{in})\ge x_0y_0$, with $x_0,y_0$ the balances before and $x_1,y_1$ after the swap (sec.~3.2.1, eq.~(10), p.~7). Uniswap v3 keeps $x\cdot y=k$ but lets a liquidity provider confine liquidity $L=\sqrt k$ to a price range $[p_a,p_b]$, a \emph{position} \citep[sec.~2, eqs.~(2.1)--(2.2), p.~2]{s6-uniswap}. Its real reserves satisfy
\[ \Big(x+\frac{L}{\sqrt{p_b}}\Big)\big(y+L\sqrt{p_a}\big)=L^2, \]
so it trades like a constant-product pool with larger ``virtual reserves'' inside the range. Outside the range it holds one asset only, ``is no longer active, and no longer earns fees''. Fee tiers are set per pool, initially 0.05, 0.30 and 1 percent (sec.~1, p.~1).

\qffield{Notation} Derived, with price $p=y/x$ and fee $f$, $\gamma=1-f$: selling $\Delta x$ returns $\Delta y=y\gamma\Delta x/(x+\gamma\Delta x)$. A position's real reserves at price $p$ inside its range are $x=L(1/\sqrt p-1/\sqrt{p_b})$ and $y=L(\sqrt p-\sqrt{p_a})$.

\qffield{Conditions} The formulas are the protocols' design as described in their 2020 and 2021 whitepapers. Deployed pools, fee switches, later versions (UniswapX, v4) and other AMM designs differ. Gas costs, transaction ordering and the maximal extractable value they enable are outside these papers.

\qffield{Practice} Numerical checks:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Price impact.} Selling 10 ETH into a 1{,}000 ETH and 2{,}000{,}000 USDC pool returns 19{,}743.16 USDC, an execution price of 1{,}974.32 against a pool price of 2{,}000 before and 1{,}960.65 after. Before the fee the shortfall is $\gamma\Delta x/(x+\gamma\Delta x)$, 0.99 percent for a trade of 1 percent of the reserve. The product $k$ rises by the fee retained.
\item \emph{Cost of providing liquidity.} With arbitrageurs moving the pool to an external price $p_0r$, a full-range position is worth $2\sqrt r/(1+r)$ times the buy-and-hold value before fees: $-5.7$ percent for $r=2$ or $1/2$, and $-20$ percent for $r=4$. That is the ``impermanent loss'' that fee income must cover.
\item \emph{Concentration.} The same $L$ on $[1600,2500]$ at a price of 2{,}000 needs 94{,}427 of capital against 894{,}427 on the full range, 9.5 times less. Below $p_a$ the position is all X (50 units); above $p_b$, all Y (100{,}000).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Fees, block times, gas and pool design}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Fees and blocks} (\nolinkurl{roadmap-amm-fees}, arXiv v2 of 23 July 2025). With a fee $\gamma$ and Poisson block arrivals at rate $\lambda$, the probability that a block contains a profitable arbitrage is $P_{\mathrm{trade}}=1/(1+\sqrt{2\lambda}\,\gamma/\sigma)$; for small fees and fast blocks, arbitrage profit is approximately loss-versus-rebalancing times $P_{\mathrm{trade}}$ and fees paid by arbitrageurs approximately LVR times $(1-P_{\mathrm{trade}})$, so ``faster blockchains will result in reduced LP losses''; with fixed gas costs added, ``lower gas fees lead to smaller losses for LPs'' (abstract and sec.~1, pp.~1--4) \citep[arXiv v2, abstract and sec.~1, pp.~1--4]{roadmap-amm-fees}. That is the gas-cost question in theory.
\item \emph{Reported prices} (\nolinkurl{s6-cfmm}, arXiv v4). A CFMM's reported price is the slope of a supporting hyperplane of its reachable set; an arbitrageur facing reference prices $c$ is ``always incentivized'' to move the pool there, so the pool tracks the reference market. A fee of $1-\gamma$ on inputs makes a path-independent CFMM path-deficient: a trader must put in $1/\gamma$ more input (sec.~3, pp.~13--16 and~20--21) \citep[arXiv v4, sec.~3, pp.~13--21]{s6-cfmm}. With a fee, a constant-product pool's price is pushed only into a band around the reference price (own reading of the path-deficiency result, not a quoted bound). Angeris, Kao, Chiang, Noyes and Chitra show that under common conditions constant-product markets ``must closely track the reference market price'' and simulate stability under a wide range of conditions \citep[arXiv v7, abstract]{s11-r12}.
\item \emph{Many assets} (\nolinkurl{roadmap-cfmm-convex}, arXiv v1, July 2021): choosing a multi-asset trade against a CFMM can be posed as a convex optimisation problem and solved reliably (abstract) \citep[arXiv v1, abstract and sec.~1]{roadmap-cfmm-convex}.
\item \emph{Uniswap v4} (\nolinkurl{exp-uniswap4}, August 2024): pools keep v3's concentrated liquidity, but a pool's creator can attach a ``hook contract'' run at set points of a call, which can ``manage the swap fee of the pool dynamically, implement custom curves, and adjust fees''; all pools sit in one singleton contract with ``flash accounting'', which nets token balances within a lock to cut gas on multi-pool trades (abstract and sec.~1, pp.~1--2) \citep[whitepaper, August 2024, pp.~1--2]{exp-uniswap4}.
\item \emph{Auctioning the pool} (\nolinkurl{exp-amamm}, arXiv v4 of 12 February 2025): an on-chain auction for the right to be ``pool manager'' of a constant-product pool, who sets the fee, receives fees and can capture some arbitrage; they ``prove that under certain assumptions'' this pool has higher equilibrium liquidity than any fixed-fee AMM (abstract) \citep[arXiv v4, abstract]{exp-amamm}. A design proposal with proofs under assumptions, not evidence of deployment.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} The whitepapers are the developers' design descriptions, not audits of deployed code. The impermanent-loss expression assumes the pool is arbitraged to an outside price and ignores fees and gas; both are own derivations, not statements of the sources.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{roadmap-uniswapx}; \nolinkurl{roadmap-cfmm-routing-efficient}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmechanism}[DeFi lending liquidations: fixed-spread and auction designs, close factors and health factors, and how much becomes liquidatable when prices fall]\label{mech:defi-lending-liquidation}
\qffield{Statement} Qin, Zhou, Gamito, Jovanovic and Gervais study Aave, Compound, MakerDAO and dYdX, over 85 percent of Ethereum lending, from their launch to April 2021. Two designs dominate: auctions (MakerDAO) and fixed-spread liquidation (Aave, Compound, dYdX), where a liquidator repays debt and claims collateral worth the repaid debt times $(1+LS)$, with LS the liquidation spread, up to a \emph{close factor}, the maximum share of the debt repayable in one liquidation (sec.~2.3, eq.~(1), PDF p.~3).

Over the period, liquidation proceeds were \$807.46 million in 28{,}138 events by 2{,}011 liquidator addresses; 641 auction liquidations were unprofitable for liquidators; Aave V2 had accumulated \$87.4 thousand of bad debt by the end of April 2021; and a 43 percent fall in ETH, like 13 March 2020, would make \$1.07 billion of collateral liquidatable on MakerDAO. Fixed-spread designs ``favor liquidators over borrowers'' because they allow more collateral than necessary to be sold (sec.~1, PDF p.~2) \citep[arXiv v2, secs.~1--2, PDF pp.~1--3]{roadmap-defi-liquidations}. Perez, Werner, Xu and Livshits find on Compound to September 2020 that ``variations of only 3\% in an asset's dollar price can result in over 10m USD becoming liquidable'', with over 70 percent of liquidatable positions liquidated immediately (abstract) \citep[arXiv v6, abstract]{oc-perez-knife-edge-2021}.

In Aave V3, the V2 rule that only half a position could be liquidated at once was relaxed: a position ``approaching insolvency (HF $<$ 0.95)'' can be liquidated in full; on layer-2 chains a grace period applies after a sequencer outage for $0.95<HF<1$ (PDF pp.~7--8 and~14) \citep[Aave V3 Technical Paper, 27 January 2022, PDF pp.~7--8 and~14]{al-aave}.

\qffield{Notation} HF the health factor, below one liquidatable; LS liquidation spread (bonus); CF close factor; LT liquidation threshold.

\qffield{Conditions} The figures are for 2019--2021 protocol versions and parameters, which governance changes; the HF definition in terms of collateral, liquidation thresholds and debt is the protocols' (not transcribed here).

\qffield{Practice} To assess a liquidation, record the protocol version and parameters at the pinned block, compute the maximum repay amount from the close factor and the HF band, and net the bonus of gas and DEX slippage before calling a liquidation profitable.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The Aave paper is the developers' description; parameters such as the e-mode example (97 percent LTV, 98 percent threshold, 2 percent bonus for stablecoins, PDF p.~6) are examples, not current values.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:amm-pool-mechanics]{AMM pools}; \hyperref[def:crypto-trading-risk-layers]{crypto trading risk layers}; \hyperref[mod:liquidity-spirals]{liquidity spirals}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{oc-heimbach-huang-2024}; \nolinkurl{oc-lehar-parlour-2022}; \nolinkurl{oc-eskandari-oracles-2021}.

\end{qfmechanism}

\begin{qfevidence}[Stablecoin runs and flights to safety, and stablecoin flows in Treasury bill yields]\label{ev:stablecoin-runs}
\qffield{Statement} Anadu et al.\ document net flows ``from riskier to safer stablecoins on days of crypto-market stress'' and estimate a break-the-buck threshold at \$1: a coin priced at or above \$1 has flows uncorrelated with its price, while below \$1 its daily outflow rate ``jumps by 34 basis points''; the 2022 (Terra) and 2023 runs show the same flight to safety, and flows tend to stay within a blockchain, as money-fund flows stay within fund families (abstract and sec.~1, PDF pp.~2 and~4--5) \citep[Staff Report~1073, September 2023, revised April 2024, abstract and sec.~1]{cd-anadu-2023}.

Ahmed and Aldasoro estimate, with instrumented local projections on daily data from 2021 to 2025, that ``a 2-standard deviation inflow into stablecoins lowers 3-month Treasury yields by 2--2.5 basis points within 10 days'', with little effect at longer tenors, and that outflows raise yields two to three times as much as inflows lower them (abstract) \citep[BIS WP~1270, May 2025 version, abstract]{rt-ahmed-aldasoro-stablecoins}.

\qffield{Conditions} Safe and risky are classified from issuers' reported reserves, whose reporting quality varies (Anadu et al., n.~6). The yield estimate is for 2021--2025 and depends on the instrument.

\qffield{Practice} A depeg can be dated as the first close below \$1, the threshold at which these authors find redemptions accelerate. \emph{Discrepancies noted}: the guide's reading note for this paper says ``the USD 0.99 break-the-buck threshold''; the paper estimates \$1. The paper's introduction calls the money-fund threshold ``95-cent'' while its later section defines breaking the buck as a market-price NAV below \$0.995 (PDF p.~5 against the money-fund background section); the latter is the usual definition.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Figures are from the BIS paper's May 2025 version; the catalogue records a later revision with data to March 2026.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[ev:money-fund-runs]{money-fund runs}; \hyperref[rule:stablecoin-standards]{international stablecoin standards}; \hyperref[def:crypto-trading-risk-layers]{crypto trading risk layers}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{wide-lyons-stablecoin}; \nolinkurl{wide-liu-makarov-schoar-terra}.

\end{qfevidence}

\begin{qfrule}[International standards for stablecoins: the FSB's redemption, stabilisation and reserve recommendations (July 2023)]\label{rule:stablecoin-standards}
\qffield{Statement} The FSB's high-level recommendations for ``global stablecoin'' arrangements require that authorities ensure ``a robust legal claim to all users against the issuer and/or underlying reserve assets'' and ``timely redemption''; ``For GSCs referenced to a single fiat currency, redemption should be at par into fiat''; and an effective stabilisation mechanism, clear redemption rights and prudential requirements (Recommendation~9, PDF pp.~13--14) \citep[Final Report, 17 July 2023, Recommendation~9, printed pp.~9--10]{cd-fsb-stablecoin-2023}. Because Recommendation~9 requires an effective stabilisation mechanism, ``so-called algorithmic stablecoins do not meet the High-Level Recommendations'' (n.~2, printed p.~1). CPMI and IOSCO apply the PFMI's settlement-finality and money-settlement principles to systemically important arrangements (\hyperref[def:crypto-trading-risk-layers]{crypto trading risk layers}).

\qffield{Conditions} These are international recommendations to authorities, not binding law; jurisdictions implement them in their own regimes (for example the EU's MiCA).

\qffield{Practice} For a design check, ask in order: who holds the legal claim and on what, at par and how fast; what reserves back it and how they are managed; and what makes a transfer final on the chain used.

\qffield{Pitfalls} A \emph{global} stablecoin is a category defined by potential reach across jurisdictions; the recommendations apply in full to that category.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[ev:stablecoin-runs]{stablecoin runs}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{cd-cpmi-iosco-stablecoin-pfmi-2022}; \nolinkurl{cd-davernas-maurin-vandeweyer-2026}.

\end{qfrule}

\begin{qfmechanism}[Auto-deleveraging on perpetual venues: last-resort loss socialisation, its trilemma, and the 10 October 2025 cascade]\label{mech:crypto-auto-deleveraging}
\qffield{Statement} Chitra describes auto-deleveraging (ADL) as ``a last-resort loss socialization mechanism for perpetual futures venues'', triggered ``when solvency-preserving liquidations fail'', after insurance funds are exhausted, and proves a trilemma: ``no policy can simultaneously satisfy exchange solvency, revenue, and fairness to traders''. On Hyperliquid on 10 October 2025, ADL closed \$2.1 billion of positions in 12 minutes; against transparent benchmark allocations the production algorithm ``overshot the minimum trader profit haircut'', costing profitable traders an estimated \$45.0 to \$51.7 million, about \$653.6 million of positions, and the comparison suggests Binance ``overutilized ADL far more'' (abstract and sec.~1) \citep[arXiv v3, abstract and sec.~1]{cd-chitra-adl-2025}.

\qffield{Conditions} The excess-haircut estimates depend on the author's benchmark allocations and on reconstructed venue data; the author is at Gauntlet, a risk-management firm for crypto protocols.

\qffield{Practice} To analyse a liquidation cascade, write each venue's waterfall (liquidation, insurance fund, ADL or socialised loss) from its rules, and record which positions ADL closed and at what price; compare with \hyperref[def:crypto-trading-risk-layers]{crypto trading risk layers}, which describes one venue's rules.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The paper's numbers are one event; whether Hyperliquid's later open-interest loss is due to ADL is the author's conjecture (sec.~1).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mech:perpetual-funding]{perpetual funding}; \hyperref[mod:liquidity-spirals]{liquidity spirals}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{cd-soska-2021}; \nolinkurl{exp-brunnermeier-pedersen-liquidity}.

\end{qfmechanism}

\begin{qfmodel}[Market and funding liquidity spirals: when margins are destabilising (Brunnermeier and Pedersen)]\label{mod:liquidity-spirals}
\qffield{Statement} Speculators who provide liquidity are financed against margins. Brunnermeier and Pedersen call margins \emph{destabilising} ``if they can increase in illiquidity''. When the financier knows the fundamental value and that prices will return to it, margins are stabilising: illiquidity lowers the margin on the side that buys low or sells high, a ``cushioning'' effect (Proposition~1). When the financier is uninformed and fundamentals have ARCH-type volatility, margins rise with observed price moves, $m=\bar\sigma+\bar\theta|\Delta p|$, including moves caused by liquidity shocks (Proposition~2).

With destabilising margins or large existing positions, liquidity can be fragile, with multiple equilibria and ``a discontinuous drop of market liquidity'' after small losses. A \emph{margin spiral} (tighter margins force selling) and a \emph{loss spiral} (losses on existing positions force selling) reinforce each other (sec.~1, printed pp.~2--4; Propositions~1--3, printed pp.~19--21) \citep[NBER WP~12939, February 2007, sec.~1 and Propositions~1--3]{exp-brunnermeier-pedersen-liquidity}.

\qffield{Notation} $m$ margin; $\bar\sigma$, $\bar\theta$ parameters of the volatility process; $\Delta p$ the observed price change; $\Lambda$ the gap between price and fundamental.

\qffield{Conditions} Model of 2007--2009. Destabilising margins need financiers who cannot tell fundamental from liquidity shocks; the model is stylised (three periods), and the empirical motivation is margins on S\&P~500 futures in 1987, 1990 and 1998.

\qffield{Practice} A crypto liquidation cascade fits the margin-spiral reading when venues raise maintenance requirements or mark against falling prices and forced sales move the mark; whether it does is an empirical question.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Propositions follow the 2007 working paper, which may differ from the \emph{Review of Financial Studies} version in \hyperref[def:common-investor-contagion]{common-investor contagion}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mech:crypto-auto-deleveraging]{auto-deleveraging}; \hyperref[def:common-investor-contagion]{common-investor contagion}; \hyperref[mech:defi-lending-liquidation]{DeFi liquidations}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{exp-brunnermeier-pedersen-liquidity} (published version).

\end{qfmodel}
