\begin{qfdefinition}[Contango, backwardation and ending stocks: storage and financing costs, scarcity premia, seasonal crop curves, the stocks-to-use ratio, and roll yield stated carefully]\label{def:contango-backwardation-stocks}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner: commodities are driven by supply (producers, costs, yield, weather, politics) and demand (population, development, alternative energy) \citep[chap.~10, secs.\ ``Supply Drivers'' and ``Demand Drivers'', PDF pp.~293--296]{s7-gliner}. \emph{Ending stock} ``effectively measures the inventory of a particular commodity''; crops are harvested once or twice a year while demand is continuous, and the stock carried over becomes the next season's beginning balance (sec.\ ``Ending Stock'', p.~296, Figure~10.1).

\emph{Contango} is near-term futures trading below later ones, from storage, transport and the opportunity cost of capital (gold in a vault). \emph{Backwardation} is near-term futures at a premium, as in a supply shortage (cocoa during an Ivory Coast export stoppage) or just before a harvest when stocks are depleted. A crop curve can show both, contango after harvest and backwardation before the next (sec.\ ``Contango and Backwardation'', pp.~297--298, Figure~10.2, corn as of 30 September 2013).

\qffield{Notation} Balance sheet: beginning stocks $+$ production $+$ imports $-$ consumption $-$ exports $=$ ending stocks; stocks-to-use $=$ ending stocks $/$ total use. Full carry: $F=S e^{(r+u-y)T}$ with storage cost $u$ and convenience yield $y$ (\hyperref[res:cost-of-carry-forward-price]{cost-of-carry forward price}).

\qffield{Conditions} The identity ``ending stocks $=$ beginning stocks $+$ production $-$ consumption'' holds for the world, not for a country, which also has trade; with imports of 5 and exports of 12 on a balance of 10, 100 and 95, ending stocks are 8, not 15. Low stocks relative to use support prices and make them sensitive to small supply changes, as in the storage model; calling them the most important driver of commodity prices overstates that relation.

\qffield{Practice} The monthly WASDE report \citep[catalogue entry]{physical-usda-wasde-2026} gives USDA's U.S.\ and world supply-and-demand balances. Contango as the normal state fits monetary metals with large above-ground stocks, such as gold; it is not a general rule for storable commodities, which can be backwardated when inventories are scarce.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Contango gives a negative and backwardation a positive roll yield only for the roll component holding the curve fixed, not for total returns, and the sign says nothing on its own about a risk premium: whether backwardation reflects scarcity or compensation for risk is a separate question. \emph{Own bounded test}. Data: EIA's public NYMEX series (contracts 1, 2 and~4, which EIA stopped updating on 5 April 2024) and EIA's weekly inventories. Backwardation is $B_t=\ln(C_{1,t}/C_{2,t})$ at complete month-ends. The inventory signal is the log deviation from the same week's mean over the previous five years, taken from the latest weekly report treated as public by settlement on $t$.

The return is next month's return from holding contract~2, and each pair of month-ends is kept only if contract~2 at $t$ is contract~1 at $t+1$ under NYMEX's last-trading-day rules. The hypotheses and pass rules were written into the script before its first run. They were not preregistered.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Scarcity: supported.} Backwardation rises as inventories fall. WTI, August 1987 to February 2024 (438 months): $\beta=-0.111$, Newey--West $t=-5.9$ ($C_1/C_4$: $t=-6.4$). Henry Hub, January 2015 to February 2024 (110 months): $\beta=-0.114$, $t=-4.3$. Both survive a two-week reporting lag.
\item \emph{Premium: not supported.} On the full price-only samples (WTI, January 1985 to February 2024, 470 months; Henry Hub, January 1994 to February 2024, 362 months), the slope of next-month returns on $B_t$ is $+0.02$ ($t=0.1$) for WTI and $-0.05$ ($t=-0.3$) for gas. Inventories add nothing. Out-of-sample $R^2$ against the historical mean is $-1.8$ percent for WTI (Clark--West $t=-0.4$) and $-0.8$ percent for gas ($t=-1.4$, from January 2004).
\end{itemize}
Settlement-to-settlement figures are closing-price associations, since the signal and the entry use the same settlement. An \emph{executable variant} forms the signal at settlement~$t$ and trades the next day's settlement in the same contract, with two ticks per round trip (fills at settlement and the cost are assumptions; for Henry Hub, NYMEX's TAS order type covered the spot and the second through seventh months by May 2019, NYMEX Submission~19-158; whether TAS was available for Henry Hub earlier in the sample is unverified). Exploratory only, added after the first run and not part of the test: in this variant WTI's long-backwardation, short-contango rule earned $+0.92$ percent a month (Newey--West $t=2.3$; $+1.91$ against $-0.16$ percent), while gas earned $+0.58$ ($t=0.9$). Two WTI and three gas months exceed $\pm50$ percent and are retained in every estimate.

\emph{A third commodity}. The same code, imported unchanged, was applied to NYMEX heating oil (EIA's ``New York Harbor No.~2 Heating Oil Future'', contracts 1 and~2) with U.S.\ distillate stocks (WDISTUS1, same Wednesday release as crude, so the crude lag of six days). The hypotheses, timing rules and acceptance criteria were recorded before the data were downloaded, though not preregistered. The expiry rule assumed (last business day of the month before delivery) is EIA's own statement of the contract it reports: ``For gasoline, heating oil, and propane each contract expires on the last business day of the month preceding the delivery month.

Thus, the delivery month for Contract~1 is the calendar month following the trade date'' \citep[Futures Prices (NYMEX), definitions, ``Contract~1'']{eia-futures-definitions}. This is EIA's description, unconfirmed against NYMEX's rule text. The roll check agrees with it in six of seven five-year blocks (the seventh, 2024, has one roll), but it runs on the dates on which EIA reports both contracts, so it is consistent with the mapping and does not establish the exact expiry date.

\emph{Month-ends are the last common observation, not always the last trading day}: before about 2007 EIA reports contract~2 on only 100--190 days a year, so 79 of the 361 selected dates fall before the last date in the month on which EIA reports either contract, 12 by more than five days (at most 21, 9~August 1996); the gap is in the panel.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Scarcity: supported.} February 1994 to February 2024 (361 months), $\beta=-0.110$, $t=-5.3$, and $-0.104$ ($t=-5.3$) with a two-week lag.
\item \emph{Premium: not supported.} With the log held-contract return the protocol specifies, the slope is $+0.33$, $t=1.55$, below the threshold of~2. Out-of-sample $R^2$ is $-0.95$ percent from February 2004, with Clark--West $t=0.31$.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Return definition}. A simple-return specification gives slope $+0.36$ ($t=1.67$) and out-of-sample $R^2=-0.9$ percent (Clark--West $t=0.37$) from simple returns, which the imported WTI and gas code uses, although the heating-oil protocol had recorded log returns. Scarcity does not use returns and is unchanged. An exploratory regression, chosen after the results were seen and not part of the verdict, uses only the 243 month pairs whose two dates are both the month's last observed date: its slope is $+0.07$, with $t=0.20$.
The executable variant does not apply: heating oil expires at the month-end itself, so the contract that is $C_2$ at $t$ is already $C_1$ the next day. EIA's series keeps one label across the 2013 change from heating oil to ultra-low-sulphur diesel; whether and how EIA spliced the two contracts is unverified.

\emph{Hedging pressure}. The protocol was written before the positions data were downloaded. Hedging pressure is commercial traders' net short share in the CFTC's legacy futures-only report for WTI (contract market code 067651, whose name changed in 2000 and to ``WTI-Physical'' in February 2022), taken from the latest report whose Tuesday positions date is at least three days before the month-end \citep[legacy futures-only historical files, 1986--2024]{cftc-cot-history}. Over 458 months, January 1986 to February 2024, it averaged $+0.08$ (net short) and does not predict the next month's held-contract return: slope $-0.008$, Newey--West $t=-0.17$; out-of-sample $R^2=-1.1$ percent, Clark--West $t=-0.28$. It is nearly uncorrelated with the basis ($-0.07$), and adding it to the basis changes neither.

Hedging pressure is not supported on this rule; release delays such as government shutdowns are not modelled. Limits: three U.S.\ energy contracts; a monthly horizon; no metals or agriculture. EIA's release tables cover only 2025--2026 \citep[holiday tables]{eia-weekly-release-schedules}, so availability rests on a conservative convention: six days after the week ending (crude), seven (gas), and eleven over Christmas and New Year. Henry Hub's pre-1997 expiry rule is inferred from the data, not from a rulebook.
