\section{High-frequency trading: strategies, speed races and the costs of other traders}

\begin{qfdefinition}[High-frequency trading: proprietary strategies, the speed tools they share, and why the label names no single mechanism]\label{def:hft-strategies}
\qffield{Statement} In the SEC's 2010 concept release the term ``high frequency trading'' is ``relatively new and is not yet clearly defined''. It typically refers to professional traders acting in a proprietary capacity whose strategies generate many trades a day, organised as proprietary trading firms, proprietary desks of broker-dealers or hedge funds \citep[sec.~IV.B, p.~45]{sec-concept-release-2010}. Five characteristics are often attributed to them: (1) high-speed programs for generating, routing and executing orders; (2) co-location and individual data feeds to cut latency; (3) very short holding periods; (4) many orders cancelled shortly after submission; (5) ending the day close to flat (p.~45; restated in \citep[sec.~2.4.3, p.~163]{alpha-laruelle}). Rather than define HFT, the release treats \emph{strategies} and \emph{tools} separately. One firm may run many strategies at once, some generating many trades and some not, and ``conceivably'' some benefit market quality and long-term investors while others harm them \citep[sec.~IV.B, p.~46]{sec-concept-release-2010}. To present issues for comment, ``without limiting the broad request'', it describes four broad types of strategy often associated with proprietary firms \citep[sec.~IV.B.1, pp.~48--57]{sec-concept-release-2010}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{passive market making}: mainly non-marketable resting bids and offers, profiting from the spread and from liquidity rebates, and taking liquidity only to liquidate a position quickly. Layering many orders can give cancellation rates of 90 percent or more, with orders often lasting a second or less (pp.~48--49);
\item \emph{arbitrage}: capturing price discrepancies between related products or markets, such as an ETF against its basket, mostly by taking liquidity, with hedged positions that may last several days. The release asks how far such strategies trade price differences between trading centres and depend on latency between direct and consolidated data feeds (pp.~51--52);
\item \emph{structural}: exploiting structural vulnerabilities in the market or in other participants. Examples are using the fastest data to trade with participants still offering executions at stale prices, and setting a new NBBO with a small order and then triggering guaranteed-match trades on the other side (pp.~52--53);
\item \emph{directional}: a significant, unhedged position on an expected intraday move. Some, such as trading a temporary departure from fundamental value, ``often may contribute'' to price discovery. The release singles out two: \emph{order anticipation}, detecting large buyers or sellers and trading ahead of them, and \emph{momentum ignition}, orders and trades, perhaps with false rumours, meant to spark a rapid price move or trigger stop orders, followed by unwinding the position (pp.~53--57).
\end{itemize}
Many of these strategies are not new; what is new is the technology for identifying and executing them (p.~48). The tools discussed separately are co-location and individual trading-centre data feeds. Speed matters ``both in the absolute sense of achieving very small latencies and in the relative sense of being faster than competitors'' (sec.~IV.B.2, pp.~57--58).

\qffield{Conditions} Interpretation: speed and automation are means shared by these strategies, not a strategy, and the economic mechanism differs between them. Passive market making is paid the spread for supplying immediacy and bears inventory and adverse-selection risk (\hyperref[mod:avellaneda-stoikov]{Avellaneda--Stoikov}, \hyperref[meth:dealer-quote-adjustment]{dealer quote adjustment}). Arbitrage enforces relations between prices (\hyperref[def:arbitrage]{arbitrage}). Trading against stale quotes earns rents from reacting first to public information (\hyperref[res:latency-arbitrage-arms-race]{latency-arbitrage arms race}). Order anticipation profits from information about others' orders rather than about values (\hyperref[def:order-anticipation]{order anticipation}). Momentum ignition is a form of bluffing (\hyperref[def:bluffing-manipulation]{bluffing and manipulation}). Effects on liquidity, price discovery and other traders' costs must therefore be judged strategy by strategy. The release's four types organise a request for comment on U.S. equities in January 2010; they are not a universal classification, and they overlap: trading against stale prices is filed under ``structural'' and cross-venue price differences under ``arbitrage''.

\qffield{Practice} Lehalle and Laruelle describe high-frequency market makers in European equities around 2009--2012 as arbitraging between the venues that quote the same stock, which keeps those prices consistent. Maker--taker fee schedules on MTFs reward this, and trading very small orders very often lowers their inventory risk \citep[secs.~2.4 and~2.4.3, pp.~158 and~163]{alpha-laruelle}. Venues court them with low latency and co-location, ``especially since the lower the latency, the smaller the inventory risk''; the authors read one venue's low ``flow reactivity'' as its high-frequency market makers offering less liquidity when activity rises (p.~158). Probing orders are a tool, not a strategy. The release defines a ``pinging'' order as an immediate-or-cancel order used to find undisplayed liquidity, and distinguishes its use in a normal search for liquidity from its use to detect and trade in front of large interest \citep[p.~47, n.~69]{sec-concept-release-2010}. An order anticipator that detects a large buyer first buys, and is largely indifferent to whether the large trader is buying or selling; a long-term investor seeking liquidity wants the opposite side, and both parties benefit from that search \citep[p.~55]{sec-concept-release-2010}. Liquidity seeking by routers is in \hyperref[def:smart-order-routing]{smart order routing}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy taxonomy listed five strategies: passive market making, statistical arbitrage, latency arbitrage, order anticipation and liquidity detection. Compared with the release:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``statistical arbitrage'' on short-horizon relative value or lead--lag signals straddles the arbitrage and directional types;
\item ``latency arbitrage'' corresponds to the structural example of trading against stale prices, and across venues to the release's arbitrage question;
\item ``liquidity detection'' is a tool that order anticipators and ordinary liquidity seekers both use, not a strategy;
\item momentum ignition, which the release names, was missing.
\end{itemize}
The legacy closing sentence, that one firm may run several strategies whose risk and social value differ, matches p.~46, where the difference in social value is a possibility to assess, not a finding. A strategy label does not establish illegality. The release treats order anticipation that involves no breach of duty or misappropriation as a question of policy; front running in breach of a duty, false rumours and manipulative conduct were already prohibited (pp.~53--55 and~57). These legal remarks are as of January 2010 and are not statements of current law. The release's estimate that HFT was 50 percent or more of U.S. equity volume came from press reports (p.~45, n.~67). Lehalle and Laruelle's ``more than 80\% of the deals in the US, 40\% in Europe, and 35\% in Japan'' is reported as hearsay (``They are said to be'', p.~163) without source or date. Neither is a measured share.

\begin{qfresult}[Latency arbitrage in a continuous limit order book: stale quotes are sniped, the speed race dissipates the rents, and investors pay through the spread (Budish, Cramton and Shim)]\label{res:latency-arbitrage-arms-race}
\qffield{Statement} Budish, Cramton and Shim's model \citep[secs.~VI.A--VI.B, pp.~1576--1583]{wide-budish-cramton-shim}. A security $x$ trades on a continuous limit order book. Its value is perfectly correlated with a public signal $y$, which jumps as a compound Poisson process with rate $\lambda_{\mathrm{jump}}$ and absolute jump size $J$, and $x$ can always be liquidated at that value. Investors arrive at rate $\lambda_{\mathrm{invest}}$, each needing to buy or sell one unit, and take liquidity. Risk-neutral trading firms make and take liquidity, and messages that arrive together are processed serially in random order. In equilibrium exactly one unit is quoted on each side, a bid at $y-s/2$ and an ask at $y+s/2$; the two quotes may belong to one trading firm or to two different firms (Proposition~1(i), p.~1583). The authors describe play with one firm in the liquidity-provider role for simplicity, noting economically equivalent equilibria in which the role rotates or is random (pp.~1579--1580). When $y$ jumps by more than $s/2$, the firm whose quote is stale sends a cancel while the other $N-1$ firms send orders to trade against (``snipe'') it, so the stale quote is sniped with probability $(N-1)/N$ (Proposition~1(iii), p.~1583). The spread equates investors' payments with the rents from sniping:
\[ \lambda_{\mathrm{invest}}\,\frac{s}{2}=\lambda_{\mathrm{jump}}\Pr\Bigl(J>\frac s2\Bigr)\,\mathbb E\Bigl[J-\frac s2\Bigm|J>\frac s2\Bigr] \]
(eq.~(3), p.~1582), which does not depend on $N$. The spread is positive with symmetric information and no inventory costs. Sniping costs grow linearly with the quantity quoted, while the benefit of quoting more grows less than linearly, so the spread widens unit by unit and the book is thin (Proposition~2, pp.~1583--1585). This assumes investors trade their whole quantity at once, which the authors note is not optimal for them. When firms can rent speed at $c_{\mathrm{speed}}$ per unit time, the zero-profit conditions for the liquidity provider and the snipers (eqs.~(5)--(6), p.~1589) give
\[ \lambda_{\mathrm{invest}}\,\frac{s^*}{2}=N^*c_{\mathrm{speed}} \]
(eq.~(7), p.~1590): the total prize, investors' cost through the spread and the total spent on speed are equal, so the rents are dissipated by the race (Proposition~3, pp.~1590--1591; page images of pp.~1589--1590). The race is a prisoner's dilemma: welfare would be higher by $N^*c_{\mathrm{speed}}$ if the firms could commit not to invest (Proposition~4, p.~1591). The prize rises with the frequency of jumps and with a mean-preserving spread of their distribution, and does not depend on the cost of speed, the speed of the fastest firms or the speed difference (Proposition~5, p.~1592).

\qffield{Notation} $\lambda_{\mathrm{jump}},\lambda_{\mathrm{invest}}$ are Poisson arrival rates, not impact slopes; $s$ is the full bid--ask spread in price units; $N$ is the number of fast trading firms; $c_{\mathrm{speed}}$ is a rental cost per unit time. Starred values are equilibrium values with endogenous entry.

\qffield{Conditions} The assumptions are deliberately strong, a ``best case'' for the continuous book: a perfectly correlated public signal, costless liquidation, no asymmetric information or inventory costs, unit investor demand, prices on a continuum, and equally fast fast firms (pp.~1576--1579). With a discrete tick the liquidity-provider role is strictly preferred and firms race for the top of the book instead (n.~19, p.~1580). The authors do not claim that markets are less liquid than before electronic trading and HFT, which they say the evidence contradicts. Their claim is that markets are less liquid than under a design without sniping, the frequent batch auctions they propose (n.~5, pp.~1554--1555; abstract, p.~1548). Evidence (sec.~I, pp.~1552--1553, summarising sec.~V, which was not read): ES futures and SPY, 2005--2011, exchange direct-feed data. In 2011 the median return correlation was 0.1016 at 10~ms and 0.0080 at 1~ms. The median duration of ES--SPY arbitrage opportunities fell from 97~ms in 2005 to 7~ms in 2011, while the median profit, about 0.08 index points per unit traded, was roughly constant, and their frequency moved with volatility. The ES--SPY prize averaged \$75 million a year. The authors call extrapolation to other instruments ``common sense'' and decline to give a total.

\qffield{Practice} With speed costs that differ across firms, the marginal fast firm earns zero profit and inframarginal firms earn strictly positive profits; total sniping rents then exceed total spending on speed (n.~27, p.~1590). Lehalle and Laruelle describe a related competitive margin. High-frequency market makers extended coverage to less liquid stocks once their hedging was built and their technology, a fixed cost, was paid; growing competition between them ``forced them to diversify their sources of revenue''. Below a liquidity threshold, hedging and short-selling costs keep them out \citep[sec.~2.4.2, pp.~161--162]{alpha-laruelle}. This is their interpretation of coverage statistics for European indexes, 2009--2012, not a test.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy principle said that speed advantages are valuable only while competitors cannot replicate them, and that once speed diffuses, durable profits must come from inventory control, better models, lower costs or privileged order flow. In this model and its ES--SPY evidence, competition does not shrink the arbitrage prize; it raises the speed needed to win it, and under free entry the rents go into speed spending, which investors bear through the spread. What erodes is a firm's \emph{relative} lead: at the margin, profit net of speed costs is zero (Interpretation of eqs.~(5)--(7), checked algebraically and numerically). Lower costs of speed do sustain profits (n.~27). Inventory control, better models and privileged order flow are not in the passages read and remain unsupported legacy assertions, kept in the artefact. The empirical constancy is for one pair, 2005--2011. The pathway's evidence on races at a UK venue (\texttt{exp-aquilina-budish-oneill-races}) and on HFT profits and competition (\texttt{wide-baron-hft-competition}) is unread.

\begin{qfresult}[A narrower spread need not mean lower costs: Lehalle and Laruelle's stylised model of a fast trader quoting inside the book]\label{res:hft-spread-investor-cost}
\qffield{Statement} Lehalle and Laruelle's stylised book \citep[sec.~2.4.3, pp.~164--165, Figure~2.21, page images]{alpha-laruelle}. Two traders, A and B, rest at the best bid and ask, a spread $\psi$ apart, and take turns becoming impatient. Each pays $\psi$ when crossing and nothing when the other crosses, so each pays $\psi/2$ per trade on average. A high-frequency trader then quotes just inside B, so that A, crossing, pays $\psi/2+\epsilon$; it at once steps in front of A, and B pays $\psi/2+\epsilon$ too. The effective spread falls to a little over $\psi/2$, but A and B now pay $\psi/2+\epsilon$ on every trade, because each must cross every time, while the fast trader pays nothing. In the authors' words, ``the effective spread is reduced on an overall basis, but \dots\ costs for other participants can remain unchanged or slightly increased'' (p.~164). In the calibrated version, $\psi_B$ is the spread before HFT and $\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}$ the tighter one an HFT quotes. An investor hitting an HFT pays $\psi_B/2+\epsilon$ with $\epsilon=\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}/2$, and if HFTs are in a fraction $q$ of trades the spread becomes $\psi_N=q\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}+(1-q)\psi_B$ (p.~165). An investor posting one order meets an HFT with probability $r$ and then pays $(\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}+\psi_B)/2$. Otherwise, with equal probability, the investor crosses and pays $\psi_B$ or stays passive and pays 0, so
\[ \mathbb E[C]=\frac{\psi_B+r\,\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}}{2},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(C)=r\Bigl(\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}+\psi_B}{2}\Bigr)^2+(1-r)\frac{\psi_B^2}{2}-\mathbb E[C]^2 \]
(Figure~2.22, p.~166, page image). The average cost of $N$ independent trades has the same mean and variance $\operatorname{Var}(C)/N$ (p.~168). Meeting HFTs more often raises the expected cost and lowers its dispersion. The dispersion falls a lot for an investor with few trades, such as a retail investor, and little for one with many, such as a pension fund (pp.~167--169, Figures~2.23--2.24).

\qffield{Notation} $\psi$ is a spread in basis points of price; $C$ is one order's cost. Interpretation: in the figure each trader's cost is measured from its own resting quote, so a passive fill costs 0 and a crossing the full spread. $C$ is therefore a cost against waiting at one's own quote, not the half-spread against the midpoint of \hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}.

\qffield{Conditions} Calibration: $\psi_B=5.41$~bp (April to November 2007) and $\psi_N=4.73$~bp (April to November 2011), for CAC~40 stocks on the primary market, Euronext Paris, only, with $q=40$ percent assumed (pp.~165--166, page image of p.~166). Interpretation, recomputed with exact arithmetic: these imply $\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}=(\psi_N-(1-q)\psi_B)/q=3.71$~bp. $\mathbb E[C]$ then runs from 2.705~bp at $r=0$ to 4.56~bp at $r=1$, which matches the ends of Figure~2.24, and is 3.26~bp at $r=30$ percent and 3.63~bp at $r=50$ percent. The model attributes the whole fall in the primary-market spread between 2007 and 2011 to HFT and holds $\psi_B$ fixed, as if the earlier spread would otherwise have persisted. It fixes $q$ rather than measuring it, ignores trading moved to other venues, and assumes that an investor who meets an HFT must cross while others cross half the time. The two spreads are observations; their attribution to HFT and the cost model are the authors' stylised interpretation, not an estimate of HFT's effect.

\qffield{Practice} The legacy caveat's advice is consistent with this: judge fast liquidity provision by what other traders pay (effective and realised spreads, \hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}), by depth, adverse selection and behaviour under stress, not by quoted spreads or message counts. The criteria are the legacy note's (Interpretation). The SEC's 2010 release asked these as open questions. Are orders lasting a second or less ``phantom liquidity that disappears when most needed'', or do many firms quoting together give a stable market with many size updates but few changes in the NBBO price? Do proprietary firms, which have replaced specialists and market makers with affirmative and negative obligations, supply liquidity in times of stress \citep[sec.~IV.B.1, pp.~48--51]{sec-concept-release-2010}? In Budish, Cramton and Shim's model the cost of being sniped is priced into the spread and grows with the depth shown (\hyperref[res:latency-arbitrage-arms-race]{latency-arbitrage arms race}). Lehalle and Laruelle add that HFTs' preference for small trades makes other traders split orders and bear more reporting and technology costs, ``fixed losses'' unevenly distributed (p.~165); this is an argument, not a measurement.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The text says that raising the probability of meeting an HFT from 30 to 50 percent ``increases the expected cost by approximately 0.5bp'' (p.~168). The book's own formula and calibration give $0.2\times3.71/2=0.37$~bp, and Figure~2.24's markers sit at about 3.27 and 3.64~bp, which agrees with 0.37 (page image; Interpretation). Its ``by 20\%'' means 20 percentage points. The legacy caveat's ``high message rates can improve displayed prices'' and ``can also increase cancellation risk and stale-book uncertainty'' are, in the passages read, a stylised mechanism and open questions, not measured effects. The pathway's empirical readings on liquidity and price discovery (\texttt{wide-hendershott-jones-menkveld}, \texttt{wide-brogaard-hft-discovery}) are unread. The model's conclusion concerns expected cost per trade under its assumptions; it is not a finding that HFT raised investors' costs.
