\section{Inventory risk and the price of immediacy}

\begin{qfmodel}[Grossman--Miller market making: the price of immediacy]\label{mod:grossman-miller}
\qffield{Statement} Competitive, risk-averse market makers supply immediacy to a liquidity trader who arrives before the trader with the offsetting need, and the price concession they charge compensates exactly for carrying the inventory until that trader arrives \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~21]{crypto-cartea}. In the simplified three-date version, $n$ identical market makers, liquidity trader LT1 holding $i$ units and liquidity trader LT2 holding $-i$ units (arriving at date~2) trade an asset worth $S_3=\mu+\epsilon_2+\epsilon_3$ at date~3, and the date-1 equilibrium is \citep[same chap., pp.~23--24]{crypto-cartea}
\[ S_1=\mu-\gamma\sigma^2\,\frac{i}{n+1}, \qquad q_1^{MM}=q_1^{LT1}=\frac{i}{n+1}, \]
so LT1 sells $\frac{n}{n+1}\,i$ at once and each market maker holds $\frac{i}{n+1}$ until date~2, when LT2 arrives, the price is efficient, $S_2=\mathbb{E}[S_3\mid\epsilon_2]=\mu+\epsilon_2$, and every agent exits with zero inventory \citep[same chap., pp.~22--24]{crypto-cartea}. The liquidity discount is $\abs{S_1-\mu}=\gamma\sigma^2\abs{i}/(n+1)$; when LT1 buys ($i<0$) the market makers earn the same amount as a premium \citep[same chap., p.~24]{crypto-cartea}. Figure~\ref{fig:grossman_miller_trading} shows the holdings and trades.
\begin{figure}[H]

\caption{Holdings $q_t^j$ and trading in the Grossman--Miller model, after \citep[Figure~2.1, p.~23]{crypto-cartea}.}\label{fig:grossman_miller_trading}
\end{figure}

\qffield{Notation} Local override for this entry, \hyperref[res:grossman-miller-trading-fees]{the fee result} and \hyperref[res:grossman-miller-liquidity-measures]{the liquidity measures}: $\sigma^2$ is the variance of one period's value shock, in squared price units and not annualised; $\gamma$ is the coefficient of absolute risk aversion; $q_t^j$ is agent $j$'s holding on leaving date $t$, and $S_t$ the date-$t$ trade price.

\qffield{Conditions} Dates $t\in\{1,2,3\}$; shocks $\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3$ independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$, each announced between dates $t-1$ and $t$; every agent has CARA utility $U(X)=-\exp(-\gamma X)$; market makers start with no inventory; the arrival of LT2 at date~2 is certain; no trading or holding costs \citep[same chap., p.~21]{crypto-cartea}. Interpretation: the concession charges for risk, not for information; no agent is informed, which separates this model from \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle's}. Idea of the proof: solve backwards; with CARA utility and normal shocks each agent's demand is $(\mathbb{E}[\text{next price}]-\text{price})/(\gamma\sigma^2)$, and market clearing at each date gives the prices \citep[same chap., pp.~22--23]{crypto-cartea}; the full working is in the derivations artefact listed under \emph{Further reading}.

\qffield{Practice} The discount rises with the size of the liquidity need $\abs{i}$, risk aversion $\gamma$ and volatility $\sigma^2$, and falls with competition $n$; as $n$ grows the premium goes to zero, $S_1$ tends to $\mu$, and LT1's immediate trade tends to $i$ \citep[same chap., p.~24]{crypto-cartea}. Competition is itself set by participation costs: in Grossman and Miller's earlier entry stage, the number of market makers falls monotonically as the participation cost $c$ rises, which raises the discount \citep[same chap., p.~25]{crypto-cartea}. Per-share fees and two empirical liquidity measures follow from the same equilibrium: see \hyperref[res:grossman-miller-trading-fees]{trading fees} and \hyperref[res:grossman-miller-liquidity-measures]{price impact and return reversal}. Interpretation: the quoted inside spread implied by the model, a bid at $\mu-p$ and an ask at $\mu+p$ with $p=\gamma\sigma^2\abs{i}/(n+1)$, is $2p$.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The model solves a single Walrasian clearing rather than a trading mechanism \citep[same chap., pp.~26--27]{crypto-cartea}, and the offsetting trader arrives for sure; the arrival time and its uncertainty, which drive inventory risk in continuous-time quoting models, are absent. Interpretation: reading every concession as risk compensation overstates inventory costs where order flow is informed.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle single-auction model} (contrast: risk-neutral market makers, price impact from information); \hyperref[mod:avellaneda-stoikov]{Avellaneda--Stoikov quoting} (generalisation: inventory risk in continuous time with random order arrival); \hyperref[mod:static-limit-order-depth]{static limit-order depth} (the same immediacy supplied through posted orders).

\qffield{Further reading} \citep{grossman-miller-1988}, the original model, including participation costs (not yet read for this entry); own full derivation, from the legacy notes: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/derivations.tex}.

\qffield{Index} Grossman--Miller; immediacy; liquidity premium; liquidity discount; inventory risk; risk transfer; dealer competition; CARA--normal equilibrium

\end{qfmodel}

\begin{qfresult}[Grossman--Miller with per-share trading fees]\label{res:grossman-miller-trading-fees}
\qffield{Statement} If every trader pays a fee $\eta$ per share bought or sold, the date-2 price is unchanged, $S_2=\mu+\epsilon_2$, but for a seller ($i>0$) the date-1 price falls to \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', pp.~25--26]{crypto-cartea}
\[ S_1=\mu-\gamma\sigma^2\,\frac{i}{n+1}-2\Big(\frac{n}{n+1}\Big)\eta, \qquad q_1^{LT1,\ast}=\frac{i}{n+1}+2\Big(\frac{n}{n+1}\Big)\frac{\eta}{\gamma\sigma^2}. \]
The initiating liquidity trader pays his own fee and, through the lower price, the fraction $n/(n+1)$ of the market makers' round-trip fees $2\eta$, and keeps more inventory after date~1 than without fees \citep[same chap., p.~26]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Notation} As in \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{the Grossman--Miller model}.

\qffield{Conditions} The model of \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{the Grossman--Miller entry}, with fees proportional to shares rather than to dollar volume, positions remaining after date~2 liquidated at date~3, and desired trades large enough that every participant prefers trading to not trading \citep[same chap., p.~25]{crypto-cartea}. Idea of the proof: fees cancel at date~2 because remaining positions are sold either then or at date~3; at date~1 they reduce the market makers' demand by the round-trip fee, $q_1^{MM}=(\mu-S_1-2\eta)/(\gamma\sigma^2)$, and clearing $i=nq_1^{MM}+q_1^{LT1}$ gives the price \citep[same chap., pp.~25--26]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Practice} Liquidity traders whose desired trade is small relative to the fee do not trade at all \citep[same chap., p.~25]{crypto-cartea}. Participation costs and fees act differently: participation costs work through the number of market makers, while fees work mainly through the discount and the immediacy obtained, since each market maker pays $2\eta$ per trade but recovers $2n\eta/(n+1)$ through the discount \citep[same chap., p.~26]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Real exchange fees are often proportional to dollar volume and differ by side and by maker or taker \citep[same chap., p.~25]{crypto-cartea}; the closed form assumes the same per-share fee for everyone.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (the base case, $\eta=0$).

\qffield{Index} trading fees; exchange fees; fee incidence; participation cost; no-trade region; liquidity discount

\begin{qfresult}[Grossman--Miller liquidity measures: linear price impact and negative autocovariance]\label{res:grossman-miller-liquidity-measures}
\qffield{Statement} Read as trading in a limit-order book, where LT1's market orders walk the book posted by the market makers, the Grossman--Miller date-1 price is linear in the quantity LT1 trades \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', pp.~26--27]{crypto-cartea}:
\[ S_1=\mu+\lambda\,q^{LT1}, \qquad \lambda=-\frac{1}{n}\,\gamma\sigma^2, \qquad q^{LT1}=i\,\frac{n}{n+1}. \]
With public news $\epsilon_1$ before LT1 trades and efficient price $\mu_t=\mathbb{E}[S_3\mid\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_t]$, the price changes $\Delta_1=S_1-S_0$ and $\Delta_2=S_2-S_1$ satisfy \citep[same chap., pp.~27--28]{crypto-cartea}
\[ \operatorname{Cov}[\Delta_1,\Delta_2]=-\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}[q^{LT1}]<0. \]

\qffield{Notation} As in \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{the Grossman--Miller model}; here $q^{LT1}$ is the quantity LT1 trades at date~1, not a holding, and $\lambda$ is the slope of price on that quantity.

\qffield{Conditions} The model of \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{the Grossman--Miller entry}, with shocks $\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3$ i.i.d.\ normal with variance $\sigma^2$, and liquidity demand independent of news \citep[same chap., pp.~27--28]{crypto-cartea}. Idea of the proof: $S_1=\mu_1+\lambda q^{LT1}$ and $S_2=\mu_2$, so the order-pressure term enters $\Delta_1$ and reverses in $\Delta_2$ \citep[same chap., p.~28]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Practice} A more liquid market has a smaller $\abs{\lambda}$, from more competition, less risk aversion or lower volatility \citep[same chap., p.~27]{crypto-cartea}. As $\lambda$ goes to zero, so does the autocovariance, and the price process converges to the efficient-price martingale \citep[same chap., p.~28]{crypto-cartea}. The two measures coincide here only because the model is essentially static; in dynamic models they capture different things, permanent effects of order flow on the efficient price as against the temporary effect in each trader's execution price \citep[same chap., p.~28]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Interpretation: an empirical regression of price changes on signed order flow, or a negative return autocovariance, mixes this inventory effect with information and bid--ask bounce; this model alone cannot attribute it. Compare the information slope of \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle's model}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (source of the equilibrium); \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle single-auction model} (contrast: a price-impact slope from information).

\qffield{Index} price impact; Kyle's lambda (contrast); liquidity measure; autocovariance of price changes; return reversal; temporary impact; transitory price pressure

\end{qfresult}

\section{Quoting with limit orders}

\begin{qfmodel}[Static limit-order depth with exponential fill probability]\label{mod:static-limit-order-depth}
\qffield{Statement} A small, risk-neutral market maker with costless inventory and infinite patience chooses depths $\delta^\pm$ from the midprice $S_t$ for a sell order at $S_t+\delta^+$ and a buy order at $S_t-\delta^-$; a buy (sell) market order arrives with probability $p^+$ ($p^-$) and reaches depth $\delta$ with probability $e^{-\kappa^\pm\delta}$, and expected profit per trade is maximised by \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~29, eq.~(2.3)]{crypto-cartea}
\[ \max_{\delta^+,\delta^-}\Big\{p^+e^{-\kappa^+\delta^+}\delta^+ + p^-e^{-\kappa^-\delta^-}\delta^-\Big\}, \qquad \delta^{\pm,\ast}=\frac{1}{\kappa^\pm}. \]
The optimal depth equals the mean depth of the book on that side \citep[same chap., p.~30]{crypto-cartea}. This is a static simplification set up in place of the dynamic Ho--Stoll dealer model, which the authors give as the usual first reference \citep[same chap., p.~28]{crypto-cartea}. Figure~\ref{fig:limit_order_market_making} shows the depths and fill probabilities.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\scalebox{0.86}{\input{execution-market-making/figures/limit_order_market_making.tikz}}
\caption{Limit-order depths and probability of execution, after \citep[Figure~2.2, p.~30]{crypto-cartea}.}\label{fig:limit_order_market_making}
\end{figure}

\qffield{Notation} Local override for this entry: $p^\pm$ are probabilities of market-order arrival, not prices, and $P^\pm(\delta)$ is the probability that an arriving market order reaches depth $\delta$, not a bond or put price.

\qffield{Conditions} Everyone agrees on the value $S_t$ (no information asymmetry); other market makers' orders form a fixed book that does not react; the market maker has no inventory or trading costs and liquidates at the midprice \citep[same chap., pp.~28--29]{crypto-cartea}. Idea of the proof: the problem separates by side, and the first-order condition $p^\pm e^{-\kappa^\pm\delta^\pm}(1-\kappa^\pm\delta^\pm)=0$ gives the depth; the arrival probabilities scale profit but not the optimal depth (Interpretation, own derivation).

\qffield{Practice} The model isolates the trade-off between margin per trade and probability of execution \citep[same chap., p.~30]{crypto-cartea}; quoting deeper earns more per fill and fills less often.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The authors list what makes it unrealistic: the arrival and fill probabilities are special, constant and exogenous; the market maker's decision does not affect other traders; and the objective is static \citep[same chap., p.~30]{crypto-cartea}. Interpretation: there is no inventory penalty and no adverse selection, so the depth does not respond to position or to informed flow; \hyperref[mod:avellaneda-stoikov]{Avellaneda--Stoikov} adds inventory.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:avellaneda-stoikov]{Avellaneda--Stoikov quoting} (generalisation: dynamic, with inventory and the same exponential intensity); \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (the immediacy this quoting supplies).

\qffield{Further reading} \citep{ho-stoll-1981}, the dynamic dealer model this simplifies (not yet read for this entry; see the pathway backlog); own full derivation, from the legacy notes: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/derivations.tex}.

\qffield{Index} limit-order placement; quote depth; fill probability; probability of execution; exponential order-book depth; static market making; Ho--Stoll (static simplification)

\end{qfmodel}

\begin{qfmodel}[Avellaneda--Stoikov inventory-aware quotes]\label{mod:avellaneda-stoikov}
\qffield{Statement} A dealer with CARA utility, inventory $q$ and horizon $T$ quotes around a reservation (indifference) price rather than the midprice $s$ \citep[sec.~2.2, eq.~(8), p.~219]{s6-stoikov}:
\[ r(s,q,t)=s-q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t). \]
With symmetric exponential execution intensities $\lambda^a(\delta)=\lambda^b(\delta)=Ae^{-k\delta}$, an expansion of the value function in $q$ with a first-order approximation of the order-arrival terms gives the same reservation price and a bid--ask spread, independent of inventory, of \citep[sec.~3.2, eqs.~(29)--(30), p.~221]{s6-stoikov}
\[ \delta^a+\delta^b=\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)+\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln\Big(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\Big). \]

\qffield{Notation} Local override for this entry: the midprice follows $dS_u=\sigma\,dW_u$, so $\sigma$ is an absolute volatility in price units per square root of the model's time unit, not the annualised volatility of returns; $s$ is the current midprice; $\delta^a=p^a-s$ and $\delta^b=s-p^b$ are the quote distances; $\lambda^{a}$, $\lambda^{b}$ are Poisson execution intensities, not price-impact slopes \citep[secs.~2.1 and 2.4, pp.~218--219]{s6-stoikov}.

\qffield{Conditions} Arithmetic Brownian midprice with no drift and a zero interest rate; exponential utility of terminal wealth plus inventory marked at the midprice; one-share limit orders, updated continuously at no cost, filled at Poisson rates that decrease with distance from the midprice \citep[secs.~2.1--2.4, pp.~218--219]{s6-stoikov}. The exponential intensity follows from a constant market-order frequency, a power-law order-size distribution and a logarithmic temporary impact, with $A=\Lambda/\alpha$ and $k=\alpha K$; a power-law impact gives a power-law intensity instead \citep[sec.~2.5, eq.~(12), p.~220]{s6-stoikov}. Exact quotes solve the HJB equation and two implicit equations for the distances \citep[sec.~3.1, eqs.~(15)--(19), pp.~220--221]{s6-stoikov}; the closed form above is an approximation \citep[sec.~3.2, p.~221]{s6-stoikov}. A long position ($q>0$) puts the reservation price below the midprice, so both quotes shift down to sell \citep[sec.~2.2, p.~219]{s6-stoikov}.

\qffield{Practice} In 1000 simulations with $s=100$, $T=1$, $\sigma=2$, $\gamma=0.1$, $k=1.5$, $A=140$ and $dt=0.005$, the inventory strategy earned a mean profit of 65.0 with standard deviation 6.6, against 68.4 and 12.7 for a strategy quoting the same average spread of 1.49 symmetrically around the midprice; final inventory had standard deviation 2.9 against 8.4 \citep[sec.~3.3, Table~1, p.~222]{s6-stoikov}. The symmetric strategy earns slightly more because it trades more, but its P\&L is much more dispersed \citep[sec.~3.3, p.~222]{s6-stoikov}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Interpretation: the closed form is a control template, not a production model; real books add queue position, tick size, discrete inventory limits, latency, rebates, hedging instruments and fill probabilities that change with regime. The approximate spread does not depend on inventory only because the intensities are exponential \citep[sec.~3.2, p.~221]{s6-stoikov}. Interpretation: the formula has no inventory limit; a hard limit needs the bounded-inventory solution read in this pathway (\texttt{wide-gueant-lehalle-tapia}, not yet read).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:static-limit-order-depth]{Static limit-order depth} (special case: no inventory risk, static); \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (the same inventory-risk premium in a three-date equilibrium).

\qffield{Further reading} Own working from the legacy notes: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/derivations.tex}.

\qffield{Index} Avellaneda--Stoikov; reservation price; indifference price; inventory skew; optimal bid--ask spread; market making with limit orders; exponential fill intensity; HJB market making

\section{Informed trading and informative prices}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Fundamental value, market value and noise: what informative prices are informative about]\label{def:fundamental-value}
\qffield{Statement} The \emph{market value} of an instrument is the price at which traders can buy or sell it. Its \emph{fundamental value} (intrinsic value) is the expected present value of all present and future benefits and costs of holding it: the value everyone would agree on if all knew everything known about the instrument, analysed it properly and perceived its benefits and costs alike \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', sec.~10.1, pp.~222--223]{alpha-harris}. The difference between the two is \emph{noise}; prices are informative when they are near fundamental values and completely informative when they equal them \citep[same sec., p.~223]{alpha-harris}. Writing $V_t$ for fundamental value and $P_t$ for the price, noise is $\epsilon_t=P_t-V_t$. Fundamental values use only information available now, so they are the best estimates of perfect-foresight values, not those values; and their changes are unpredictable, since a predictable change would mean they did not yet reflect the information behind the prediction \citep[same sec., p.~223]{alpha-harris}. Relative to a price, information is of three kinds:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{public}: available to any trader, such as public news and past prices and volumes \citep[same chap., pp.~223 and~240]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{private}: held only by some traders, whether learnt first or produced by their own research \citep[same chap., pp.~223 and~228]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{stale}: already in the price, meaning it cannot be used to forecast future price changes; traders who act on it are \emph{pseudo-informed}, and in fact uninformed \citep[same chap., sec.~10.5.2.1, p.~229]{alpha-harris}.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} $V_t$ fundamental value, $P_t$ market price, $\epsilon_t=P_t-V_t$ noise; the sign is a convention, since Harris defines noise only as the difference.

\qffield{Conditions} Fundamental values are not observed, so whether a price is informative or noisy cannot easily be determined \citep[same chap., p.~223]{alpha-harris}. Prices become informative in two ways: when values are common knowledge no one trades at other prices; otherwise informed traders' trading moves prices towards their estimates, which is the more important route because values are rarely common knowledge \citep[same chap., sec.~10.6.1, pp.~236--237]{alpha-harris}. Information gets into the price when all traders understand its significance or when informed traders push the price towards their estimates \citep[same chap., p.~229]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Whether information is stale can rarely be checked directly. Harris's guide: information that is unique and that others could not have anticipated is probably not stale; information acted on slowly, widely known, cheap to obtain, or reasonably anticipated probably is. His example: Occidental Petroleum rose 10 percent the day after its long-ill chairman died and fell back the next day, because his death was expected and its date was not material \citep[same chap., p.~229 and box, p.~230]{alpha-harris}. Fischer Black would call stock prices informative if they lay between half and twice their fundamental values \citep[same chap., box, p.~223]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Two conditioning sets are in use. Harris's fundamental value conditions on everything known to anyone, private information included. Hasbrouck calls the expectation of the payoff conditional on \emph{all public information} the fundamental value or efficient price \citep[sec.~3.2, p.~26]{s6-hasbrouck}: the $m_t$ of the \hyperref[def:volatility-components]{random-walk decomposition}. Harris's noise is therefore not Hasbrouck's pricing error $s_t=p_t-m_t$. A price equal to the public-information expectation has a zero pricing error but nonzero noise whenever private information has not yet reached it (Interpretation). The legacy notes defined fundamental value as ``implied by all payoff-relevant information'', which reads as the perfect-foresight value that Harris distinguishes from it. They also described private information as ``costly or restricted''; private information can be produced lawfully from public data by research \citep[chap.~``Insider Trading'', p.~585]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:volatility-components]{fundamental and transitory volatility} (the statistical version, conditioned on public information); \hyperref[res:informed-trading-aggregation]{informed trading and aggregation} (how prices become informative); \hyperref[def:market-efficiency]{market efficiency}; \hyperref[def:inside-information]{inside information}; \hyperref[def:outside-spread]{outside spread} (value traders' estimates in use).

\qffield{Further reading} The legacy blocks it replaces, verbatim: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/derivations.tex}.

\begin{qfresult}[Informed trading makes prices informative: the price as an aggregate of value estimates]\label{res:informed-trading-aggregation}
\qffield{Statement} Informed traders buy when the price is below their estimate of fundamental value and sell when it is above. Their buying pushes prices up and their selling pushes them down, so their trading moves prices towards their estimates \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', secs.~10.2--10.3, pp.~223--224]{alpha-harris}. Harris's algebraic illustration \citep[same chap., box, p.~225]{alpha-harris}: $N$ traders hold unbiased forecasts $f_i=V+e_i$ of the value $V$, each wants the position $D_i=a(f_i-P)$, and the security is in zero net supply. Market clearing, $\sum_ia(f_i-P)=0$, gives
\[ P=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Nf_i=V+e_M, \qquad e_M=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Ne_i. \]
With independent errors, $e_M\to0$ as $N$ grows, by the law of large numbers: prices are most informative when many informed traders collect information independently. Interpretation: if the errors are also independent of $V$ with common variance $\sigma^2$, then $\mathbb{E}[P\mid V]=V$ and $\operatorname{Var}(P-V)=\sigma^2/N$.

\qffield{Conditions} When forecasts differ in accuracy, the best combination is a weighted average that gives more weight to the more accurate. Well-informed traders tend to be wealthier and take larger positions, so prices approximate such an optimally weighted average: the market is a statistical calculator \citep[same chap., p.~224 and box, p.~226]{alpha-harris}. Interpretation: with trader-specific slopes $a_i$ the same clearing condition gives $P=\sum_ia_if_i/\sum_ia_i$, still unbiased when each $f_i$ is; with independent errors of variances $\sigma_i^2$, $\operatorname{Var}(P-V)=\sum_ia_i^2\sigma_i^2/(\sum_ia_i)^2$, minimised by $a_i\propto1/\sigma_i^2$ at $\bigl(\sum_i\sigma_i^{-2}\bigr)^{-1}$, below every $\sigma_i^2$. Many mistaken traders, or one large one, make prices less informative, until their losses drive them out \citep[same chap., p.~224]{alpha-harris}. Informed traders do not trade to make prices informative: price impact is their cost, and they want prices to adjust only after they have built their positions \citep[same chap., p.~224]{alpha-harris}. They profit only from uninformed traders; competition among them pushes prices closer to values and lowers their profits until the least successful just cover their costs \citep[same chap., secs.~10.6.3 and~10.6.5, pp.~237--239]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Trade aggressively when the information will soon be common knowledge or many others will act on it, since the first to trade profit most and a flood of orders makes liquidity suppliers wary; trade slowly (stealth trading) when the advantage will last, so that others cannot infer that the trader is well informed \citep[same chap., sec.~10.4, pp.~225--226]{alpha-harris}. The legacy notes added a third speed, \emph{inactive}, for information already in the price; that is stale information, on which no trading pays \citep[same chap., p.~229]{alpha-harris}, rather than a trading speed. The most profitable traders have estimates that are both precise and \emph{orthogonal}, uncorrelated with other traders'; they face little competition for liquidity when they trade. Precision and orthogonality trade off: a precise but crowded estimate and an imprecise but orthogonal one can be equally profitable \citep[same chap., sec.~10.6.2.1, p.~237]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Aggregation removes independent errors, not shared ones. Interpretation: if the errors have common variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$ (which requires $\rho\ge-1/(N-1)$), then $\operatorname{Var}(e_M)=\tfrac1{N^2}[N\sigma^2+N(N-1)\rho\sigma^2]=\sigma^2[\rho+(1-\rho)/N]\to\rho\sigma^2$ for $\rho\ge0$, so a shared data source, model or stale signal leaves an error that no number of traders removes; Harris notes that traders who use the same information and methods have highly correlated estimates \citep[same chap., p.~237]{alpha-harris}. Harris also says the market's forecast error is smaller than the average individual error whenever the individual errors are not identical, and that the price estimates value better than any individual trader can \citep[same chap., box, p.~225, page image]{alpha-harris}. Interpretation, by error measure: the signed errors average exactly, $e_M=\tfrac1N\sum_ie_i$, so ``smaller'' needs a loss. For one realisation, squared error gives $e_M^2\le\tfrac1N\sum_ie_i^2$, since the gap is $\tfrac1N\sum_i(e_i-e_M)^2$, with equality only when all $e_i$ are equal, as Harris's condition says. Absolute error gives $|e_M|\le\tfrac1N\sum_i|e_i|$ by the triangle inequality, with equality whenever the errors share a sign, so $e=(1,2)$ is not identical yet gives $1.5$ on both sides. In expectation, with independent, zero-mean, nondegenerate errors, both inequalities are strict, since errors of opposite sign then occur with positive probability. ``Better than any individual'' holds in expected squared error only when accuracies are equal: with variances $0.01$ and $1$ the equal-weighted average has variance $0.2525$, worse than the better trader's, while inverse-variance weights give $0.0099$. The legacy proposition assumed the price is the average forecast; in the box this follows from linear demands with a common slope and zero net supply, an assumption Harris says simplifies the arithmetic without changing the qualitative results. The legacy mechanism had informed traders buy when $P_t<V_t$: they act on estimates, and lose when their estimates are poor \citep[same chap., p.~223]{alpha-harris}.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Informed-trader types: value traders, news traders, information-oriented technical traders and arbitrageurs]\label{def:informed-trader-types}
\qffield{Statement} Harris classifies informed traders by what they estimate \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', sec.~10.5, pp.~226--235, Table~10-3, p.~235]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{value traders} estimate total fundamental value from all available information; slow, because careful review guards against biased estimates;
\item \emph{news traders} estimate changes in value from new information, assuming the price already reflects everything else; fast on public news, slower on information they produce by their own research;
\item \emph{information-oriented technical traders} identify price patterns left by informed traders' systematic mistakes, such as under- or overreaction; fast;
\item \emph{arbitrageurs} estimate relative values of instruments that share valuation factors; fast.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Value and news traders profit in different circumstances. Price departs from value either because value changes and price lags, where news traders, first to learn the news, profit; or because uninformed traders push price away from value, where value traders profit \citep[chap.~``Value Traders'', sec.~16.1, pp.~338--339]{alpha-harris}. Their mistakes put them on opposite sides. News traders who misjudge the size of a change leave underreaction for value traders to trade on, or overreaction for them to trade against (Table~10-1, p.~230); pseudo-informed traders acting on stale news lose to value traders (box, p.~229). Value traders who mistake a price move driven by news for uninformed pressure lose to news traders \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', p.~239]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Organisation follows speed: large value traders have pyramid-shaped organisations with layers of review, news traders flat organisations that decide quickly \citep[same chap., pp.~227--229]{alpha-harris}. Technical traders who trade on the predictable impact of \emph{uninformed} traders are not informed traders: they act as dealers if they supply liquidity, or as order anticipators if they front-run \citep[same chap., p.~231]{alpha-harris}. Information-oriented technical strategies decay as informed traders learn from their mistakes \citep[same chap., p.~231]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Liquidity roles} (slice~35). Harris's Table~8-4 lists value traders, news traders, information-oriented technical traders and arbitrageurs as the informed traders \citep[chap.~``Why People Trade'', sec.~8.2.1, pp.~194--196]{alpha-harris}. As liquidity suppliers, value traders trade when uninformed demand has moved prices away from value and are ``the ultimate suppliers of liquidity'', supplying depth, while news traders trade on changes in value before prices adjust \citep[chap.~``Liquidity'', sec.~19.3.4, pp.~402--403]{alpha-harris}; arbitrageurs move liquidity between markets (\hyperref[def:arbitrage]{arbitrage}). The legacy distinction, whether an order corrects stale prices, absorbs non-value flow or links related prices, maps onto news traders, value traders and arbitrageurs.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy taxonomy described technical traders as exploiting ``predictable errors by other traders''. In Harris that holds for errors by informed traders; errors by uninformed traders belong to sentiment-oriented technical traders, who are order anticipators (\hyperref[def:order-anticipation]{order anticipation}). The legacy principle \emph{value traders versus news traders} added dealer inventory pressure as a separate source of value-trader profit. In Harris it is a channel of uninformed demand (see \hyperref[def:outside-spread]{outside spread}).

\begin{qfdefinition}[Winner's curse: what winning a common-value trade reveals, and how bids adjust]\label{def:winners-curse}
\qffield{Statement} Buyers who compete for an item of common but unknown value suffer the \emph{winner's curse} when they pay more than it is worth. The highest bidder tends to be the one who most overestimated the value, so bids near one's own estimate lose on average when they win. Buyers avoid it by lowering their bids to reflect what winning would tell them about their estimate; sellers whose offers are taken symmetrically raise their offers \citep[chap.~``Value Traders'', sec.~16.2.1.2, pp.~341--342]{alpha-harris}. Interpretation: let $\widehat V$ be the buyer's estimate, $c$ an all-in cost, and $\omega(b)=\mathbb{E}[\widehat V-V\mid\widehat V,\ \text{win at bid }b]$ the expected overestimate given one's own estimate and winning at bid $b$. The expected profit of winning is $\widehat V-\omega(b)-b-c$, so the highest bid that breaks even solves $b=\widehat V-\omega(b)-c$.

\qffield{Conditions} The required adjustment is larger \citep[same sec., p.~342]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the more bidders there are, because outbidding many implies a larger overestimate;
\item the harder the value is to estimate, because extreme estimates are then further from the truth.
\end{itemize}
Against market power: with only one or very few competitors a bidder may underbid in the hope of winning cheaply, so bids are more aggressive against several competitors than against one or two; this matters only with very few \citep[same sec., p.~342]{alpha-harris}. In a two-sided single-price auction the curse matters little, because traders do not trade at their own bids and offers and the clearing price is near the median estimate. In continuous markets they do, and aggregation is sequential, so traders who misestimate lose when they push prices \citep[same sec., pp.~342--343]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Oil companies overpaid for drilling leases until they allowed for the curse; their geologists were right on average over all tracts but optimistic about the tracts they won \citep[same chap., box, p.~342]{alpha-harris}. Value traders face the curse whenever they trade on a large apparent mispricing and widen their outside spreads in response; Harris attributes part of late-1990s Internet-stock volatility to prices bouncing within wide outside spreads \citep[same chap., p.~343 and box]{alpha-harris}. Do not compete against foolish bidders: one cannot profit bidding against strategies that ensure losses \citep[same chap., p.~342]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy proposition wrote the zero-profit bid as $b^\star=\widehat V-\omega_N-c$ with $\omega_N$ conditioned only on winning among $N$ buyers. Conditioning must also be on one's own estimate, and the event of winning depends on the bid itself, and in equilibrium on rivals' strategies. The expression is therefore a fixed-point condition, not a closed form. It also gives the highest acceptable bid, not the profit-maximising one, which shades further when competitors are few (Interpretation). The legacy severity list added that common data reduce dispersion while independent noisy estimates increase selection risk; that is not in the passages checked (Interpretation: shared information makes estimates move together, which reduces how much winning reveals).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:outside-spread]{outside spread} (the curse widens value traders' spreads); \hyperref[res:informed-trading-aggregation]{informed trading and aggregation} (correlated and independent estimates); \hyperref[mod:glosten-milgrom]{Glosten--Milgrom model} (adverse selection, the analogous inference a dealer makes from the side of a trade).

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Market efficiency: weak, semistrong and strong forms, and the definition with information costs]\label{def:market-efficiency}
\qffield{Statement} In an \emph{efficient market}, prices reflect all information that traders can acquire and profitably trade upon. The definition builds in the costs of acquiring information and of acting on it, and the impact of informed trading on prices \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', sec.~10.6.6, p.~240]{alpha-harris}. Harris sets it beside the three traditional forms \citep[same sec., p.~240]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{weak form}: prices reflect all information in past prices, so no one can predict price changes from past prices alone;
\item \emph{semistrong form}: prices reflect all public information, which includes all public news and past prices and volumes in all securities and contracts;
\item \emph{strong form}: prices reflect all public and private information as soon as it is known; since informed traders could never profit, only markets in instruments whose values are common knowledge are strong-form efficient.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Prices never fully reflect all the information traders could collect: information that is too expensive or too inconsequential is not acquired \citep[same sec., p.~240]{alpha-harris}. Harris's market paradox: if prices were fully informative, informed trading would not pay, so no one would make them informative. Prices and values change constantly, so gaps open and informed trading closes them: prices are informative but not always \citep[same chap., sec.~10.6.4, pp.~238--239]{alpha-harris}. Lehalle and Laruelle make the same point with Grossman and Stiglitz (1980), not consulted: market dynamics must contain enough inefficiency to reward those who improve the information in prices \citep[Introduction, p.~4]{alpha-laruelle}. How informative prices are depends on the costs of information and of trading, and on there being uninformed traders to lose to informed ones \citep[same chap., secs.~10.6.3 and~10.7, pp.~238 and~243--244]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Informative prices are not free. What uninformed traders lose to informed traders pays for much of the information in prices, so policies that frustrate informed traders can make prices less informative and markets more liquid; restrictions on insider trading are an example \citep[same chap., sec.~10.6.7, p.~241]{alpha-harris}. Harris concludes that these restrictions narrow spreads and make prices less informative in the short run \citep[chap.~``Insider Trading'', sec.~29.4, pp.~597--598]{alpha-harris}. The trade-off is not universal: publishing material fundamental information raises both liquidity and informativeness \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', sec.~10.6.8, pp.~241--242]{alpha-harris}. Trading halts before material releases protect uninformed traders, especially those who offer liquidity, at the cost of a few minutes' delay in price formation, a trade Harris judges worth making \citep[same chap., box, p.~241]{alpha-harris}. Most published studies find markets weak-form efficient, and semistrong-form efficient for public information that is easy to obtain and interpret \citep[same chap., p.~240]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Liquidity and informative prices} (slice~35). Liquid markets make informed trading cheaper, so informed traders can afford more research and trade on smaller pieces of information, and ``Prices therefore should be more informative in liquid markets than in illiquid markets''; informed traders profit only if uninformed traders are willing to lose to them \citep[chap.~``Good Markets'', sec.~9.5.4, pp.~215--216]{alpha-harris}. The legacy principle that visibility, update speed and uninformed flow decide how much information enters prices is a summary of this mechanism and of \hyperref[res:informed-trading-aggregation]{informed trading and aggregation}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Comparing market structures needs the change in benefits, not their totals, and the benefits of informative prices and of liquidity are both hard to measure \citep[same chap., sec.~10.6.7, p.~241]{alpha-harris}. The legacy notes put volumes in the weak-form information set; Harris's weak form uses past prices, and his semistrong set adds volumes. They also called the cost-sensitive definition \emph{economic} efficiency, a name Harris does not use. Their trade-off said informed trading transfers wealth from ``slower'' liquidity traders and that efficiency-raising policies reduce ``displayed'' liquidity. Harris speaks of uninformed traders and liquidity suppliers, and of liquidity in general. A second legacy statement of the paradox said markets persist between its limits because information is costly, temporary and unevenly distributed. Harris's resolution rests on prices and values changing constantly, with information costs limiting what prices reflect (pp.~238--240); ``unevenly distributed'' is implicit in his informed and uninformed traders rather than stated as part of the resolution.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Inside information: material nonpublic information and who may not trade on it (United States, as summarised in 2002)]\label{def:inside-information}
\qffield{Statement} For economic analysis, Harris defines \emph{inside information} as material information about the value of a security that is not available to public traders, and \emph{material} information as information that would cause prices to change if it were widely known; \emph{insider trading} is trading on it \citep[chap.~``Insider Trading'', sec.~29.1, p.~584]{alpha-harris}. Inside information keeps its status however many people pass it on, and loses it only when it is publicly available, through a broadly distributed press release or a public filing \citep[same sec., p.~585]{alpha-harris}. The legal definitions come from statutes, regulations and case law, are complex, and call for an attorney \citep[same sec., p.~584]{alpha-harris}. The SEC's summary, as quoted by Harris from its web page of January 2002, covers trading in breach of a fiduciary duty or other relationship of trust and confidence while in possession of material nonpublic information, and tipping, trading by those tipped, and misappropriation \citep[same sec., box, p.~585]{alpha-harris}. Those who may not trade on such information include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{corporate insiders}: senior managers, directors, large shareholders, key employees, and anyone involved in corporate activities that will materially affect the firm's value \citep[sec.~29.1.1, p.~587]{alpha-harris};
\item employees of law, banking, brokerage and printing firms given the information to serve the corporation, and government employees who learn it at work \citep[sec.~29.1, box, p.~585]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{tippees}: friends, family and business associates of insiders, and the friends of their friends \citep[same sec., pp.~584--585]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{misappropriators}, who take confidential information from an employer or other source. Chiarella's conviction was reversed in 1980 because he owed no fiduciary duty to the firms whose stock he traded; Harris says that under the later law he would be convicted for misappropriation \citep[same sec., box, p.~586]{alpha-harris}. Winans was convicted under the misappropriation doctrine \citep[same sec., box, p.~587]{alpha-harris}.
\end{itemize}
Analysts whose private information comes only from analysing public information are not trading on inside information \citep[same sec., p.~585]{alpha-harris}. Harris's box ``Inside information, skilled speculation, or good advice?'' shows why the line is hard to draw from the trade alone: the same well-timed purchase before a takeover may rest on a tip, on independent analysis of public information, or on analysis that the trader then sold to the target's management; and a trader who had a tip will claim the second \citep[sec.~29.2, box, p.~590]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} Jurisdiction and date: United States law as Harris summarises it in 2002. Most countries prohibit insider trading, but only a few, mainly the United States, Canada and Great Britain, regularly and seriously enforce their laws \citep[chap.~``Insider Trading'', p.~584]{alpha-harris}. Regulation FD (2000) requires an issuer that discloses material nonpublic information to anyone to disclose it publicly \citep[same chap., box, p.~586]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Corporations restrict their employees' trading: many require corporate approval before a trade, may refuse it before a release, or allow trading only in a window after quarterly earnings \citep[same chap., sec.~29.1.1, p.~588]{alpha-harris}. Exchange surveillance looks for large price changes on large volume before significant news \citep[same chap., sec.~29.2, p.~588]{alpha-harris}. Insider trading is hard to prove: a sophisticated speculator can claim independent analysis of public data, and trading on information that is not revealed in a discrete event is essentially undetectable from prices and volumes \citep[same chap., pp.~589--590]{alpha-harris}. Like all informed trading, insider trading hurts liquidity suppliers and raises spreads for uninformed traders \citep[same chap., sec.~29.3.1.2, pp.~591--592]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Controls on insiders' own trading} (slice~35). Harris lists, for the United States in 2002, reporting of insider trades to the SEC, prearranged trading programmes that let insiders trade while knowing material information, return of short-swing profits on positions opened and closed within six months, restrictions on short positions in the employer's stock, corporate pre-approval of trades, and trading windows after quarterly earnings (sec.~29.1.1, pp.~587--588). Current U.S.\ rules, as of the eCFR text for 14 October 2026: Rule 10b5-1 gives an affirmative defence for trades under a plan adopted before the trader was aware of the information; since its 2022 amendments (effective 27 February 2023) directors and officers must wait out a cooling-off period of the later of 90 days or two business days after the issuer's results for the quarter in which the plan was adopted are disclosed, up to 120 days, and other persons except the issuer 30 days \citep[paras.~(b) and (c)(1)(ii)(B)]{cfr-240-10b5-1} \citep[summary and dates, p.~80362]{sec-34-96492}; the same release added disclosure of plan adoptions and terminations and a Form~4 flag for plan trades (summary, p.~80362). Form~4 must be filed ``before the end of the second business day following the day on which the subject transaction has been executed'' \citep[para.~(g)(1)]{cfr-240-16a-3}. Regulation~FD requires public disclosure ``simultaneously'' for intentional and ``promptly'' for non-intentional selective disclosures \citep[para.~(a)]{cfr-243-100}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Harris's categories are legal categories summarised for economists, not a legal test; check the law in force. The reporting deadlines, prearranged-plan rules and penalties he gives \citep[same chap., pp.~587--591]{alpha-harris} are as of 2002; the reporting deadline and the plan rules were checked against current law in slice~35 (above), the penalties were not. The legacy notes used ``temporary insider'' and a ``relationship, benefit or confidence'' test for tipping. Neither term appears in the passages checked. They also named auditors, consultants and exchange staff, whom the SEC summary does not list. A legacy principle, \emph{signal tradability requires permission}, said that data provenance, confidentiality duties, firm restricted lists, compliance approvals and execution capacity decide whether a predictive signal may be used. The passages checked support confidentiality duties (misappropriation), employer approval of employees' own trades (p.~588), and the dependence of informed trading profits on liquidity \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', box, p.~236]{alpha-harris}. Firm restricted lists, compliance approval of signals and the provenance of purchased data are not in them. A legacy caveat, \emph{skilled speculation versus inside information}, named alternative data ``with clean rights'' and channel checks as legitimate sources; the passages checked do not discuss either, and their legal status was not checked. \begin{qfresult}[Insider-trading profitability, detection and deterrence, and the policy debate (United States, as described in 2002)]\label{res:insider-trading-deterrence}
\qffield{Statement} Harris's account of when trading on inside information pays and when it is caught \citep[chap.~``Insider Trading'', secs.~29.2--29.3, pp.~588--595]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{competition among insiders}: one insider with information that will not soon be released can trade slowly and extract the most; many insiders who learn it at once must race, which quickly reveals the information and lowers their total profit \citep[sec.~29.3.1.2, p.~592]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{discreteness of release}: trading on information revealed in a discrete event can be traced back from it, whereas trading on information that firms do not reveal for months or years is essentially undetectable from prices and volumes \citep[sec.~29.2, p.~590]{alpha-harris}; restrictions are therefore effective mainly against information that will soon be public \citep[sec.~29.3.2.1, p.~595]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{deterrence}: a rational criminal trades when the expected benefit exceeds the expected cost, the probability of being caught times the penalty, so a low probability of detection requires a large penalty \citep[sec.~29.2, p.~590]{alpha-harris}.
\end{itemize}
Interpretation: with expected gross gain $G$, execution and leakage costs $K$, a risk charge $R$, detection probability $q$ and penalty $F$, the trade is deterred when $G\le K+R+qF$. If the penalty were only disgorgement of the gain, $F=G$, the condition becomes $(1-q)G\le K+R$, which a small $q$ rarely satisfies; hence fines on top of disgorgement.

\qffield{Conditions} United States as Harris describes it in 2002. Penalties then included disgorgement, fines and, rarely, prison; they were heavier for traders closer to the source. He gives maximum criminal penalties of ten years and a million dollars, a maximum civil penalty of three times the profit gained or loss avoided, and an SEC bounty of up to 10 percent of civil penalties collected \citep[sec.~29.2, pp.~590--591 and box]{alpha-harris}. These figures are dated and were not checked against current law. $R$ is a cost only for a risk-averse trader; a risk-neutral trader would not subtract it (Interpretation).

\qffield{Practice} Exchange surveillance identifies most cases. It looks for large price changes on large volume and asks the listed firm whether news explains them; trading before releases of significant unexpected news often indicates insiders or confederates \citep[sec.~29.2, p.~588]{alpha-harris}. Investigators then match a list of those who knew with a list of those who profited, in stocks, options or bonds, through links such as family, neighbours or classmates \citep[same sec., pp.~588--589 and box]{alpha-harris}. In the Colt Industries case, deep out-of-the-money options bought by friends and relatives of a law-firm employee were traced through the buyers' home addresses \citep[same sec., box, p.~589]{alpha-harris}. \emph{The policy debate} (slice~35). Harris's arguments for restricting insider trading: fairness and investor confidence (rebutted by the claim that firms can restrict it by contract, and answered by the difficulty of private enforcement), liquidity (insiders, like all informed traders, widen spreads and hurt limit-order traders), and corporate control (insiders who may trade share less information, may choose projects for their trading advantage, and may front-run their firms' own trades); against: informative prices (answered by the fact that enforcement works mainly against information soon to be public, so the efficiency gain is small), costs of enforcement and selective prosecution, and Manne's entrepreneurial incentives (answered by the need for positions to be taken before outcomes are known, and the general ban on insiders' short positions) \citep[sec.~29.3, pp.~591--597]{alpha-harris}. His summary: the laws ``narrow bid/ask spreads and make prices less informative in the short run'' (pp.~597--598). The legacy proposition that a restriction improves welfare iff its liquidity and control gains exceed enforcement and delay costs, $L+C>E+D$, is an accounting identity that lists these terms; its content lies in their sizes, which Harris does not quantify. The legacy enforcement process (detect abnormal trading before an event, list who knew, match traders to access, test alternative explanations, then sanction) restates the surveillance and investigation steps above.

\qffield{Pitfalls} These footprints are leads, not proof. Relationships between the two lists do not prove insider trading, and a sophisticated speculator will claim independent analysis of public data \citep[same sec., pp.~589--590]{alpha-harris}. The legacy footprint list also named widened spreads, quote fading and dark-pool activity before news. These are not among the signals in the passages checked, although wider spreads in the presence of insiders follow from adverse selection \citep[sec.~29.3.1.2, p.~591]{alpha-harris}. The legacy profitability list named market liquidity as a factor; Harris makes that point for informed trading generally (chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', box, p.~236). Its ``traceability'' factor is a paraphrase of Harris's investigative method, not his term.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Price formation process: immediate impact and its decay]\label{def:price-formation-process}
\qffield{Statement} The \emph{price formation process} (PFP) is how trades and information become prices. Lehalle and Laruelle report a consensus that it consists of the impact of each trade combined with the variation of the asset's ``fair price'', after Bouchaud et al.\ (2002), not consulted \citep[Introduction, p.~27]{alpha-laruelle}. They split it into two phases \citep[same, Figure~8, pp.~27--29]{alpha-laruelle}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{immediate impact}: trades move the price through the balance of buyers and sellers, and of liquidity-providing and liquidity-consuming orders, together with moves of the spread;
\item \emph{decay}, or the \emph{resilience} of the book: liquidity returns, or does not, according to the distance between the best quotes and investors' views of the fair price.
\end{enumerate}
Pressure inconsistent with participants' consensus generates only temporary impact; pressure consistent with it is not pushed back, and the impact is permanent \citep[same, pp.~3 and~28]{alpha-laruelle}. The PFP is mainly driven by information \citep[same, p.~7]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Conditions} The more investors with views of the fair price take part, the faster impact decays. Market makers with inventory constraints contribute to the decay without any view of the fair price, but only if their inventory exceeds the turnover of a price jump \citep[Introduction, p.~28]{alpha-laruelle}. Post-trade transparency speeds the decay by warning traders that the last price is out of line with their views \citep[same, p.~28]{alpha-laruelle}. A design tension runs through it: the more information traders have, the better the PFP should be, but each trader fears information leakage, front-running and excessive impact \citep[same, pp.~19--20]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Practice} Market makers buy from early sellers and sell to later buyers, so that most temporary oscillations shrink to a spread related to the asset's risk \citep[Introduction, pp.~3--4]{alpha-laruelle}. Volatility is the random, unexpected component of the PFP, and the more efficient the microstructure, the lower intraday volatility \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.5.1, pp.~171--172]{alpha-laruelle}. In a fragmented market, a consolidated tape organises how information is shared \citep[Introduction, p.~7]{alpha-laruelle}; the legacy fragmentation and outage blocks are assigned to \texttt{market-structure-execution-quality}. The authors see high-frequency traders' growing role as a possible threat to the decay, since they need not hold a view of the fair price and have small inventory limits \citep[same, p.~28]{alpha-laruelle}; that question belongs to \texttt{hft-market-design}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The impact of a single large order depends on what others are doing. If many others buy at the same time, the impact of a large buy is permanent; if most sell, it can be almost invisible. Impact is measured only by averaging over many market configurations \citep[Introduction, p.~3]{alpha-laruelle}. ``Clean'' and ``noisy'' PFP, the legacy terms, are not defined by the authors, although they speak of a ``less noisy PFP'' \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.5.1, p.~177]{alpha-laruelle}. Interpretation: the noise is the transitory component of \hyperref[def:volatility-components]{price changes}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:informed-trading-aggregation]{informed trading and aggregation} (the information side); \hyperref[res:spread-component-identification]{component identification} (permanent and transitory price effects of trades); \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle single-auction model} (permanent impact from information); \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (temporary concession for immediacy); \hyperref[def:intraday-overnight-returns]{returns by time scale}; \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact} (reaction and prediction impact, and the two readings of impact).

\begin{qfdefinition}[Arbitrage: hedge portfolio, basis, fair value, arbitrage spread and bounds; pure and speculative arbitrage]\label{def:arbitrage}
\qffield{Statement} Arbitrageurs trade instruments with correlated prices. An \emph{arbitrage opportunity} arises when those prices diverge from their normal relation: the arbitrageur buys the relatively cheap instruments, sells the relatively expensive ones, and profits if the prices converge \citep[chap.~``Arbitrageurs'', sec.~17.1, p.~348]{alpha-harris}. The positions form a \emph{hedge portfolio}, whose legs are scaled by hedge ratios against a reference instrument, the \emph{arbitrage numerator}, chosen to minimise the portfolio's risk. Its \emph{carrying costs} include financing, dividends paid on short positions, storage and spoilage, offset by dividends, lending fees and interest received \citep[same sec., pp.~348--349]{alpha-harris}. With $B_t$ the \emph{basis}, the difference in prices between the instruments in the hedge portfolio, and $B_t^\star$ its \emph{fair value}, the basis if all were correctly priced relative to each other, which depends on carrying costs and must be estimated, the \emph{arbitrage spread} is
\[ A_t=B_t-B_t^\star \]
\citep[same sec., p.~349]{alpha-harris}. Arbitrageurs trade only when the basis lies outside the \emph{arbitrage bounds}, the values on either side of fair value at which they are just willing to trade \citep[same sec., p.~349]{alpha-harris}. Interpretation: if one unit of basis converges fully to fair value, the trade earns $|A_t|$ gross; with all-in round-trip cost $K_t$ and a charge $R_t$ for residual basis risk it is attractive only when $|A_t|>K_t+R_t$, so the bounds are $B_t^\star\pm(K_t+R_t)$.

\qffield{Conditions} Harris distinguishes two types by the behaviour of the hedge portfolio's value \citep[same chap., sec.~17.3, pp.~350--363]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{pure arbitrage}: the value is strictly mean reverting, because some mechanism forces convergence. The mechanisms are \emph{shipping} (the same instrument in two places, bounded by shipping and financing costs), \emph{delivery} (contract and cash prices converging at maturity, the basis set by carrying costs before it) and \emph{conversion} (the same risk in different forms, bounded by the costs of converting and carrying). Cyclic arbitrages such as currency cross-rates are a kind of conversion arbitrage.
\item \emph{speculative arbitrage}, also called risk arbitrage: the value is nonstationary, because instrument-specific factors make it wander, and arbitrageurs bet that short-run mean reversion dominates. Kinds: spreads (maturity, calendar, yield-curve and credit spreads), pairs trading, statistical arbitrage with factor models, and merger arbitrage, the usual sense of ``risk arbitrage''.
\end{itemize}
Arbitrage is understood two ways \citep[same chap., secs.~17.6 and~17.9, pp.~375--378]{alpha-harris}. In the \emph{price characterisation}, arbitrageurs are informed traders in relative values who enforce the law of one price \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', sec.~10.5.4, pp.~232--235]{alpha-harris}. In the \emph{quantity characterisation}, they discipline slow prices, ship common-factor risk between markets and repackage it, manufacturing derivative positions; the arbitrage spread is the payment for these services.

\qffield{Practice} Arbitrageurs are dealers or value traders in the hedge portfolio: fast dealer-type arbitrageurs trade short-term reversals, and value-type arbitrageurs take large positions when sure of fair value \citep[chap.~``Arbitrageurs'', sec.~17.2, pp.~349--350]{alpha-harris}. Dealers span time, matching buyers and sellers who arrive at different times in one market; arbitrageurs span place, matching those who arrive together in different markets \citep[same chap., sec.~17.7, p.~376]{alpha-harris}. Free entry narrows arbitrage spreads until they just cover the costs of the most efficient arbitrageurs. These are often traders with a natural interest in one leg, such as grain operators near a delivery point \citep[same chap., sec.~17.8, pp.~377--378]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Economic function} (slice~35). Arbitrageurs ``buy in cheaper markets and sell in more expensive markets'', connecting sellers in one market to buyers in another \citep[chap.~1, p.~6]{alpha-harris}; as liquidity suppliers they are ``not suppliers of liquidity but porters of liquidity'', demanding it where it is available and supplying it where it is demanded, and they compete with dealers, who connect buyers and sellers arriving at different times in one market \citep[chap.~``Liquidity'', sec.~19.3.6, pp.~404--405]{alpha-harris}. The legacy condition that they enforce the law of one price only when costs, funding, hedge error and execution risk are small enough restates \hyperref[res:arbitrage-risks]{arbitrage risks}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Full convergence is the assumption behind the $|A_t|$ in the condition above; with partial or slow convergence the gross gain is the expected narrowing net of extra carry (Interpretation; see \hyperref[res:arbitrage-risks]{arbitrage risks}). The legacy notes listed four parallel types: pure, speculative, risk and statistical arbitrage. In Harris risk arbitrage is another name for speculative arbitrage, and statistical arbitrage one of its kinds. They also made cross-rate arbitrage a fourth pure mechanism beside shipping, delivery and conversion; Harris treats it as a cyclic conversion arbitrage. Even pure arbitrage can lose if convergence is slow or financing fails \citep[same chap., secs.~17.3.1 and~17.9, pp.~350 and~378]{alpha-harris}.

\begin{qfresult}[Arbitrage risks: implementation, basis, model and carrying-cost risk, and staying power]\label{res:arbitrage-risks}
\qffield{Statement} Harris names four arbitrage risks \citep[chap.~``Arbitrageurs'', sec.~17.4, pp.~363--373]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{implementation risk}: transaction costs larger than expected, from adverse execution prices with market orders or failed executions with limit orders. It is worst when one leg has filled and another has not, when markets move fast, and when the arbitrage spread is small relative to short-term volatility;
\item \emph{basis risk}: the basis moves against the position, because specific factors diverge or common factors are mispriced. Total basis risk scales with the size of the position, and adverse moves force liquidation, at the worst time in a pure arbitrage, when the trader cannot finance the losses;
\item \emph{model risk}: misunderstanding the value relation, so that the trader takes the wrong side, carries unrecognised risks or uses wrong hedge ratios. The commonest error is treating a move of the basis towards a changed fair value as an opportunity;
\item \emph{carrying-cost risk}: higher financing, borrowing, storage or dividend costs than expected; unexpected price rises whose losses on short legs must be funded at once; slow convergence; and forced buy-ins of borrowed securities, which tend to come when the basis has moved against the arbitrageur.
\end{itemize}
Risky arbitrage needs compensation, so arbitrage bounds are wide when arbitrage is risky \citep[same sec., p.~364]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} Arbitrage opportunities have two causes \citep[same chap., sec.~17.5, pp.~373--375]{alpha-harris}. In the \emph{slow price adjustment} scenario a common factor changes and some prices lag. Arbitrage then moves prices to their new values, and the arbitrageur should trade quickly, demanding liquidity. In the \emph{uninformed liquidity demand} scenario values are unchanged and uninformed traders push prices apart in different markets. The arbitrageur then supplies liquidity, competing with value traders and dealers, and need not trade aggressively. Opportunities do not arise when an instrument-specific value changes: then the fair value of the basis has moved, and a pairs trade against it loses \citep[same chap., pp.~358--360 and~368]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Never leverage to the limit of capital: those who do have no staying power when the basis moves against them \citep[same chap., secs.~17.4.2 and~17.4.4.2, pp.~368 and~372]{alpha-harris}. LTCM, highly leveraged in mean-reverting positions, failed in 1998 although many positions later paid \citep[same chap., box, p.~369]{alpha-harris}. In illiquid markets, trade the illiquid leg first, or interleave small orders in both legs, so as never to be far from hedged \citep[same chap., pp.~366--367]{alpha-harris}. To judge whether a move in the spread is an opportunity, ask which leg moved and why. In Harris's GE against S\&P~500 futures example, a widening caused by the index falling while GE lags is more likely an opportunity than one caused by GE rising, which may be firm-specific news \citep[same chap., box and Table~17-2, pp.~361--362]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy risk list also named \emph{staying power} and \emph{specific-factor} risk. In Harris staying power is the consequence of basis and carrying-cost risk under leverage, and specific factors are a source of basis risk and model error, not separate categories. The legacy principle \emph{common versus specific factor test} said residualisation decides whether a spread is an opportunity or new information. The passages support the test; ``residualisation'' is the legacy term for removing common-factor exposure and is not Harris's.

The pathway \texttt{funding-crowding} develops forced liquidation and crowding.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Order anticipation: front running, quote matching, sentiment-oriented technical trading, squeezes and stop gunning]\label{def:order-anticipation}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Order anticipators} profit from information about other traders' orders, not about fundamental values, by trading before them \citep[chap.~``Order Anticipators'', pp.~245 and~256--257]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{front runners} trade on orders others have decided to arrange. Ahead of aggressive traders they profit from the orders' price impact. Ahead of passive traders they are \emph{quote matchers}, or penny jumpers, who step in front of standing limit orders and keep the limit order as an option to exit: long stock plus the implicit put of a large buy order is a call \citep[sec.~11.1, pp.~245--250]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{sentiment-oriented technical traders} predict trades uninformed traders have not yet decided to make, from cash-flow timing, hedging needs, asset exchanges, gamblers' interests and speculators' mistakes \citep[sec.~11.2, pp.~251--254]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{squeezers} corner one side of a market so that those who must close positions have to trade with them \citep[sec.~11.3, pp.~254--256]{alpha-harris}.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Gunning the market}, pushing prices to trigger clustered stop orders and trading with them, is a manipulation of stop orders that Harris treats alongside these strategies \citep[sec.~11.4, p.~256]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} Effects \citep[same chap., secs.~11.1.3--11.1.4, 11.2.1 and~11.3, pp.~250--256]{alpha-harris}. Front running accelerates the impact of the traders it front-runs. It makes prices more informative in the short run when it front-runs informed traders, but in the long run it lowers their profits and investment in information, and so informativeness. It makes prices less informative when it front-runs uninformed traders. It generally makes markets less liquid: front runners' profits are other traders' costs, they offer liquidity back at worse prices, and they drive away the orders they feed on. Front runners benefit large traders only in narrow conditions: better traders who do not compete with each other and do not squeeze. Sentiment-oriented technical trading makes prices less informative, especially in bubbles, and loses when value traders push prices back, so it pays best in hard-to-value instruments. Squeezes make prices less informative and greatly raise others' costs.

\qffield{Practice} Time precedence with a meaningful minimum price increment makes quote matchers pay to step ahead, to the traders on the other side rather than the passive traders they front-run; a small tick weakens this protection. Passive traders defend themselves by hiding or splitting orders or switching to market orders, which raises their costs and reduces displayed size \citep[same chap., pp.~250 and~257]{alpha-harris}. Traders protect themselves from front runners by guarding order information, from sentiment traders by trading quickly, and from squeezers by keeping several ways to close \citep[same chap., p.~257]{alpha-harris}. NYSE and AMEX specialists, as Harris describes them in 2002, had privileged access to the order flow and used it in the same ways \citep[chap.~``Specialists'', sec.~24.5, pp.~501--508]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item speculating on short-term forecasts from the order flow;
\item quote matching against a heavy side of their book;
\item \emph{cream skimming}, stepping in front of the book to fill only the more profitable, usually small retail, marketable orders;
\item anticipating stop orders in their books;
\item \emph{stopping stock}, guaranteeing an execution price and then deciding when to fill, a look-back timing option that harms the limit-order traders who would have filled the order.
\end{itemize}
Public order precedence, the public liquidity preservation principle and audit trails constrain these privileges \citep[same chap., sec.~24.2.2, pp.~498--499]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Not all front running is illegal: it is illegal when the information is obtained improperly, through a confidential brokerage relationship or eavesdropping, but an observant floor trader may legally infer a broker's order \citep[chap.~``Order Anticipators'', sec.~11.1.1, pp.~246--247]{alpha-harris}. Squeezers and gunners defend themselves as speculators, which makes the laws against them hard to enforce \citep[same chap., pp.~256--257]{alpha-harris}. The legacy taxonomy listed quote matching and stop gunning as categories parallel to front running. In Harris quote matching is front running of passive traders, and gunning a manipulation of stop orders. The specialist strategies describe a floor-based market structure of 2002 and are dated.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Bluffing and market manipulation: rumourmongers, price manipulators and the asymmetric-impact condition]\label{def:bluffing-manipulation}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Bluffers} try to fool other traders into trading unwisely and then profit from them. Harris names two techniques \citep[chap.~``Bluffers and Market Manipulation'', p.~259 and Table~12-3, p.~274]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{rumourmongers} spread false information, or true information presented so that it will be misread;
\item \emph{price manipulators} arrange trades at prices, volumes and times meant to change others' opinions of value. The trades may be genuine arm's-length trades or \emph{wash trades} with confederates that create artificial activity.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Market manipulation} occurs when bluffers or their victims change prices from what they would otherwise be. Bluffing is profitable when the price impact of the bluffer's purchases differs from that of its sales: if buys move prices more, buy first and sell later, and conversely \citep[same chap., sec.~12.3, pp.~270--273]{alpha-harris}. In Harris's example, liquidity suppliers raise prices 10 cents per 100 contracts for 1{,}000-contract orders but only 5 cents for 500-contract orders. Buying 4{,}000 contracts in 1{,}000s and selling them in 500s then averages 12.50 bought against 12.875 sold, a profit of \$1.5 million on a 1{,}000-unit contract, and leaves the price at 12 rather than 10 \citep[same sec., p.~270, page image]{alpha-harris}. With the same impact per unit for all trades, the same round trip loses \$3 million (p.~271; both figures rechecked, Interpretation).

\qffield{Conditions} Bluffers imitate informed speculators: both promote their positions and trade to show confidence. The difference is that speculators trade on well-founded opinions about value, whereas bluffers manufacture the signals; bluffs make prices less informative and are more likely followed by reversals \citep[same chap., sec.~12.2.1, pp.~265--267]{alpha-harris}. Value traders foil bluffs by trading against prices pushed from value, unless bluffers have more capital and force them out. Bluffs therefore succeed most in securities value traders do not follow or cannot trade: illiquid, hard to value or hard to borrow. Long-side bluffs are probably commoner than short-side ones \citep[same chap., sec.~12.2.2, pp.~268--269]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Liquidity suppliers who set prices from net order flow must make buys and sells of every size, speed and timing move prices equally per unit traded; otherwise bluffers can exploit them \citep[same chap., sec.~12.3, pp.~271--273]{alpha-harris}. Momentum traders and liquidity suppliers, who react to the trades they see, are the most vulnerable \citep[same chap., sec.~12.4, p.~274]{alpha-harris}. Transaction costs limit bluffing, which trades intensively \citep[same chap., p.~273]{alpha-harris}. A legacy principle, \emph{impact symmetry disciplines bluffing}, restated this rule. It added that impact should depend on ``net pressure and risk'', of which Harris names only net quantity, and that a zero-net sequence then cannot create a higher average sale price than purchase price. That conclusion holds in Harris's example, where each trade's impact is immediate and permanent. In impact models with decay and changing liquidity, equal treatment of buys and sells does not exclude manipulation (see \hyperref[res:no-price-manipulation]{absence of price manipulation}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} A price reversal does not prove manipulation, because good speculators also misjudge values. Conviction usually requires evidence that the bluffer spread information known to be false or arranged wash trades, or testimony on intent; careful bluffers therefore pass as speculators \citep[same chap., sec.~12.2.3, pp.~269--270]{alpha-harris}. Harris's legality column (Table~12-3): false rumours and wash trades are illegal in most jurisdictions and easy to prosecute if caught; misleadingly framed true information and genuine trades meant to mislead are of questionable legality and hard to prove. The legacy taxonomy made wash traders a third type beside rumourmongers and price manipulators; in Harris wash trading is a technique of price manipulators.

\section{Information and price impact}

\begin{qfmodel}[Kyle single-auction model: informed trading and linear price impact]\label{mod:kyle-single-auction}
\qffield{Statement} One risk-neutral insider who knows the asset value $v\sim N(\mu,\sigma_v^2)$, price-insensitive noise traders with net order $u\sim N(0,\sigma_u^2)$ independent of $v$, and competitive risk-neutral market makers who see only the net order flow $y=x+u$ and set $S(y)=\mathbb{E}[v\mid y]$ \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', pp.~31--32]{crypto-cartea} have a linear equilibrium \citep[chap.~``Strategic Trader Models I: Informed Traders'', p.~93, eqs.~(4.3)--(4.5)]{catalogue-0496}:
\[ x(v)=\beta(v-\mu), \qquad S(y)=\mu+\lambda y, \qquad \beta=\frac{\sigma_u}{\sigma_v}, \qquad \lambda=\frac{\sigma_v}{2\sigma_u}. \]
The insider's ex ante expected profit is $\tfrac12\sigma_v\sigma_u$ \citep[same chap., p.~97, eq.~(4.17)]{catalogue-0496}, and the posterior variance of $v$ after the auction is exactly half the prior, whatever the order flow: price reveals half of the insider's information \citep[same chap., p.~98, eq.~(4.20)]{catalogue-0496}.

\qffield{Notation} Source notation: \texttt{catalogue-0496} writes $p_0$ for $\mu$, $\Sigma_0$ for $\sigma_v^2$, $\sigma_\mu$ for $\sigma_u$ and $P$ for the price; \texttt{crypto-cartea} writes $\sigma$ for $\sigma_v$. Here $\lambda$ is a price-impact slope, in price per share.

\qffield{Conditions} A single auction; the insider does not observe $u$ before trading; normality of $v$ and $u$; market makers earn zero expected profit given the order flow, so price is semi-strong efficient \citep[same chap., pp.~91--92]{catalogue-0496}; \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~32]{crypto-cartea}. There is no other linear equilibrium, but nonlinear equilibria are not excluded \citep[chap.~``Strategic Trader Models I: Informed Traders'', p.~97]{catalogue-0496}. Idea of the proof: given a linear price rule the insider's expected profit $x(v-\mu)-\lambda x^2$ is quadratic, so $\beta=1/(2\lambda)$; given a linear strategy, $v$ and $y$ are jointly normal and the projection $\mathbb{E}[v\mid y]$ is linear in $y$; the two conditions fix $\beta$ and $\lambda$ \citep[same chap., pp.~93--96]{catalogue-0496}.

\qffield{Practice} Doubling the noise-trader standard deviation doubles the insider's order and expected profit and leaves the ex ante price unchanged \citep[same chap., p.~97]{catalogue-0496}. Market makers are risk-neutral, so any premium compensates their informational disadvantage and is borne by the price-insensitive liquidity traders \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~31]{crypto-cartea}. Interpretation (own derivation): the insider trades only part of the mispricing, because a larger order reveals more through $y$; $\lambda$ rises with $\sigma_v$ and falls with $\sigma_u$.

\qffield{Pitfalls} A linear price rule implies a negative price for a large enough sell imbalance, so it is at best an approximation of actual price behaviour \citep[chap.~``Strategic Trader Models I: Informed Traders'', p.~96]{catalogue-0496}. The joint-normality assumption drives the predictions on trade size and volume \citep[same chap., p.~98]{catalogue-0496}. The textbook account in \texttt{crypto-cartea} prints the equilibrium price as $\mu+2(x+u)\sigma_u/\sigma$, so $\lambda=2\sigma_u/\sigma$ \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~33]{crypto-cartea}; this is the reciprocal of the value in \texttt{catalogue-0496}, eq.~(4.5), and of the value implied by its own preceding step $\beta=(2\lambda)^{-1}$. The authors' errata correct the price to $\mu+(x+u)\sigma/(2\sigma_u)$, so $\lambda=\sigma/(2\sigma_u)$, in agreement with \texttt{catalogue-0496} \citep[entries for p.~33]{crypto-cartea-errata}. Do not copy $\lambda$ from the printed page.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (contrast: a premium for risk, not for information); \hyperref[res:grossman-miller-liquidity-measures]{Grossman--Miller liquidity measures} (contrast: an inventory price-impact slope).

\qffield{Further reading} \citep{s6-kyle}, the original model and its dynamic version (not yet read for this entry); own full derivation, from the legacy notes: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/derivations.tex}.

Stability: draft. Currency: timeless (theory).

\section{Adverse selection and the bid--ask spread}

\begin{qfmodel}[Glosten--Milgrom sequential-trade model: spreads from adverse selection]\label{mod:glosten-milgrom}
\qffield{Statement} In a pure dealership market, investors arrive one at a time, randomly and anonymously, see the specialist's bid $b_t$ and ask $a_t$, and buy one unit at the ask, sell one unit at the bid, or leave; some arrivals are informed about the asset's future value $V$, the rest trade for liquidity reasons \citep[sec.~II, pp.~3--4]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. An arrival with information $\mathcal{F}_t$ has reservation price $Z_t=\rho_t\,\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{F}_t]$ and buys if $Z_t>a_t$, sells if $Z_t<b_t$; competitive, risk-neutral specialists with information $\mathcal{S}_t$ earn zero expected profit on each purchase and each sale, so the quotes solve \citep[p.~6]{glosten-milgrom-1983}
\[ a_t=\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{S}_t,\ Z_t>a_t], \qquad b_t=\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{S}_t,\ Z_t<b_t]. \]
Then $a_t\geq\mathbb{E}_t[V]\geq b_t$, with strict inequalities whenever informed buying above and informed selling below $\mathbb{E}_t[V]$ have positive probability: adverse selection alone produces a spread, with no inventory cost, risk aversion or monopoly power \citep[Prop.~1, pp.~7--9]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. Each transaction price is the specialist's updated expectation of $V$, so transaction prices form a martingale with respect to the specialist's and the public information \citep[Prop.~2, p.~10]{glosten-milgrom-1983}.

\qffield{Notation} $V>0$ is the asset's value, realised at a future date, with finite variance; $\rho_t$ is the arriving investor's liquidity (time-preference) parameter, with $\rho=1$ for the specialist; $\mathcal{F}_t$ and $\mathcal{S}_t$ are the information of the arrival and of the specialist; $\mathbb{E}_t$ is expectation given the information both share \citep[pp.~4--7]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. The paper writes $A_t,B_t$ for $a_t,b_t$, $F_t,S_t$ for $\mathcal{F}_t,\mathcal{S}_t$.

\qffield{Conditions} Every participant is risk-neutral with utility $\rho xV+c$ over shares $x$ and consumption $c$; $\rho$ is independent across traders and carries no information about $V$ \citep[pp.~4--5]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. Uninformed participation is exogenous; trades are single units; short positions in cash or stock cost nothing; zero profit on each side is motivated by Bertrand competition between equally informed specialists, and existence of the quotes is not shown in general \citep[pp.~4--6]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. If the specialist sees only public information, the ask is the smallest $a$ with $a\geq\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{H}_t,Z_t>a]$ and the bid the largest $b$ with $b\leq\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{H}_t,Z_t<b]$ \citep[p.~7]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. Idea of the proof of Proposition~1: conditional on a buy, the ask equals the expectation of $V$ given the buy; since buying selects arrivals whose own expectation exceeds a threshold and $\rho$ is uninformative, that conditional expectation is at least $\mathbb{E}_t[V]$ \citep[p.~8]{glosten-milgrom-1983}.

\qffield{Practice} The spread is larger, for given information, when insiders' information is finer, when the ratio of informed to uninformed arrivals rises, or when the dispersion of the uninformed liquidity parameter around its median falls \citep[Prop.~5, pp.~15--16]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. If buy and sell probabilities stay above $\theta_a,\theta_b>0$, prices converge and the root-mean-square spread over the first $k$ trades is at most $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(V)/(\theta_a\theta_b k)}$ \citep[Prop.~3, p.~11]{glosten-milgrom-1983}; with Poisson arrivals the average spread is then of the order of one over the square root of volume, because trades reveal information \citep[pp.~18--19]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. The specialist and the arrivals come to agree on the expected value of $V$ \citep[Prop.~4, p.~13]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. The market maker need not know which traders are informed: since well-informed traders rarely reveal themselves, dealers treat every order as possibly informed and revise prices with the side of each trade \citep[chap.~``Dealers'', pp.~288--289]{alpha-harris}. The two-point version with closed-form half-spreads is \hyperref[res:glosten-milgrom-half-spreads]{a separate result}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} If the liquidity motive is weak relative to the insiders' advantage, the zero-profit quotes may preclude trade, and the market shuts down until enough public information neutralises the insiders' advantage \citep[p.~9]{glosten-milgrom-1983}; if the bounded-probability condition fails, the spread need not shrink: the market may close, or every trade may occur at the bid \citep[p.~12]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. Proposition~5 compares spreads for given information; it does not say that stocks with more insiders have wider spreads on average, since more insiders also reveal information faster \citep[p.~17]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. The authors list unit lots, the absence of risk aversion and inventory costs, and a fixed underlying value as limitations \citep[sec.~IV, pp.~24--25]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. The locators are to the 1983 discussion paper, not the 1985 journal article that the catalogue names.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:glosten-milgrom-half-spreads]{Glosten--Milgrom half-spreads} (special case: two values, closed form); \hyperref[res:glosten-milgrom-market-collapse]{market collapse} (special case: price-sensitive liquidity traders); \hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{adverse-selection spread component} (special case: a textbook example); \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle single-auction model} (contrast: batch order flow and a strategic insider); \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (contrast: a spread for inventory risk, without information).

\begin{qfresult}[Glosten--Milgrom half-spreads with two values]\label{res:glosten-milgrom-half-spreads}
\qffield{Statement} Let the asset's value be $V_H$ with probability $p$ and $V_L<V_H$ otherwise, let a fraction $\alpha$ of traders be informed, and let competitive risk-neutral market makers quote $a=\mu+\Delta_a$ and $b=\mu-\Delta_b$ around $\mu=\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{F}]$. Zero expected profit on each side gives the half-spreads \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~35]{crypto-cartea}
\[ \Delta_a=\frac{\alpha p}{\alpha p+(1-\alpha)/2}\,(V_H-\mu)=\frac{V_H-\mu}{1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{1/2}{p}}, \qquad \Delta_b=\frac{\mu-V_L}{1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{1/2}{1-p}}. \]
With trading over time, $p_t=\mathbb{P}(V=V_H\mid\mathcal{F}_t)$ and $\mu_t=\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{F}_t]$ are updated by Bayes' rule from the order flow, the same formulas hold with $p_t,\mu_t$, and each execution price equals the expected value given the history and the side of the trade, so the price at execution times is a martingale \citep[same chap., p.~36]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Notation} Local override for this entry and \hyperref[res:glosten-milgrom-market-collapse]{market collapse}: $p$ is the probability of the high value, not a price; $\alpha$ is the fraction of informed traders; $\Delta_a,\Delta_b$ are the ask and bid half-spreads; $\mathcal{F}$ is public information, including the order flow. Source notation: \texttt{crypto-cartea} writes $v$ for $V$.

\qffield{Conditions} A simplified, essentially static version of the model: one-unit orders; informed traders know $V$ and buy one unit if $a<V_H$ and $V=V_H$, sell one unit if $b>V_L$ and $V=V_L$; liquidity traders are price-insensitive and buy or sell with probability $\tfrac12$ each, whatever the value; market makers are risk-neutral, carry inventory at no cost and compete to zero expected profit, using only public information \citep[same chap., pp.~34--35]{crypto-cartea}. Idea of the proof: a buy comes from a liquidity trader with weight $(1-\alpha)/2$, who pays $\Delta_a$, and from an informed trader with weight $\alpha p$, who costs $V_H-\mu-\Delta_a$; setting the weighted profit to zero gives $\Delta_a$, and the bid is symmetric \citep[same chap., p.~35]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Practice} Reading asymmetric information as toxicity, $\alpha$ is its prevalence, and $p$ with $V_H-\mu$, or $1-p$ with $\mu-V_L$, its magnitude on the buy or sell side; market makers widen each half-spread as the prevalence and magnitude of toxicity on that side rise \citep[same chap., pp.~35--36]{crypto-cartea}. Interpretation (own derivation): as $\alpha\to0$ both half-spreads vanish, and as $\alpha\to1$ the quotes reach $V_H$ and $V_L$, where only informed traders would trade.

\qffield{Pitfalls} This is a textbook simplification: the paper allows a general value distribution and price-sensitive uninformed traders \citep[sec.~II, pp.~3--6]{glosten-milgrom-1983}. With price-insensitive liquidity traders a monopolist would quote $a=V_H$, $b=V_L$; the formulas rely on competition between market makers \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~35]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:glosten-milgrom]{Glosten--Milgrom model} (generalisation); \hyperref[res:glosten-milgrom-market-collapse]{market collapse} (extension: price-sensitive liquidity traders); \hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{adverse-selection spread component} (contrast: a parametrisation by the dealer's pricing error).

\qffield{Index} Glosten--Milgrom half-spread; ask half-spread; bid half-spread; toxicity; prevalence of informed trading; Bayesian quote updating; zero-profit market making

\end{qfresult}

\begin{qfresult}[Glosten--Milgrom with price-sensitive liquidity traders: market collapse]\label{res:glosten-milgrom-market-collapse}
\qffield{Statement} If liquidity trader $i$ trades only when the half-spread is at most his urgency $c_i$, with $c_i$ distributed with cumulative distribution function $F$ across liquidity traders, the zero-profit half-spreads are defined implicitly by \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~37]{crypto-cartea}; \citep[entry for p.~37]{crypto-cartea-errata}
\[ \Delta_a=\frac{V_H-\mu}{1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{(1-F(\Delta_a))/2}{p}}, \qquad \Delta_b=\frac{\mu-V_L}{1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{(1-F(\Delta_b))/2}{1-p}}. \]
Widening a half-spread drives out liquidity traders; if urgency is small, the only solutions may be the extreme ones, $\Delta_a=V_H-\mu$ and $\Delta_b=\mu-V_L$: the market collapses, only informed traders, who are then indifferent, might trade, and any trade reveals the value \citep[chap.~``A Primer on the Microstructure of Financial Markets'', p.~37]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Notation} As in \hyperref[res:glosten-milgrom-half-spreads]{the two-value half-spreads}; $c_i$ is the cash-equivalent gain to liquidity trader $i$ from completing the desired trade.

\qffield{Conditions} The two-value model of \hyperref[res:glosten-milgrom-half-spreads]{the half-spreads entry}, with only the fraction $1-F(\Delta)$ of would-be liquidity buyers or sellers trading at half-spread $\Delta$ \citep[same chap., p.~37]{crypto-cartea}. ``Small'' urgency means that no liquidity trader's urgency exceeds the expected value of the asset: $F(\mu-\epsilon)=1$ for some $\epsilon>0$ \citep[same chap., p.~37, n.~5]{crypto-cartea}.

\qffield{Practice} The paper gives the same mechanism: when the proportion of insiders is high or the liquidity motive of the uninformed weak, the zero-profit quotes can preclude trade, a lemons problem as in Akerlof, and the market reopens when public information neutralises the insiders' advantage \citep[pp.~9 and~17]{glosten-milgrom-1983}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The book prints $F(\Delta_a)$ in the bid formula; the authors' errata correct it to $F(\Delta_b)$, as given above \citep[entry for p.~37]{crypto-cartea-errata}. Do not copy the bid formula from the printed page.

\begin{qfresult}[Spread components: the adverse-selection component, its two views, and the total spread]\label{res:adverse-selection-spread-component}
\qffield{Statement} The bid--ask spread is split, for analysis, into a \emph{transaction-cost} (transitory) component $C$, which pays the dealer's normal costs of doing business, and an \emph{adverse-selection} (permanent) component $A$, which recovers from uninformed traders what the dealer loses to informed ones \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', pp.~298--299]{alpha-harris}. A dealer first estimates the value $V_0$ from all current information, then the values $V^B$ and $V^S$ she would estimate if the next trader were a buyer or a seller; $A=V^B-V^S$, and she quotes \citep[same chap., Figure~14-1, p.~301]{alpha-harris}
\[ a=V^B+\tfrac12C, \qquad b=V^S-\tfrac12C, \qquad a-b=A+C. \]
If the dealer learns nothing more from the next trade than its side, the conditional estimate becomes her new value: after a buy both quotes rise by $A/2$ \citep[same chap., Figure~14-2, pp.~301--302]{alpha-harris}. In a simple example based on the Glosten--Milgrom model, a dealer believes that the security is worth $V$ unconditionally, that the next trader is equally likely to buy or sell and is informed with probability $\alpha$, and that it is worth $V+e$ if an informed trader buys and $V-e$ if an informed trader sells \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', p.~320]{alpha-harris}. Information view: conditional on a buy the dealer's value estimate is $(1-\alpha)V+\alpha(V+e)=V+\alpha e$, conditional on a sell $V-\alpha e$, so the adverse-selection component is $2\alpha e$ \citep[same chap., p.~320]{alpha-harris}. Accounting view: expected profit on the next trade is $\tfrac12(a-b)-\alpha e$, so breaking even requires $a-b=2\alpha e$ \citep[same chap., pp.~320--321]{alpha-harris}. Both views give the same component \citep[same chap., p.~300]{alpha-harris}; in the example $A=2\alpha e$ and $C=0$.

\qffield{Notation} $A$ and $C$ are amounts of the full spread, in price units; $a$, $b$ are the ask and bid. Source notation: Harris names the components in words and writes $V_0^B$, $V_0^S$ for $V^B$, $V^S$ (Figure~14-1); in his appendix he writes $P$ for $\alpha$, $E$ for $e$ (the dealer's pricing error when the trader is informed), and $A$, $B$ for the ask and bid $a$, $b$, so his $A$ there is not the component.

\qffield{Conditions} Normal costs of doing business are ignored; the example assumes that dealers know the value when informed traders trade, which the result does not need \citep[same chap., pp.~300 and~320]{alpha-harris}. The two views agree when the loss from trading with an informed trader equals the pricing error, as when the dealer cannot restore inventory before prices adjust; if the dealer can rebalance but does not recognise the informed trade, informed traders keep trading with him until the price adjusts, and his cumulative loss is still the original pricing error \citep[same chap., p.~302]{alpha-harris}. Low barriers to entry and competition from public limit orders, which undercut wide dealer quotes, limit monopoly spreads \citep[same chap., p.~298]{alpha-harris}; in a competitive dealer market free entry and exit drive dealers to normal profits, so the total spread is this component plus a transaction-cost component that covers operating costs, financing, any monopoly profit and any premium for inventory risk \citep[same chap., pp.~298--299]{alpha-harris}. $V^B$ and $V^S$ lie equally far from $V_0$, at $V_0\pm A/2$, when informed buyers and sellers are equally likely and their expected pricing errors equal \citep[same chap., p.~301]{alpha-harris}. A three-way split (order processing, inventory, adverse selection) separates the inventory-risk premium out of $C$; Hasbrouck likewise notes that a spread impounds noninformational costs and inventory effects as well as asymmetric information \citep[sec.~5.5, p.~53]{s6-hasbrouck}. In the structural sequential-trade version with a cost $c$ per trade, $a=\mathbb{E}[V\mid\text{buy}]+c$ and $b=\mathbb{E}[V\mid\text{sell}]-c$; the bid and ask sequences are martingales but trade prices are not \citep[sec.~5.4.2, p.~49]{s6-hasbrouck}. There $c$ is a per-side amount, so Harris's $C$ corresponds to $2c$ (Interpretation).

\qffield{Practice} The component rises with the probability that the next trader is informed, with the materiality of the information and with trade size, because dealers believe large traders are often well informed \citep[chap.~``Dealers'', pp.~289--291 and~295]{alpha-harris}. Price changes from the adverse-selection component are permanent, while the transaction-cost component causes bid--ask bounce \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', pp.~299--300]{alpha-harris}. Volatile instruments have wider spreads, both through inventory risk for risk-averse dealers and as a proxy for asymmetric information \citep[same chap., pp.~312--313]{alpha-harris}; the other determinants are in \hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants}. Uninformed traders lose to informed ones whichever order they use: their limit orders fill quickly when informed traders want the other side and fail to fill when informed traders compete on the same side, and their market orders pay spreads widened by adverse selection, so that the component is in effect a fee dealers charge market-order traders for bearing adverse-selection risk \citep[same chap., sec.~14.3, pp.~303--304, Table~14-1]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes said that $A$ is twice the dealer's mark-to-market loss against a correctly signed informed trade. In the example that holds only if $\alpha=1$: against an informed buyer the dealer's loss relative to $V$ is the whole pricing error $e$, while $A/2=\alpha e$ is the expected revision given a buy, that is, the expected loss per buy, informed and uninformed buyers together, of a dealer whose ask is at $V$ (Interpretation: own derivation from the example, pp.~300--302 and~320). The two views equate a probability-weighted loss with a conditional revision, not $A$ with the loss on one informed trade. This is a textbook example, not the Glosten--Milgrom paper, whose quotes are expectations conditional on a trade at the quote (\hyperref[mod:glosten-milgrom]{the model}); the legacy note that attributed $a=V+\alpha e$, $b=V-\alpha e$ to Glosten and Milgrom followed this example. The accounting view fixes only the width $a-b$; the level $V\pm\alpha e$ comes from the information view (Interpretation). Harris prints the dealer's profit against an informed buyer as $A-(V-E)$; his stated result requires $A-(V+E)$ \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', p.~321]{alpha-harris}.

\section{Dealer inventory and quote adjustment}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Dealer inventory imbalance and the two inventory risks]\label{def:dealer-inventory-risk}
\qffield{Statement} A dealer's \emph{target inventory} is the position it wants to hold, and its \emph{inventory imbalance} is actual inventory minus target \citep[chap.~``Dealers'', p.~283]{alpha-harris}. Dealers face two inventory risks, distinguished by whether future price changes are correlated with the imbalance \citep[same chap., p.~285]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{diversifiable inventory risk}: price changes independent of the imbalance; gains and losses are equally likely, they do not cause losses in the long run, and dealing in many instruments reduces their effect on total profit \citep[same chap., pp.~285--286]{alpha-harris};
\item \emph{adverse-selection risk}: price changes inversely correlated with the imbalance, because informed traders take the side their information favours, make order flow one-sided and push the dealer's inventory away from target before prices move \citep[same chap., p.~286]{alpha-harris}.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} If long and short positions are equally costly, a dealer that does not also speculate, hedge or invest has a target of zero; where shorting costs more, targets are positive; speculating dealers set targets by their views \citep[same chap., pp.~283--284]{alpha-harris}. Large imbalances are costly to finance and expose the dealer to losses if prices move, and an imbalance beyond the dealer's capital forces liquidation \citep[same chap., p.~284]{alpha-harris}; a dealer can also hedge an imbalance with a correlated instrument \citep[same chap., p.~285, Table~13-2]{alpha-harris}. Interpretation: the costs of carrying inventory thus group into price risk, the financing of long and the borrowing of short positions, the use of capital, and the basis risk and costs of hedges; recall risk and risk-limit use, which the legacy notes also listed, are not in the passages checked.

\qffield{Practice} Dealers avoid both risks by keeping inventory under control, so most attend to \emph{market values}, the prices that produce two-sided order flow, more than to fundamental values, which they discover as a by-product \citep[same chap., pp.~286--287]{alpha-harris}. Adverse selection is still the most important determinant of dealer profitability \citep[same chap., p.~286]{alpha-harris}. Inventory risk does not need informed traders: diversifiable inventory risk is present when no one is better informed, risk-averse dealers require a premium for it, mainly in highly volatile instruments, and that premium is part of the transaction-cost spread component \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', pp.~299 and~313]{alpha-harris}; see \hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{spread components}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} ``Diversifiable'' here means unrelated to the dealer's imbalance; within one instrument it is still a risk that a risk-averse dealer is paid to bear, as in \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller} (Interpretation). A falling inventory after buys does not by itself say which risk is present; separating them needs the subsequent price path (Interpretation).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:dealer-quote-adjustment]{dealer quote adjustment} (how dealers control the imbalance); \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (a model of the first risk); \hyperref[mod:glosten-milgrom]{Glosten--Milgrom model} (a model of the second); \hyperref[mod:inventory-based-quotes]{inventory-based quotes} (models of quotes as functions of the imbalance).

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Dealer quote adjustment for inventory and suspected informed trading]\label{meth:dealer-quote-adjustment}
\qffield{Statement} Dealers control inventory and adverse selection with four levers: the level of both quotes, their skew, the width of the spread and the sizes shown.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Inventory.} To reduce inventory, lower bid and ask, decrease the bid size and raise the ask size; to increase it, do the opposite; an impatient dealer can take another trader's offer or hit another trader's bid, or hedge with a correlated instrument \citep[chap.~``Dealers'', pp.~284--285, Table~13-2]{alpha-harris}.
\item \emph{After a suspected informed trade.} After selling to a suspected well-informed buyer, raise the ask and lower its size to discourage further informed buying, raise the bid and its size to rebuild inventory, or buy at another trader's ask or buy a correlated instrument; after buying from a suspected informed seller, do the reverse \citep[same chap., pp.~287--288, Table~13-3]{alpha-harris}.
\item \emph{Before the next trade.} Set the ask at the value expected if the next trader buys and the bid at the value expected if the next trader sells, adjusting more when informed trading is more likely and the information more material \citep[same chap., pp.~288--290]{alpha-harris}; see \hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{the adverse-selection component}.
\item \emph{Size.} Avoid displaying large size, so that traders must ask for it and reveal themselves \citep[same chap., p.~291]{alpha-harris}.
\end{enumerate}

\qffield{Conditions} Dealers rarely know who is informed: informed traders hide behind brokers and claim to be uninformed, and rules of thumb (large, active, impatient traders are often informed; small retail traders rarely) are useful but unreliable, so every order is treated as possibly informed \citep[same chap., pp.~288--289]{alpha-harris}. Spreads widen with asymmetric information, volatility and weak utilitarian trading interest \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', pp.~312--313]{alpha-harris} (\hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants}).

\qffield{Practice} Searching for prices that bring two-sided order flow is price discovery: it finds market values \citep[chap.~``Dealers'', p.~284]{alpha-harris}. Demanding liquidity to rebalance gives a negative realised spread \citep[same chap., p.~284]{alpha-harris}. After a buy, raising quotes is right if the buyer was informed and a mistake if not, when value traders sell to the dealer and realised spreads fall; doing nothing is the reverse; buying at another trader's ask may limit losses against an informed buyer but is a big mistake against an uninformed one \citep[same chap., pp.~291--292, Table~13-4]{alpha-harris}. Poorly informed dealers more often mistake uninformed for informed clients, so they turn inventory quickly and earn smaller realised spreads; well-informed dealers can hold larger positions longer and earn larger realised spreads \citep[same chap., pp.~292--294]{alpha-harris}. Value traders trade when prices diverge from fundamental value, often after such mistakes or after risk-averse dealers obtain large concessions for taking big positions, and in doing so restore dealers' inventories \citep[same chap., pp.~291 and~294]{alpha-harris}. Some dealers avoid informed traders by trading only with retail or known clients, refusing anonymous size, or slicing large orders over time, which lets them offer more liquidity to the flow they accept \citep[same chap., pp.~290--291]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Informed traders can bluff, sending small orders on one side to move a dealer before trading on the other; against traders they cannot identify, dealers should adjust prices at the same rate per quantity whatever the side, speed, size or sequence of orders \citep[same chap., pp.~292 and~294]{alpha-harris}. Interpretation: refusing flow protects the dealer but reduces the immediacy available to others; one-sided flow turns dealing into warehousing risk. These are heuristics without an objective function; \hyperref[mod:avellaneda-stoikov]{Avellaneda--Stoikov} derives the inventory skew from one.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:dealer-inventory-risk]{dealer inventory risk} (prerequisite); \hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{adverse-selection spread component} (the spread set before the next trade); \hyperref[mod:avellaneda-stoikov]{Avellaneda--Stoikov quoting} (a model of the inventory skew); \hyperref[mod:glosten-milgrom]{Glosten--Milgrom model} (a model of the revision after each trade); \hyperref[mod:inventory-based-quotes]{inventory-based quotes} (textbook models of quotes as functions of inventory); \hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants} (why spreads differ across instruments).

\begin{qfmodel}[Inventory-based dealer quotes: reservation prices linear in inventory (Stoll; Ho--Stoll as reported by O'Hara)]\label{mod:inventory-based-quotes}
\qffield{Statement} In Hasbrouck's version of Stoll's one-period model, a dealer with CARA utility $U(W)=-e^{-\gamma W}$ holds $n$ shares of a security with payoff $X\sim N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2)$, borrowing and lending at a zero rate, and posts a bid $B$ for one share that leaves him indifferent to its execution; equating certainty equivalents gives \citep[sec.~11.2, eq.~(11.2), p.~110]{s6-hasbrouck}
\[ B=\mu_X-(2n+1)\,\frac{\gamma\sigma_X^2}{2}. \]
If the dealer could first trade at a notional price $P$, his subjective valuation, his optimal holding would be $n^\ast=(\mu_X-P)/(\gamma\sigma_X^2)$, and at that optimum $B=P-\gamma\sigma_X^2/2$ \citep[same sec., pp.~110--111]{s6-hasbrouck}. The same indifference for a one-share sale gives $A=\mu_X-(2n-1)\gamma\sigma_X^2/2$ (Interpretation: own derivation; the source gives only the bid), so the quotes are
\[ B=r-\tfrac12\gamma\sigma_X^2, \qquad A=r+\tfrac12\gamma\sigma_X^2, \qquad r=\mu_X-\gamma\sigma_X^2\,n=P-\gamma\sigma_X^2\,(n-n^\ast): \]
the midpoint of the quotes falls linearly as inventory rises above target, and the spread $\gamma\sigma_X^2$ does not depend on inventory.

\qffield{Notation} $n$ is the dealer's inventory and $n^\ast$ his target; $r$ is the reservation (indifference) midpoint; $A$, $B$ are the dealer's ask and bid. The source writes $\alpha$ for the risk aversion $\gamma$, and $\mu_x$, $\sigma_x^2$ in eq.~(11.2). The legacy notes wrote $r_t=m_t-\kappa(q_t-q^\star)$ with spread $s_t$; here $\kappa=s=\gamma\sigma_X^2$, $q^\star=n^\ast$ and $m=P$.

\qffield{Conditions} One period, a normally distributed payoff, exponential utility, one-share trades, and no information asymmetry: the quotes compensate only for being pulled off the optimal portfolio \citep[same sec., p.~110]{s6-hasbrouck}. Idea of the proof: with CARA utility and normal wealth the certainty equivalent is $\mu_W-\gamma\sigma_W^2/2$; set it equal with and without the trade, and solve for the price \citep[same sec., p.~110]{s6-hasbrouck}. With two correlated securities the bid for the first is $B_1=\mu_1-\tfrac12\gamma\sigma_1\big[(1+2n_1)\sigma_1+2\rho n_2\sigma_2\big]$, lower when the securities are positively correlated, but at the optimum it is again $P_1-\gamma\sigma_1^2/2$ and the correlation drops out, a consequence of CARA utility \citep[same sec., eq.~(11.3), p.~111]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Practice} O'Hara's account of the multiperiod model of Ho and Stoll (1981): a monopolistic, risk-averse dealer facing Poisson buy and sell arrivals whose rates are linear in his ask and bid adjustments around a fixed true price $p$, quoting $p+a$ and $p-b$ to maximise expected utility of terminal wealth over a finite horizon; the problem has no known closed form, and Ho and Stoll approximate it with a first-order expansion and quadratic utility \citep[sec.~2.3, pp.~29--35]{catalogue-0496}. As O'Hara reports them, three properties follow: the spread is the risk-neutral monopoly spread, set by the slopes of the arrival functions, plus a risk adjustment that rises with relative risk aversion, transaction size, the stock's variance and the time remaining; transaction uncertainty by itself does not affect the spread; and the spread does not depend on inventory, the dealer instead shifting both quotes (with true price 50, quotes 48 and 52 move to 47 and 51 after a purchase at the bid) by an amount set by risk aversion, the stock's risk and his wealth \citep[same sec., pp.~35--36]{catalogue-0496}. Garman's fixed-quote dealer is ruined with probability one, within days for realistic parameters, which is why quotes must depend on inventory; in Amihud and Mendelson's risk-neutral model with inventory bounds both quotes fall as inventory rises, but the spread \emph{widens} with distance from the preferred position and the quotes are not symmetric about the true value \citep[secs.~11.1.1--11.1.2, pp.~107--109]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Neither original paper was consulted: the model is Hasbrouck's version of Stoll (1978), and the Ho--Stoll properties are O'Hara's report, not checked against the paper, \texttt{ho-stoll-1981}, which the route's \texttt{catalogue-0527} does not identify by title. A spread independent of inventory is a feature of these models, not a general result (Amihud and Mendelson above). O'Hara notes that a fixed true price is fundamental to the Ho--Stoll analysis, and that its finite horizon, with liquidation certain at the end, implies spreads narrowing predictably through the day, which seems unrealistic \citep[sec.~2.3, p.~36]{catalogue-0496}. Empirically, quotes rising after a customer purchase fit inventory control and adverse selection alike; NYSE specialist quote changes are explained mostly by trades, with inventories adding little, and dealers manage positions more through nonpublic quotes, interdealer trading and discretion over participation than through posted quotes \citep[secs.~11.4--11.5, pp.~115--117]{s6-hasbrouck}; see \hyperref[res:spread-component-identification]{component identification}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:avellaneda-stoikov]{Avellaneda--Stoikov quoting} (generalisation: continuous time, with the spread also independent of inventory in its approximation); \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (contrast: risk compensation set by market clearing, not by a quoting dealer); \hyperref[def:dealer-inventory-risk]{dealer inventory risk} (prerequisite); \hyperref[meth:dealer-quote-adjustment]{dealer quote adjustment} (practice).

\section{Measuring and decomposing the spread}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Spread measures: quoted, inside, relative, effective and realised spreads]\label{def:spread-measures}
\qffield{Statement} With best bid $b_t$, best ask $a_t$ and midpoint $m_t=\tfrac12(a_t+b_t)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{quoted spread} is $a_t-b_t$; the \emph{inside spread} is the highest bid's distance from the lowest ask across all dealers, and is no wider than the narrowest dealer's spread \citep[chap.~``Dealers'', p.~280]{alpha-harris};
\item the \emph{relative spread} is the spread as a fraction of price, the only basis on which spreads of different instruments compare \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', p.~312]{alpha-harris};
\item for a trade at $p_t$ with sign $Q_t=+1$ if buyer-initiated and $-1$ if seller-initiated, the \emph{effective spread} is twice the signed difference from the midpoint at the time of the trade, and the \emph{realised spread} twice the signed difference from the midpoint a set interval $\Delta$ later, commonly 5, 10, 15 or 60 minutes \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', pp.~424--425]{alpha-harris}:
\end{itemize}
\[ ES_t=2Q_t(p_t-m_t), \qquad RS_t=2Q_t(p_t-m_{t+\Delta}), \qquad ES_t=RS_t+2Q_t(m_{t+\Delta}-m_t). \]
The last term, the signed midpoint revision after the trade, is sometimes used as an estimate of the trade's price impact \citep[sec.~14.2.2, eq.~(14.2), p.~148]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Notation} $Q_t$ is the trade sign. Harris's effective and realised spreads are doubled, full-spread amounts; Hasbrouck's \emph{effective cost} $p_t-m_t$ and \emph{realised cost} $p_t-m_{t+5}$ are per-side amounts written for a buy order, with the midpoint five minutes after the order's receipt, as in the SEC's Rule 605 \citep[same sec., p.~148]{s6-hasbrouck}. Harris calls the undoubled signed difference the \emph{liquidity premium} \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', p.~425]{alpha-harris}. The legacy notes wrote $\epsilon_t$ for $Q_t$, $s_t$ for the quoted spread and $PI_t$ for the impact term, and measured the relative spread against $m_t$.

\qffield{Conditions} The effective spread equals the quoted spread when every purchase is at the ask and every sale at the bid; it is smaller with price improvement and larger when large orders fill outside the quotes. Realised and effective spreads are equal if the midpoint does not move over the interval; usually it moves with the trade, so realised spreads are smaller \citep[same chap., p.~425]{alpha-harris}. When the analyst did not take part in the trades, signs are inferred, usually by the Lee--Ready rule, which overstates costs for traders who do not only take liquidity and cannot see orders filled in several trades \citep[same chap., p.~423]{alpha-harris}. Delayed trade reports bias effective costs down, and hidden orders and market-maker improvement distort the inferred sign \citep[sec.~14.3.3, pp.~150--151]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Practice} Quoted minus effective spread measures the price improvement dealers give; effective minus realised spread measures dealers' losses to well-informed traders \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', pp.~425--426]{alpha-harris}. The realised cost is the revenue of a dealer who sold to the customer at $p_t$ and covered at the later midpoint \citep[sec.~14.2.2, p.~148]{s6-hasbrouck}. The effective spread is the least noisy benchmark estimator, because its benchmark is contemporaneous; the realised spread grows noisier the longer the interval \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', p.~427]{alpha-harris}. The quoted spread measures the cost of immediacy for small orders only; depth and resiliency are other aspects of liquidity \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', p.~312]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Harris also uses \emph{realised spread} for the difference between the prices at which a dealer actually sells and buys on a round trip, which can be negative when quotes move against the inventory \citep[chap.~``Dealers'', p.~280]{alpha-harris}; that is a dealer's outcome, not the midpoint-based estimator above. Mixing doubled and per-side conventions changes figures by a factor of two. Interpretation: effective minus realised spread is the permanent component only if the interval is long enough for prices to adjust and short enough to exclude unrelated news, and quote moves for inventory that have not yet reversed fall into it too; see \hyperref[res:spread-component-identification]{component identification}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{spread components} (what the measures estimate); \hyperref[res:roll-spread-estimator]{Roll's estimator} (alternative: a spread from trade prices alone); \hyperref[def:outside-spread]{outside spread} (contrast: an undisplayed willingness to trade).

\begin{qfresult}[Roll's serial-covariance spread estimator]\label{res:roll-spread-estimator}
\qffield{Statement} Let trade prices be $P_t=V_t+\tfrac12SQ_t$, where the fundamental value $V_t$ is a random walk with independent innovations $\varepsilon_t$ of mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$, $S$ is the spread, and the trade sign $Q_t=\pm1$ is a buy with probability one-half, independently of earlier signs and of $\varepsilon_t$ and $\varepsilon_{t+1}$. Then \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', box, p.~415]{alpha-harris}
\[ \operatorname{Var}(\Delta P_t)=\sigma^2+\tfrac12S^2, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})=-\tfrac14S^2, \qquad S=2\sqrt{-\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})}, \]
and all autocovariances of order two or more are zero \citep[sec.~3.4, p.~30]{s6-hasbrouck}. Roll's estimator replaces the covariance by its sample value \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', p.~415]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Notation} $S$ is the full spread, in price units; changes are between successive trades. Hasbrouck writes $p_t=m_t+cq_t$ with half-spread $c=S/2$, efficient-price innovation $u_t$ of variance $\sigma_u^2$, and $\gamma_0,\gamma_1$ for the variance and first autocovariance of price changes, so $\gamma_0=2c^2+\sigma_u^2$, $\gamma_1=-c^2$, $c=\sqrt{-\gamma_1}$ and $\sigma_u^2=\gamma_0+2\gamma_1$ \citep[sec.~3.4, eqs.~(3.3)--(3.5), pp.~29--30]{s6-hasbrouck}. Harris calls the estimate an effective spread \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', p.~434]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} The spread is constant and noninformational: competitive dealers quote $m_t\pm c$ to cover a cost $c$ per trade \citep[sec.~3.4, p.~29]{s6-hasbrouck}. Idea of the proof: $\Delta P_t=\varepsilon_t+\tfrac12S(Q_t-Q_{t-1})$; every cross-product has zero expectation except $\mathbb{E}Q_{t-1}^2=1$, which gives $-\tfrac14S^2$ for the first autocovariance \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', p.~415]{alpha-harris}. The variance splits into a fundamental part $\sigma^2$ and a transitory part from bid--ask bounce \citep[same chap., pp.~415--416]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} The estimator is used where quotes are unavailable \citep[sec.~3.4, p.~30]{s6-hasbrouck}; Harris advises using it only then \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', p.~434]{alpha-harris}. For one NYSE stock (PCO, October 2003), $\hat\gamma_1=-0.0000294$ gives $c=\$0.017$ and a spread of $\$0.034$, against a time-weighted average quoted spread of $\$0.032$ \citep[sec.~3.4, p.~30]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} In that sample the assumptions fail: the spread ranged from \$0.01 to \$0.49, successive trade signs had correlation about $0.34$, and midpoint changes were positively correlated with the latest trade sign \citep[sec.~3.4, p.~30]{s6-hasbrouck}. The biases go both ways. Positively autocorrelated signs, $\operatorname{Corr}(q_t,q_{t-1})=\rho>0$, give $\gamma_1=-c^2(1-2\rho)$ and bias $\hat c$ down; signs positively correlated with the efficient-price innovation, as with informed trading, give $\gamma_1=-c(c+\rho\sigma_u)$ and bias $\hat c$ up \citep[sec.~4.4, Exercises~4.2--4.3, pp.~38--39]{s6-hasbrouck}. A positive sample autocovariance is not uncommon, and estimation error alone can produce one when the model is correct; the square root is then undefined \citep[sec.~4.5, p.~39]{s6-hasbrouck}. Harris says violations typically make it underestimate the spread \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', p.~434]{alpha-harris}, and that reversals lasting beyond one trade make Roll's variance components understate transitory volatility \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', p.~416]{alpha-harris}. The legacy notes listed informed trading among causes of a less negative covariance; under Hasbrouck's Exercise~4.3 it makes the covariance more negative. Price discreteness and hidden liquidity, also listed there, are not discussed in the passages checked.

\begin{qfresult}[Separating transitory and permanent spread components: bounce, revisions and identification]\label{res:spread-component-identification}
\qffield{Statement} The transaction-cost component makes prices bounce between bid and ask, so its price changes reverse; the adverse-selection component moves the dealers' value estimates, so its price changes are permanent and unpredictable; both depend on whether a buyer or a seller initiated the trade, and the usual decomposition explains current and future price changes with the signs of trades \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', pp.~299--300 and~302--303]{alpha-harris}. In Hasbrouck's generalized Roll model the efficient price moves with the trade and with public news, and trades occur at a cost $c$ either side of it \citep[sec.~8.2, eq.~(8.1), pp.~67--68]{s6-hasbrouck}:
\[ m_t=m_{t-1}+\lambda q_t+u_t, \qquad p_t=m_t+cq_t, \qquad \text{ask}-\text{bid}=2(c+\lambda), \]
\[ \gamma_0=c^2+(c+\lambda)^2+\sigma_u^2, \qquad \gamma_1=-c(c+\lambda), \]
with all higher autocovariances zero \citep[sec.~8.3, eq.~(8.3), p.~68]{s6-hasbrouck}. Two moments cannot identify the three parameters $\lambda$, $c$, $\sigma_u^2$: trade prices alone do not separate the components. Only the variance of efficient-price changes, $\lambda^2+\sigma_u^2=\gamma_0+2\gamma_1$, is identified without restrictions \citep[same sec., pp.~68--69]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Notation} $q_t=\pm1$ is the trade sign; $\lambda$ is the adverse-selection (information) cost and $c$ the noninformational cost, both per side; $u_t$ is nontrade public information, uncorrelated with $q_t$; $\gamma_0,\gamma_1$ are the variance and first autocovariance of $\Delta p_t$. In Harris's terms the adverse-selection component is $2\lambda$ and the transaction-cost component $2c$ (Interpretation).

\qffield{Conditions} Timing: after the trade at $t-1$ the efficient price is $m_{t-1}$; public news $u_t$ arrives; dealers set bid and ask symmetrically about $m_{t-1}+u_t$; the trade $q_t$ arrives and the efficient price becomes $m_t$ \citep[sec.~8.2, p.~68]{s6-hasbrouck}. Setting $\lambda=0$ gives Roll's model; setting $\sigma_u^2=0$ or $c=0$ identifies the rest, but such restrictions are economically strong \citep[sec.~8.3, p.~69]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Practice} With trade signs, analyses indicate that in most markets the adverse-selection component is larger than the transaction-cost component \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', p.~303]{alpha-harris}. Inventory control predicts the same short-run quote response as adverse selection: after a customer buys, the dealer's quotes rise either way. The two separate in the long run, since information effects are permanent while inventory effects vanish once offsetting trades restore the dealer's position; for NYSE specialists, most quote dynamics are attributable to trades, with inventories adding little explanatory power \citep[sec.~11.4, p.~115]{s6-hasbrouck}. Identification therefore needs more than prints: trade signs and subsequent quote revisions and, to separate inventory, dealer positions.

\qffield{Pitfalls} An estimated permanent component includes any inventory-driven quote change that has not reversed within the horizon studied, and the transitory component includes any mean reversion from uninformed order imbalances (Interpretation). Where the dealer is counterparty to every one-unit trade, $q_t=-\Delta I_t$ for inventory $I_t$ \citep[sec.~11.3.2, p.~112]{s6-hasbrouck}, and a regression on current and lagged trades and inventories is then collinear; the NYSE data avoid this because the specialist does not take part in every trade \citep[sec.~11.4, p.~115]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\begin{qfresult}[Determinants of bid--ask spreads: primary factors, order-driven equilibrium and observable proxies]\label{res:spread-determinants}
\qffield{Statement} Three primary factors set spreads \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', sec.~14.6.1, pp.~311--313]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{asymmetric information} widens them, most when informed traders know material information;
\item \emph{volatility} widens them: it raises the value of the timing option that standing limit orders and dealer quotes give away when they cannot be adjusted at once, makes diversifiable inventory risk costly to risk-averse dealers, and proxies for asymmetric information;
\item \emph{utilitarian trading interest} narrows them: active markets spread dealers' fixed costs over more trades, make inventory easier to lay off, draw public limit orders, and dilute informed flow.
\end{itemize}
In a continuous order-driven market the equilibrium spread makes the marginal trader indifferent between limit and market orders; it would be zero if traders were identical and equally informed, could cancel instantly and free, and cared neither for time nor risk \citep[same chap., secs.~14.4.1--14.4.2, pp.~304--310]{alpha-harris}. Relaxing those assumptions gives Harris's Table~14-2 \citep[same chap., p.~311]{alpha-harris}: information asymmetry (increases spreads; great importance), time to cancel limit orders (increases; high), volatility (increases; high), limit-order management costs (increases; moderate), the value of traders' time (increases; moderate), the commission difference between limit and market orders (one for one), and risk aversion (increases; low).

\qffield{Conditions} With costly limit-order management, the equilibrium spread equals the expected cost of managing limit orders plus twice the value of the timing option \citep[same chap., p.~307]{alpha-harris}; with only a commission difference, it equals that difference \citep[same chap., p.~306]{alpha-harris}. If all traders were equally uninformed, uncertainty would narrow spreads, because value estimates are less volatile than values; uncertainty widens spreads through asymmetry \citep[same chap., p.~310]{alpha-harris}. The primary factors interact: asymmetry widens spreads, which deters uninformed traders and lowers volume, which widens them further \citep[same chap., p.~312]{alpha-harris}. Public limit-order traders without dealers' costs can undercut dealers, who survive by exploiting their view of the order flow \citep[same chap., sec.~14.5, pp.~310--311]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Observable \emph{secondary} determinants stand in for the primary ones \citep[same chap., sec.~14.6.2, pp.~314--317]{alpha-harris}. For asymmetric information: disclosure rules, market-condition reports, analyst and press coverage, economy-wide commodity contracts, diversified portfolios and diversified firms, established rather than emerging industries, firm age, enforced insider-trading rules, and expected material news such as earnings. For volatility: financial and operating leverage and uncertain growth for stocks; perishability, storage costs, low inventories and weather for commodities; political, inflation and interest-rate uncertainty for currencies; inflation, rates and credit quality for bonds. For utilitarian interest: trading activity (volume, number of trades), firm size, debt issue size and on-the-run status, and contracts that replicate risks many traders want to hedge. When asymmetric information is severe or utilitarian interest small, spreads can be so wide that the market fails; small firms then finance through banks and venture capitalists, whose due diligence, restrictions and supervision rights need a private relationship \citep[same chap., sec.~14.6.3, p.~317]{alpha-harris}. Empirically, daily spreads rise with daily volatility, the spread rewarding market makers for the risk they bear, and across small-tick instruments spreads follow $\psi_i=a+b\,\sigma_i/\sqrt{N_i}$, with $N_i$ the daily number of trades \citep[chap.~2, secs.~2.2 and~2.3.1, pp.~144--145 and~150--151]{alpha-laruelle}; see \hyperref[meth:spread-volatility-regression]{spread--volatility regression}. When the spread is stuck at one tick, liquidity providers respond by changing size at the best quotes rather than the spread \citep[same chap., sec.~2.2, p.~148]{alpha-laruelle}. In crashes liquidity suppliers withdraw: in 1987 buyers waited until they believed all sellers were satisfied, and some Nasdaq dealers took their phones off the hook \citep[chap.~``Bubbles, Crashes, and Circuit Breakers'', pp.~561 and~563]{alpha-harris}.

For large-tick instruments, Lehalle and Laruelle add a bounce term inside the same scaling by trading activity, $\psi_i=a+(b_1\sigma_i+b_2\,\eta\cdot\mathrm{tick})/\sqrt{N_i}$, as in \hyperref[meth:spread-volatility-regression]{the spread--volatility regression} (eq.~(2.1), page image of p.~145). Here $\eta=N_c/(2N_a)$ is half the ratio of price continuations to alternations in traded prices: the more bid--ask bounce, the more alternations and the smaller $\eta$, which is one half when moves are random \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.2, eq.~(2.1), pp.~144--145]{alpha-laruelle}. When the spread is stuck at one tick, liquidity providers who cannot step inside it increase the size at the best bid and offer, so a larger book plays the role of a narrower spread (p.~148). Interpretation: the tick is the constraint; bounce and book size are observed outcomes; matching rules (time priority or pro-rata allocation) govern who is filled within the larger queue (\hyperref[conv:cme-globex-trading]{CME Globex trading conventions}). The legacy principle \emph{Large Tick Futures} (slice 19) stated that futures ``often'' have economically large ticks, with bounce, reversals and larger displayed quantities ``relative to comparable equities''. The mechanism matches these passages, which are not specific to futures; the claim about futures ticks and the comparison with equities were not checked.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Secondary factors are correlated with more than one primary factor and with each other, so an observed effect can have the opposite sign to the prediction (emerging industries attract volume), and tests need controls \citep[same chap., p.~314]{alpha-harris}. Volatility has a second, opposite effect through gambling and hedging interest, which raise activity \citep[same chap., pp.~316--317]{alpha-harris}. Trading activity is the best observable predictor of spreads, but Harris keeps it secondary to preserve the distinction between theoretical and observable determinants \citep[same chap., p.~312]{alpha-harris}. These are qualitative predictions; the importance ratings in Table~14-2 are Harris's judgement, not estimates. The legacy notes' ``availability of close risk substitutes'' is not Harris's wording: his factor is risk replication for hedgers. A legacy principle, \emph{volatility is a liquidity price}, listed smaller displayed size and faster cancellations among liquidity providers' responses to volatility. The passages checked support wider spreads and, in crashes, withdrawal. Lehalle and Laruelle report book size moving inversely to the spread over the day \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.2, p.~148]{alpha-laruelle}; that is co-movement, not a measured response to volatility. The legacy mechanism \emph{Bid-Ask Bounce} and principle \emph{Spread and Depth Substitute Imperfectly}, merged in slice~35, restate the statistic $\eta$ and the book-size compensation above; their readings, that a low $\eta$ means more of the observed movement is microstructure noise, and that a larger book replaces a narrower spread only for orders small enough to fit inside it, are interpretations consistent with Lehalle and Laruelle (pp.~145 and~148), not statements of theirs.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Outside spread: the prices at which value traders will trade]\label{def:outside-spread}
\qffield{Statement} A value trader's \emph{outside spread} is the interval between the prices at which he is willing to buy and to sell; value traders rarely quote these prices, so as not to reveal their value estimates or give free trading options \citep[chap.~``Value Traders'', sec.~16.2, p.~340]{alpha-harris}. Writing $\widehat V$ for his value estimate, he buys only below $b^o$ and sells only above $a^o$, and $s^o=a^o-b^o$. Interpretation: if every cost and risk of a one-unit trade could be summarised in an all-in half-cost $c>0$, the expected surplus would be $\widehat V-P-c$ from buying and $P-\widehat V-c$ from selling. He would trade only outside the no-trade band $[\widehat V-c,\widehat V+c]$, so $b^o=\widehat V-c$ and $a^o=\widehat V+c$. The spread widens with \citep[same chap., pp.~340--344]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item adverse selection from news traders who know of value changes he has not yet learnt;
\item the winner's curse: the risk that his estimate is wrong, larger when values are hard to estimate and when many others estimate them, with losses from the market impact of trading on a wrong estimate;
\item the costs of research, which must be recovered also on the instruments analysed that offer no trade.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Value trading is profitable only when price differs from fundamental value, which happens in two ways. New information changes the value: news traders, by definition the first to learn it, profit and push the price to the new value. Or uninformed traders push the price away from value: value traders profit and push it back. Value traders therefore profit only by trading against uninformed traders' demands for liquidity \citep[same chap., sec.~16.1, pp.~338--339]{alpha-harris}. They do so directly, through limit orders or blocks, or indirectly through dealers whose inventories the uninformed demand has unbalanced \citep[same chap., secs.~16.1.1--16.1.2, pp.~339--340]{alpha-harris}. Outside spreads are much wider than dealer spreads: value traders trade when order flow is one-sided and cannot learn their mistakes from it, take larger positions held until prices revert, so bear more inventory risk and financing cost, trade less volume over which to spread fixed costs, and earn at best half the outside spread when price returns to value, while a dealer often earns the whole spread on a round trip \citep[same chap., sec.~16.3, pp.~344--345]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Value traders buy when dealers lower their quotes to shed inventory taken from uninformed sellers, and so supply depth to the market and resiliency against uninformed price moves; dealers take larger positions where value traders follow the market closely \citep[same chap., pp.~340, 345--346]{alpha-harris}. When their value estimates differ enough, value traders' outside spreads overlap and they trade with each other \citep[same chap., p.~343]{alpha-harris}. Not buying does not oblige a trader to sell: uncertain estimates mean he should speculate only when he believes an instrument is very mispriced, and the larger the apparent mispricing, the more likely it is a mistake \citep[same chap., box, p.~343]{alpha-harris}. In continuous markets the winner's curse bites because traders trade at the prices they quote, and the market aggregates estimates sequentially; in a two-sided single-price auction it matters little \citep[same chap., pp.~342--343]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The outside spread is not observed in quotes or trades (Interpretation: it is inferred from where value traders act). The legacy notes listed leverage and borrowing among its financing costs; Harris names the cost of financing larger positions, without that detail \citep[same chap., p.~344]{alpha-harris}. A single half-cost $c$ understates the problem: adverse selection and the winner's curse are not per-unit costs but depend on what a trade at a given price reveals and on how many others are estimating the value, which is why Harris treats them as risks that widen the spread (Interpretation). The legacy notes gave value traders three sources of profit: information changes, uninformed liquidity demand and dealer inventory pressure. In Harris, information changes are where news traders profit; value traders gain there only by correcting news traders' under- or overreaction \citep[chap.~``Informed Traders and Market Efficiency'', Table~10-1, p.~230, and p.~239]{alpha-harris}. Dealer inventory pressure is the indirect channel of uninformed demand \citep[chap.~``Value Traders'', pp.~339--340]{alpha-harris}.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Fundamental and transitory volatility: the random-walk and transitory components of prices]\label{def:volatility-components}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Volatility} is the tendency of prices to change unexpectedly. \emph{Fundamental volatility} comes from unanticipated changes in instrument values; \emph{transitory volatility} comes from trading by impatient uninformed traders, which pushes prices away from values until they revert. Total volatility is the sum of the two \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', pp.~410, 413--414]{alpha-harris}. In statistical form, the trade price is a random-walk efficient price plus a stationary pricing error \citep[sec.~8.6, p.~72]{s6-hasbrouck}:
\[ p_t=m_t+s_t, \qquad m_t=m_{t-1}+w_t, \qquad \Delta p_t=w_t+\Delta s_t, \]
where $s_t$ has mean zero, is covariance-stationary, and may be serially correlated and correlated with $w_t$. Fundamental volatility is the variance $\sigma_w^2$ of $w_t$. If the price changes have the moving-average representation $\Delta p_t=\theta(L)\varepsilon_t$, then $\sigma_w^2=\theta(1)^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, the same for every structural model consistent with the data, while the variance of the pricing error has only a lower bound \citep[same sec., eqs.~(8.10)--(8.13), pp.~73--74]{s6-hasbrouck}. Over long horizons $\sigma_w^2\approx\operatorname{Var}(p_t-p_{t-k})/k$, because microstructure effects matter less as the interval grows \citep[sec.~8.3, p.~69]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Notation} $p_t$ is the trade price, $m_t$ the efficient (fundamental) price, $s_t=p_t-m_t$ the pricing error, $w_t$ the efficient-price innovation; $\theta(1)$ is the sum of the moving-average coefficients. Harris writes $V_t$ for value and $\varepsilon_t$ for its change. The legacy notes wrote $P_t=M_t+u_t$; Hasbrouck's $u_t$ is public news in the generalized Roll model, so this entry uses $s_t$.

\qffield{Conditions} Fundamental volatility comes only from \emph{unexpected} changes in the factors that set values. Predictable price changes that pay holders for carrying costs and risk, such as a zero-coupon bond's creep towards par, are not volatility; an unexpected change in rates is \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', pp.~411--412]{alpha-harris}. When news is common knowledge prices can change without trading; when few know it, prices move on high volume as the informed trade; and the price revisions that make up the adverse-selection spread component contribute to fundamental volatility \citep[same chap., p.~411]{alpha-harris}. Transitory volatility includes bid--ask bounce, produced by the transaction-cost (transitory) spread component, and the price effects of large orders and cumulative uninformed imbalances that reverse when value traders and arbitrageurs act; it includes both the price change and its reversal. Value traders, arbitrageurs and dealers do not cause it but resolve it \citep[same chap., pp.~413--414]{alpha-harris}. Roll's model is the special case $s_t=\tfrac12SQ_t$ with trade signs independent of $w_t$, which gives the two-term variance $\sigma^2+\tfrac12S^2$ (see \hyperref[res:roll-spread-estimator]{Roll's estimator}).

\qffield{Practice} Fundamental factors differ by instrument: supply and demand, interest rates and storage costs for commodities; rates and credit quality for bonds; management, resources and technologies, product and input markets and rates for stocks; inflation, macroeconomic policy and trade and capital flows for currencies. Volatility is higher for commodities that are costly to store or perishable, especially with long production pipelines, variable demand or inelastic demand; for firms whose value depends on unresolved uncertainties, such as research outcomes or the growth behind high price--earnings ratios; for instruments exposed to political risk; and for the equity of highly leveraged firms \citep[same chap., pp.~411--413]{alpha-harris}. Because transitory price changes revert, negative serial correlation of price changes is a strong indicator of transitory volatility: over successive trades for bid--ask bounce, and over minutes to months for uninformed order imbalances \citep[same chap., p.~414]{alpha-harris}. A variance ratio $V_{M,N}=[\operatorname{Var}(p_t-p_{t-M})/M]/[\operatorname{Var}(p_t-p_{t-N})/N]$ equals one for a random walk; when microstructure effects dominate short horizons, $V_{M,N}>1$ for $M<N$ \citep[sec.~8.7, eq.~(8.14), pp.~74--75]{s6-hasbrouck}. Transitory volatility moves with uninformed traders' transaction costs and is small in liquid markets \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', p.~414]{alpha-harris}. Regulators can have no lasting effect on fundamental volatility but can change transitory volatility, in either direction \citep[same chap., p.~410]{alpha-harris}. Circuit breakers slow fundamental price changes; their net effect on transitory volatility is indeterminate, lowering it when they restrain uninformed traders, order anticipators and bluffers and raising it when they restrain informed traders, dealers and arbitrageurs, and empirical studies have been inconclusive. Margins and transaction taxes cannot discriminate between these trader types \citep[chap.~``Bubbles, Crashes, and Circuit Breakers'', secs.~28.3.2--28.3.4, pp.~575--577]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Negative serial correlation is not proof of transitory volatility: successive spot prices of a perishable commodity are prices of different items, for different delivery dates, so their negative correlation can be fundamental \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', box, p.~413]{alpha-harris}. Its absence is not proof of a random walk either: lagged price adjustment makes price changes positively autocorrelated \citep[sec.~8.6, Exercise~8.2, p.~74]{s6-hasbrouck}, and negative autocorrelation at some lags can offset positive autocorrelation at others to give a variance ratio near one \citep[sec.~8.7, p.~75]{s6-hasbrouck}. Roll's variance components understate transitory volatility when reversals take longer than one trade \citep[chap.~``Volatility'', p.~416]{alpha-harris}. The split of $\operatorname{Var}(\Delta p_t)=\sigma_w^2+\operatorname{Var}(\Delta s_t)+2\operatorname{Cov}(w_t,\Delta s_t)$ beyond $\sigma_w^2$ is not identified; assigning the covariance term to transitory volatility, as the legacy notes did, is a convention, and Harris's ``sum of the two'' holds when the components are uncorrelated (Interpretation). The legacy notes also listed \emph{episodic} and \emph{continuing} volatility as further sources. Harris uses episodic volatility for episodes of large price changes in short intervals \citep[same chap., p.~410]{alpha-harris}, a description rather than a third source; continuing volatility does not appear in the passages checked.

\begin{qfmethod}[Garman--Klass volatility from open, high, low and close prices]\label{meth:garman-klass-volatility}
\qffield{Statement} Lehalle and Laruelle estimate intraday volatility over a fixed interval from its open, high, low and close prices $p^O,p^H,p^L,p^C$ and its average price $\bar p$ \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.5.1, p.~172]{alpha-laruelle}:
\[ \mathrm{Vol}_{GK}=\frac{\sqrt{\tfrac12\left(p^H-p^L\right)^2-(2\log2-1)\left(p^C-p^O\right)^2}}{\bar p}. \]
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose the interval. Lehalle and Laruelle use 5 minutes to show intraday seasonality and 15 minutes to compare markets \citep[same chap., secs.~2.2 and~2.5.1, pp.~146 and~172]{alpha-laruelle}.
\item Record the open, high, low and close of each interval and apply the formula.
\item Average across intervals or days; the comparison of FTSE~100 and IBEX~35 components used a 20-day moving average of daily averages \citep[same chap., sec.~2.5.1, p.~172]{alpha-laruelle}.
\end{enumerate}

\qffield{Notation} The superscripts $O,H,L,C$ avoid a clash with the transaction-cost component $C$. The legacy notes used log ratios, $\sigma_{GK}=\sqrt{\tfrac12(\log H/L)^2-(2\log2-1)(\log C/O)^2}$; since $\log(p^H/p^L)\approx(p^H-p^L)/\bar p$ for small ranges, the two forms agree to first order (Interpretation).

\qffield{Conditions} Volatility is not observed: each estimate presumes a model. Under an arithmetic Brownian price the natural estimator sums squared trade-to-trade changes, but on high-frequency data that estimator is polluted by bid--ask bounce, and with jumps they must be removed first. Garman--Klass is a measure not polluted too much by the bounce. Implied volatility measures something else, the uncertainty priced into options \citep[same chap., sec.~2.2, pp.~146--147]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Practice} Lehalle and Laruelle use it for the intraday seasonality of volatility, with volume, spread and book size, on Royal Dutch Shell, 2011--2016 (Figure~2.12, pp.~146--148), and to compare the intraday volatility of FTSE~100 and IBEX~35 components after MiFID, finding no significant effect of the UK's fragmentation on intraday volatility \citep[same chap., sec.~2.5.1, pp.~172--174]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The coefficient $2\log2-1$ and the estimator's properties come from Garman and Klass (1980), which was not consulted; Lehalle and Laruelle give the formula without derivation or assumptions. The estimate is per interval and relative to price; scaling it to a daily or annual figure needs an assumption about how variance adds across intervals (Interpretation). When the high and low are trade prices they include part of the spread, so on short intervals the range still carries some bounce (Interpretation).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:volatility-components]{fundamental and transitory volatility} (what the total contains); \hyperref[meth:spread-volatility-regression]{spread--volatility regression} (a use of interval volatilities); \hyperref[res:roll-spread-estimator]{Roll's estimator} (the bounce that trade-based estimators pick up); \hyperref[def:intraday-overnight-returns]{returns by time scale} (open-to-close and overnight returns).

\qffield{Further reading} The original estimator \citep{garman-klass-1980}, not consulted. The legacy block, verbatim: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/derivations.tex}.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Intraday, overnight and close-to-close returns: volatility and correlation by time scale]\label{def:intraday-overnight-returns}
\qffield{Statement} For day $t$ with opening price $p^O_t$ and closing price $p^C_t$, Lehalle and Laruelle define the \emph{intraday} and \emph{overnight} returns \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.5.1, eqs.~(2.2)--(2.3), p.~175]{alpha-laruelle}:
\[ r^{\mathrm{intra}}_t=\log p^C_t-\log p^O_t, \qquad r^{\mathrm{on}}_t=\log p^O_t-\log p^C_{t-1}, \]
and compute the correlation between two stocks separately on each series, over a six-month sliding window, averaging the pairwise correlations across an index's components \citep[same sec., pp.~174--175]{alpha-laruelle}. Interpretation: the close-to-close (\emph{extraday}) return is exactly their sum, $r^{\mathrm{cc}}_t=r^{\mathrm{on}}_t+r^{\mathrm{intra}}_t$. Volatility likewise has an intraday and an extraday scale: investors with extraday constraints bear extraday volatility, and intraday volatility only through its effect on that scale \citep[same chap., sec.~2.5, p.~169]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Notation} Superscripts $O$ and $C$ for open and close, as in \hyperref[meth:garman-klass-volatility]{Garman--Klass}, avoid a clash with the transaction-cost component $C$.

\qffield{Conditions} The two returns come from different market mechanisms: overnight the market is organised as an auction fixing, during the day as a continuous double auction. The authors hold that the opening fixing is less contaminated by microstructure noise than the closing fixing, and so better reflects investors' consensus on the price \citep[same chap., sec.~2.5.1, p.~177]{alpha-laruelle}. Interpretation: $\operatorname{Var}(r^{\mathrm{cc}})=\operatorname{Var}(r^{\mathrm{on}})+\operatorname{Var}(r^{\mathrm{intra}})+2\operatorname{Cov}(r^{\mathrm{on}},r^{\mathrm{intra}})$, so extraday risk mixes the two scales and their covariance.

\qffield{Practice} On the components of the CLES~60, DAX, CAC~40 and IBEX~35, 1991--2012, correlation rises mainly on the overnight side, and overnight correlation explains well the spikes in extraday correlation, to which it is closer; intraday correlation acts as a baseline that overnight correlation keeps bouncing above \citep[same sec., Figures~2.27--2.28, pp.~174--177]{alpha-laruelle}. Intraday correlation spiked in summer 2010 and in August 2011, and for three of the indices was in October 2010 at its highest level since 1991 \citep[same sec., pp.~174 and~176]{alpha-laruelle}. The authors read intraday correlation as an intermediate marker in a contagion process by which microscopic causes have macroscopic effects \citep[same sec., p.~174]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The authors attribute part of the March--October 2010 rise in extraday correlation to rising intraday correlation, the consequence of a less noisy PFP, and offer growing high-frequency activity as one possible explanation \citep[same sec., p.~177]{alpha-laruelle}: a conjecture, not a test. The legacy notes said daily correlation spikes come from different mechanisms at different horizons; the authors find the spikes mainly overnight. The legacy notes tied overnight correlation to auction and news revaluation and overnight volatility to information arrival. The auction is in the source; news arriving overnight is not stated in the passages checked (Interpretation). A legacy principle said intraday frictions matter for daily risk when they change the closing or auction price or the path used by benchmark investors. The source asks whether changes in intraday price formation show at the extraday scale \citep[same chap., sec.~2.5, p.~169]{alpha-laruelle}, and compares the noise in the two fixings (p.~177); benchmark paths are not discussed there. Interpretation: the returns depend on which prices are used, auction prints or last trades, and the open and close of a fragmented market may differ by venue.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:garman-klass-volatility]{Garman--Klass volatility} (volatility within intraday intervals; the FTSE~100 and IBEX~35 comparison of the same section); \hyperref[def:volatility-components]{fundamental and transitory volatility} (variance across horizons); \hyperref[def:price-formation-process]{price formation process}. The legacy Flash Crash and systemic-risk blocks of the same section of the source are assigned to \texttt{hft-market-design} and \texttt{market-structure-execution-quality}.

\qffield{Further reading} The legacy blocks it replaces, verbatim: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/derivations.tex}.

\begin{qfmethod}[Spread--volatility regression in levels and logs]\label{meth:spread-volatility-regression}
\qffield{Statement} To test how the spread moves with volatility, Lehalle and Laruelle proceed in five steps \citep[app.~A, sec.~A.10, pp.~292--298]{alpha-laruelle}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the spread $\psi_i$ and the volatility $\sigma_i$ over the same intervals; their example uses 15-minute intervals of Rio Tinto from January 2012.
\item Fit $\psi_i=a_0+a_1\sigma_i+\epsilon_i$ by ordinary least squares: $\hat a_1=\sum_i(\sigma_i-\bar\sigma)(\psi_i-\bar\psi)/\sum_i(\sigma_i-\bar\sigma)^2$, $\hat a_0=\bar\psi-\hat a_1\bar\sigma$.
\item Inspect the distributions: both variables looked log-normal.
\item Fit $\log\psi_i=a_0+a_1\log\sigma_i+\epsilon_i$, which in levels is the multiplicative model $\psi=A_0\sigma^{a_1}e^{\epsilon}$, $A_0=e^{a_0}$.
\item If $\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_\epsilon^2)$, then $\mathbb{E}[\psi]=A_0\sigma^{a_1}e^{\sigma_\epsilon^2/2}$, so predict levels with $\hat\psi=\hat A_0\sigma^{\hat a_1}e^{\hat\sigma_\epsilon^2/2}$, which they call the unbiased predictor.
\end{enumerate}
A positive $\hat a_1$ is consistent with the spread rewarding market makers for the risk they take: the higher the volatility, the more they ask \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.3.1, p.~150]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Notation} $\psi$ is the spread, $\sigma$ the volatility over the interval, $a_0,a_1$ the intercept and slope, $\sigma_\epsilon^2$ the residual variance. $A_0$ is the level intercept, not the adverse-selection component $A$.

\qffield{Conditions} The least-squares assumptions are those of the simple linear model: the regressor measured without error; errors with mean zero, constant variance and no correlation with each other or with the regressor; normal errors for the $t$ tests \citep[app.~A, sec.~A.10.1, pp.~293--295]{alpha-laruelle}. Across small-tick instruments, Lehalle and Laruelle report the relation $\psi_i=a+b\,\sigma_i/\sqrt{N_i}$, with $N_i$ the average number of trades per day and $a,b$ shared within an asset class; for large-tick instruments, $\psi_i=a+(b_1\sigma_i+b_2\,\eta\,\mathrm{tick})/\sqrt{N_i}$, where $\eta=N_c/(2N_a)$ is half the ratio of price continuations to alternations, smaller the more the price bounces \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.2, pp.~144--145]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Practice} For Rio Tinto, the level fit gave $\hat a_0=2.4894$, $\hat a_1=0.0485$, $R^2=0.2404$; the log fit $\hat a_0=0.5609$, $\hat a_1=0.2252$, $R^2=0.1553$; the back-transformed predictor, with $\hat\sigma_\epsilon=0.2509$, explains 0.1097 of the variance of the spread in levels \citep[app.~A, Figures~A.14, A.17 and~A.18, pp.~296--298]{alpha-laruelle}. Daily spreads rise with daily volatility on BNP Paribas and BP, 2008--2012; points off the line in August 2011 coincide with the short-selling ban, when market makers could not meet their commitments in financial stocks \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.3.1, Figure~2.13, pp.~150--151]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The fit is descriptive: in the example volatility explains a quarter or less of the spread's variance. Unless the comparison controls for them, other variables drive both series. Tick size: when the spread is stuck at one tick, liquidity providers add size at the best quotes instead, so in large-tick assets volatility may show in depth rather than in the spread \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.2, p.~148]{alpha-laruelle}. Time of day: spread, volatility, volume and book size share an intraday seasonality, high after the open, so pooling intervals across the day mixes the seasonal pattern with the relation \citep[same sec., pp.~145--148]{alpha-laruelle}. Trading activity: the cross-sectional relation scales volatility by $1/\sqrt{N_i}$ \citep[same sec., pp.~144--145]{alpha-laruelle}. Harris's general warning applies: observable determinants are correlated with each other and with more than one primary factor, so tests need controls \citep[chap.~``Bid/Ask Spreads'', p.~314]{alpha-harris}. The legacy notes also listed venue and depth as controls; the passages checked do not discuss them as regression controls. Causation runs both ways, since a wider spread adds bounce to trade-based volatility (Interpretation). The predictor is unbiased only with known $a_0$, $a_1$ and $\sigma_\epsilon^2$; with estimates plugged in it is approximately unbiased (Interpretation). The legacy mechanism \emph{Spread-Volatility Link}, merged in slice~35, restates this entry: market makers widen spreads when volatility raises adverse-selection and inventory risk, and the relation is cleaner when volatility is scaled by trading activity and the tick is accounted for; Lehalle and Laruelle summarise that ``High volume combined with low volatility and a tight bid--ask spread characterize a liquid stock'' \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.3, p.~149]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants} (the theory being tested: volatility as a primary determinant); \hyperref[meth:garman-klass-volatility]{Garman--Klass volatility} (an interval volatility); \hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures} (which spread to regress).

\begin{qfmechanism}[Bubbles and crashes: how mispricing builds and unwinds]\label{mech:bubble-crash-dynamics}
\qffield{Statement} A \emph{bubble} is a rise of prices substantially above fundamental values, quickly or over a long period; a \emph{crash} is a very fast fall, often but not always after a bubble. Since fundamental values are not common knowledge, bubbles and crashes can occur at all \citep[chap.~``Bubbles, Crashes, and Circuit Breakers'', sec.~28.1, p.~556]{alpha-harris}. The typical sequence \citep[same chap., sec.~28.1.1, pp.~556--557]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Buyers grow overly optimistic about new technologies or markets, often without recognising what prices already reflect, and push prices up.
\item Momentum traders and order anticipators buy and accelerate the rise; early buyers grow more confident and early sellers buy back to stop their losses.
\item Value traders and arbitrageurs cannot sell enough (no position to sell, too little capital to carry a short, short selling hard) or will not (they expect uninformed buying to continue, or doubt their own value estimates).
\item Sellers start trading aggressively: long-term holders, early buyers taking gains, contrarians, value traders, arbitrageurs. Momentum buyers lose interest and optimists lose confidence.
\item Late buyers sell to stop losses, margin calls and stop orders force more selling, and order anticipators sell ahead of them: prices fall quickly. Panic can take prices below value, and a bounce follows.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{The price accelerator} (Harris, after a manuscript by Treynor): rising prices move wealth from pessimists with short positions to optimists with long ones; the richer optimists may buy more, and the losing pessimists may buy to cover margin calls, so prices rise further; the sellers are mild optimists and mild pessimists \citep[same chap., box, p.~556]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} Most bubbles and crashes involve both fundamental and transitory volatility. A bubble usually starts on good news, a fundamental change; pseudo-informed traders then overreact, the transitory part. It usually bursts on bad news, but the volume and price response is out of proportion to the news because of the overpricing that preceded it; the reversion towards value counts as transitory volatility, since it occurs only because uninformed traders inflated prices, and panic below value adds more \citep[same chap., sec.~28.1.2, pp.~557--558]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} Harris's 1987 account shows the mechanisms together: portfolio insurance traded like stop orders, and uncertainty about how much of it was waiting kept buyers out; NYSE order printers could not keep up, so traders did not know whether their orders had filled; index futures led the cash market down and traded at a discount of more than 10 percent once index arbitrageurs stopped; and buy-side traders could not reach Nasdaq dealers \citep[same chap., sec.~28.2.2, pp.~559--563]{alpha-harris}. Crashes rarely have a single cause: traders become confused about risk, attend to the wrong issues and, when events move fast, panic \citep[same chap., sec.~28.2.8, p.~571]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} A large fall without news of commensurate importance does not show that the market failed. Most crashes correct earlier pricing errors; if they were failures, prices would rebound fully once traders saw them below value, but after most crashes prices rebound little \citep[same chap., box, p.~559]{alpha-harris}. Attribution therefore looks at the reversal after the event, at what preceded it, and at overloaded systems, forced selling and broken links between markets, as in 1987 (Interpretation, from the cases on pp.~557--563). Bubbles are hard to identify in real time, and analysts generally understand the causes of extreme volatility better after the fact \citep[same chap., p.~555]{alpha-harris}. The legacy list named policy among the stories that start a bubble; Harris's general account names technology and new markets, and his Japanese example turns on loose monetary policy \citep[same chap., sec.~28.2.7, pp.~570--571]{alpha-harris}.
