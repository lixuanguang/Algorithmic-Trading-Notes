\section{Markets, traders and venues}

\begin{qfdefinition}[What markets do: liquidity, price discovery, risk transfer and capital allocation; private and public benefits; why the mechanism matters]\label{def:market-functions}
\qffield{Statement} Harris organises the study of trading around five characteristics of market quality: liquidity, transaction costs, informative prices, volatility and trading profits, and the secondary question of how market structure, ``trading rules and information systems'', affects each \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.2, p.~4]{alpha-harris}. What markets produce falls into private benefits, which accrue to traders when they trade (they trade voluntarily, so they expect to gain), and public benefits, positive externalities that reach people who do not trade, in two classes: those from informative prices and those from liquid markets \citep[chap.~9, secs.~9.1.1--9.3, pp.~205--206]{alpha-harris}. These functions can be organised as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{liquidity}: the buy side buys exchange services, of which liquidity, ``the ability to trade when you want to trade'', is the most important, and the sell side sells it \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.1, p.~32]{alpha-harris}; patient traders get better prices, and impatient traders ``pay for the privilege of trading when they want to trade'' (chap.~1, p.~5); the dimensions are in \hyperref[def:liquidity-dimensions]{liquidity and market quality};
\item \emph{price discovery}: markets ``aggregate information from many different sources to produce prices that are closer to fundamental values than anyone could consistently estimate by himself or herself'' (chap.~9, sec.~9.4, p.~206); how informed traders do this is \hyperref[res:informed-trading-aggregation]{informed trading and aggregation};
\item \emph{risk transfer}: hedgers trade to exchange risks (chap.~8, sec.~8.1.3, pp.~182--188); liquid hedging and insurance markets let producers specialise, and risk sharing through public corporations lets large projects be financed, which lowers costs of capital and prices even for those who never trade (chap.~9, secs.~9.5.2--9.5.3, pp.~214--215);
\item \emph{capital allocation}: informative primary-market prices decide which new projects receive capital, and informative secondary-market prices help investors choose, pay and replace managers of existing capital (chap.~9, secs.~9.4.2--9.4.3, pp.~211--212); when prices do not reflect how well managers perform, good managers may be dismissed and poor ones kept (p.~211).
\end{itemize}
\emph{The mechanism matters.} Hasbrouck: ``The market-clearing price, at least at it arises in usual Walrasian tatonnement, rarely appears in microstructure analyses''; at one instant there are many prices, by direction, urgency, identity and relationship to the counterparty \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.1.3, p.~4]{s6-hasbrouck}. Harris: market structure ``determines what people can know and do in a market'' and so the power relations among traders \citep[chap.~5, p.~89]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} The list of functions is a classification, not a theorem; Harris adds that markets work well only when negotiation costs are small relative to the value traded, so economies mix markets with firms, which are small command economies (chap.~9, p.~209). Which benefit dominates is an empirical matter for each market. The welfare ranking of these functions is Harris's opinion, which he labels as such: public policy should first serve utilitarian traders, then the public benefits of liquid, informative markets, and support profit-motivated traders only as far as they serve those (sec.~9.6, pp.~216--217); see \hyperref[def:market-regulation-design]{regulation as market design}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Trade-offs.} Objectives conflict: rules that restrict insider trading ``usually increase liquidity while making prices less informative'' (chap.~9, p.~218); and the transparency that improves price formation is what each trader wants to avoid revealing (Lehalle and Laruelle, quoted in \hyperref[def:market-structures-venues]{market structures and venues}). \emph{Visible activity against economic importance.} Harris's own estimates for the United States (Table~3-4, ``Author's estimates'', c.\ 2000) put common stocks at about 20 percent of capital wealth and real estate at about 50 percent; derivatives represent no wealth because they are in zero net supply; ``Stocks represent less wealth than the widespread attention given to them by the media would suggest'' \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.4.1, p.~45]{alpha-harris}. The figures are dated rough estimates; the point is that trading volume and attention are not measures of a market's economic weight.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Immediacy has a price: the spread compensates traders who absorb inventory, processing and adverse-selection risk; the components and their identification are \hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants} and \hyperref[res:spread-component-identification]{spread components}, and funding risk is an addition to Harris's list. That liquid markets ``lower financing costs'' is Harris's argument through investors' required returns (p.~215), not a measured effect. Harris's claim that liquidity benefits even non-traders through cheaper goods is an argument about externalities, stated without evidence in these pages.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:zero-sum-skill-luck]{zero-sum trading, skill and luck}; \hyperref[def:trader-motives]{trader motives}; \hyperref[def:liquidity-dimensions]{liquidity and market quality}; \hyperref[def:market-efficiency]{market efficiency}; \hyperref[def:market-regulation-design]{regulation as market design}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chaps.~1 and~9; Hasbrouck, chap.~1.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Trader motives: utilitarian, profit-motivated and futile traders; patience and the liquidity side; instruments for hedging; sources of a trading edge]\label{def:trader-motives}
\qffield{Statement} Harris classifies traders by why they trade, using stylised traders who each trade for one reason \citep[chap.~8, p.~177]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{utilitarian traders} trade for a benefit other than trading profit: investors and borrowers move money forward or backward through time, asset exchangers trade one asset for another of greater immediate use, hedgers trade risks, gamblers trade for entertainment, and also fledglings (to learn), cross-subsidisers (to pay for broker services through commissions) and tax avoiders (sec.~8.1, p.~178; Table~3-1, p.~33);
\item \emph{profit-motivated traders} trade only because they rationally expect to profit: speculators, who are informed traders or parasitic traders (order anticipators and bluffers), and dealers, who sell liquidity (sec.~8.2, pp.~194--196, Table~8-4);
\item \emph{futile traders} ``believe that they are profit-motivated traders. Although they expect to trade profitably, their expectations are not rational'' (p.~177): inefficient traders, of whom the commonest are pseudo-informed traders trading on old news, victimised traders whose agents fail their fiduciary duties, and rogue traders who hide losses from employers or clients (sec.~8.3, pp.~197--198).
\end{itemize}
A trader with a \emph{long} position owns the instrument and profits when prices rise; one with a \emph{short} position ``ha[s] sold something that they do not own'' and repurchases, or \emph{covers}, later (chap.~3, p.~32). Equivalent negative exposure through derivatives is an extension beyond Harris's definition.

\qffield{Conditions} Viewing trading as a game, with players submitting orders under venue rules, follows Harris's point that market structure ``determines what people can know and do'' and so ``the power relationships among different types of traders'' (chap.~1, p.~7; chap.~5, p.~89). Motives are a modelling device: ``In practice, traders often trade for many reasons'', and Harris's sidebar \emph{Multiple Identities} says many traders ``simultaneously invest, speculate, and gamble'' (p.~177).

Motive is therefore inferred from context and exposure, not from the order: Harris notes that ``Order type therefore is not always the best indicator of whether a trader is offering liquidity or taking it'' \citep[chap.~19, sec.~19.3.1, p.~400]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Patience and the liquidity side}: impatient traders search actively and demand liquidity; patient traders search passively and supply it (sec.~19.1.2, p.~396); Hasbrouck adds that with electronic access the role ``is a strategic choice that can be quickly reversed'' \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.2, p.~5]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Instruments for a problem.} Investors favour high expected return, low risk and low transaction cost; borrowers the cheapest instruments to create, service and repay (chap.~8, p.~180). Hedgers like liquid markets and instruments closely correlated with their risks (sec.~8.1.3.4, p.~188).

Harris's hedging examples give the vehicle trade-offs: forwards need the parties to find and trust each other and agree delivery terms, so they suit traders who deal with each other in the normal course of business and ``forward markets generally are not liquid'' (pp.~184--185); futures are standardised forwards guaranteed by a clearinghouse, liquid, and a \emph{linear} hedge that removes upside as well as downside (p.~185), with the delivery location differing from the hedger's (pp.~185--186); options are a \emph{nonlinear} hedge that limits downside for a premium and keeps upside (pp.~186--187); swaps exchange fixed for floating cash flows and leave basis risk, since a bank's deposit rates are not the swap's index rate (pp.~187--188).

Basis risk is developed in \hyperref[def:futures-hedge-basis]{futures hedging and basis}. \emph{Comparative advantage.} ``Successful profit-motivated traders must have some advantage that allows them to trade profitably. Their various advantages usually involve information'' (p.~196), and free entry drives the net profits of alternative strategies towards equality after all costs (chap.~1, pp.~7--8). Harris advises speculators to expose themselves ``only to the risks they understand best'' (p.~186).

Applied to signals, a feature is useful only if it captures information or execution capacity that competitors do not fully exploit; a high in-sample score without an economic reason is usually a selection result, not an edge. The first condition is Harris's advantage requirement; the second concerns selection bias, treated in \hyperref[meth:skill-luck-significance-tests]{tests of skill against luck} and \hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting}, and is untested here. Practitioners list information, models, execution, risk bearing and discipline as edges; only information and the cost of trading are Harris's in these pages.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Arbitrageurs are not simply informed traders who supply or demand liquidity: Harris counts them as informed traders about relative values (Table~8-4) and, as liquidity suppliers, calls them ``porters of liquidity'' (chap.~19, p.~404); see \hyperref[def:arbitrage]{arbitrage}. Treating noise traders as rebalancing, cash needs, index changes, behavioural demand or errors mixes utilitarian traders with futile ones; Harris's point is that utilitarian and futile traders lose on average to profit-motivated traders ``because trading is a zero-sum game'' (p.~177). Pseudo-informed traders are a kind of inefficient trader, not a separate type. Gamblers are utilitarian in Harris's scheme because they get entertainment, not because of payoff convexity.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:zero-sum-skill-luck]{zero-sum trading}; \hyperref[def:informed-trader-types]{informed trader types}; \hyperref[def:order-anticipation]{order anticipation}; \hyperref[def:bluffing-manipulation]{bluffing and manipulation}; \hyperref[def:market-intermediaries]{market intermediaries}; \hyperref[def:market-functions]{what markets do}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris's chapter~8 is the source of the classification.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfresult}[Zero-sum trading accounting, its two corollaries, and what past profit says about skill]\label{res:zero-sum-skill-luck}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Accounting.} Harris: trading is ``a zero-sum game in an important accounting sense'': ``the combined gains and losses of buyers and sellers always sum to zero. If a buyer profits from a trade, the seller loses the opportunity to profit by the same amount'' \citep[chap.~8, p.~176]{alpha-harris}; elsewhere, ``when gains and losses are measured relative to the market average'' (chap.~1, p.~6). For a trade of $q$ at price $P$ and any common terminal value $V$, the buyer's gain $q(V-P)$ and the seller's $q(P-V)$ sum to zero; with costs $C_i\ge0$ the sum of net gains is $-\sum_iC_i$.

\emph{Corollary 1}: ``Since trading is a zero-sum game, speculators and dealers cannot profit on average if they trade only among themselves. They can profit only if utilitarian traders are willing to trade'' (chap.~9, p.~206). \emph{Corollary 2} (the arithmetic of active management, derived here, not from Harris): if active managers' holdings aggregate, value-weighted, to the market portfolio, their aggregate gross active return is zero and their aggregate net active return is $-\sum_iw_ic_i\le0$; a single manager beats a passive alternative with drag $c_P$ only if $a_i>c_i-c_P$. \emph{Skill and luck}: write profit as $\Pi_{t+1}=S_{t+1}+L_{t+1}$, skill plus luck, and let $\mathcal F_t$ be the evaluator's information. Then
\[ E[\Pi_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,\Pi_t]=E[\Pi_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]\quad\text{if}\quad E[S_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,\Pi_t]=E[S_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]\ \text{and}\ E[L_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,\Pi_t]=E[L_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]. \]
Both conditions are needed. Unconditional zero-mean luck, $E[L]=0$, is not enough: persistent luck makes past profit predictive without skill.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Accounting.} The identity needs a common valuation of the asset for both sides (a common mark or terminal value); it says nothing about utility, so a hedger who pays to shed risk or an asset exchanger can gain in welfare while losing in money (chap.~8, pp.~176--177). Corollary~1 needs a closed set of traders and costs that are not income of the group (fees paid to members are transfers within it). Corollary~2 needs the actives to hold the market in aggregate, which fails if other holders (for example, non-market holdings by passive owners or issuers) take the other side.

\emph{Skill and luck.} The result assumes that $S_{t+1}$ is independent of past profit given current information and that luck satisfies the second condition above. If skill is \emph{not} in $\mathcal F_t$, past profit is informative about it: with persistent skill $S\sim N(\mu,\tau^2)$ and i.i.d.\ luck of variance $\sigma^2$, the regression slope of $\Pi_{t+1}$ on $\Pi_t$ is $\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2)$, which is the shrinkage a performance evaluator applies.

Harris states the evaluator's version: predicting from past performance needs past performance to reflect skill, the skill to keep working in future market conditions, and the manager to keep it \citep[chap.~22, sec.~22.3, p.~451]{alpha-harris}; a statistical test works because luck ``is random and completely unpredictable'' and its net effect on average returns shrinks with time, while skill has a systematic effect (sec.~22.4.1, p.~454).

\qffield{Practice} Simulation: 200{,}000 simulated managers with $\tau=0.5$, $\sigma=1$ give a slope of 0.200 against $\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2)=0.200$; with no skill and i.i.d.\ luck the slope is $-0.006$; with no skill and AR(1) luck of persistence 0.5 and unconditional mean zero the slope is 0.501, so past profit predicts luck; with skill in the information set and conditionally mean-zero luck the incremental slope is 0.0005. Random bilateral trades among ten traders marked at a common value sum to zero gross ($-7\times10^{-13}$) and to minus total costs net; value-weighted active returns of managers aggregating to the market sum to zero, and do not when they do not.

In practice: test skill claims against a benchmark, over long samples (Harris notes more than 20 years of returns are typically needed for a reliable test, p.~453), and correct for survivorship (sec.~22.6, pp.~470--471).

\qffield{Pitfalls} ``Zero-sum'' is an accounting statement about money, not a claim that trade creates no value. Harris's two wordings differ: relative to a common terminal value (p.~176) and ``relative to the market average'' (p.~6); the second makes it a statement about excess returns. A skill claim must explain why the decision was right before the outcome was known, why the edge should persist, and why it is not already competed away, which matches Harris's three conditions and his free-entry argument (chap.~1, pp.~7--8). A proposition that conditions on skill being known cannot be used to say that past profit is useless for an evaluator who does not know it.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:trader-motives]{trader motives}; \hyperref[meth:relative-performance-evaluation]{relative performance evaluation}; \hyperref[meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics]{backtest evaluation diagnostics}; \hyperref[def:market-functions]{what markets do}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chaps.~8, 9 and~22.

\end{qfresult}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Market intermediaries: dealers and market makers, brokers and their services, broker-dealers, and the suppliers of liquidity]\label{def:market-intermediaries}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Dealers} trade with their clients when the clients want to trade and profit by buying low and selling high; \emph{brokers} arrange trades as agents and profit from commissions; firms that do both are \emph{broker-dealers} or dual traders \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.1.2, pp.~33--34]{alpha-harris}. ``The sell side exists only because the buy side will pay for its services'' (p.~34). Dealers sell liquidity at their bid and ask; to profit they must buy and sell equal volumes and so must find the prices that balance supply and demand; Harris classes them as uninformed about fundamental values. Market makers provide liquidity on demand in small quantities and trade in and out many times a day; block facilitators provide liquidity to large traders, may take days or weeks to unwind, and generally choose their clients \citep[chap.~8, sec.~8.2.2, pp.~195--196]{alpha-harris}.

\emph{Why brokers are cheaper than trading directly}: they solve clearing and settlement problems, give access to exchanges and dealers their clients cannot reach, know better who might trade, negotiate better, and represent orders when clients are absent \citep[chap.~7, sec.~7.1, pp.~140--141]{alpha-harris}; ``The most important, but perhaps least appreciated'' of these is clearing and settlement, because order-driven markets arrange trades among strangers (p.~141).

\qffield{Conditions} Concepts timeless; institutional examples as of 2002. \emph{Who supplies liquidity.} Harris's five suppliers, by what they know and the dimension they supply \citep[chap.~19, secs.~19.3.2--19.3.7, pp.~401--405]{alpha-harris}: market makers supply immediacy to small traders and avoid informed traders; block dealers supply depth to clients they know to be uninformed; value traders, ``the ultimate suppliers of liquidity'', supply depth and make markets resilient, and lose only to better news or value traders (p.~403); precommitted traders post aggressive limit orders to lower the cost of trades they intend anyway and supply immediacy at narrow spreads, which can drive dealers out (p.~404); arbitrageurs are ``not suppliers of liquidity but porters of liquidity'' between markets (p.~404).

Harris's short list: ``Impatient traders take liquidity. Dealers, limit order traders, and some speculators offer liquidity. Brokers and exchanges organize liquidity'' (p.~394). Capital standards for dealers only ensure that they \emph{can} supply liquidity; ``no amount of capital will make them willing'' under heavy adverse selection (p.~402).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Brokered transactions.} Harris's Table~7-1 distinguishes order-flow, block, new and seasoned offering, and merger-and-acquisition business (p.~140). Order-driven exchanges ``are essentially brokerages'' (chap.~3, p.~35), and automated order-driven systems are ``essentially electronic brokers'' (chap.~7, p.~139); regulators classify similar systems sometimes as exchanges and sometimes as brokers (p.~139). Introducing brokers pass orders to clearing members, and sponsored subscribers of electronic systems trade under their broker's real-time credit limits (pp.~142--143). Inventory risk and quote management are \hyperref[def:dealer-inventory-risk]{dealer inventory risk}; agency problems are \hyperref[def:broker-agency-conflicts]{broker agency conflicts}; block roles are \hyperref[def:block-trade]{block trades}; fast intermediaries are \hyperref[def:hft-strategies]{high-frequency trading strategies}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Harris's five types of liquidity supplier each occupy a niche (p.~405), so calling all passive flow ``market making'' hides the source of depth. Confirmation, custody, margin and records extend Harris's post-trade clearing and settlement, and information control belongs to his block-trading material (\hyperref[def:block-trade]{block trades}); in Harris custody is a service of depositories and custodians (chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2.4, p.~38). Dealer compensation from rebates and information is an additional interpretation.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:trader-motives]{trader motives}; \hyperref[def:market-structures-venues]{market structures and venues}; \hyperref[def:liquidity-dimensions]{liquidity and market quality}; \hyperref[def:outside-spread]{the outside spread}; \hyperref[mod:inventory-based-quotes]{inventory-based quotes}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chaps.~7, 13 and~19.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Dealers and their quotes: the four problems of dealing, two-sided and one-sided, firm and soft quotes, trading with dealers, and how dealers attract order flow (as of 2002)]\label{def:dealer-quotes-order-flow}
\qffield{Statement} ``Dealers are profit-motivated traders who allow other traders to trade when they want to trade. The liquidity service they sell---immediacy---is valuable to impatient traders'' \citep[chap.~13, sec.~13.1, p.~279]{alpha-harris}. They are passive traders who buy when clients want to sell and sell when they want to buy, and lose when market conditions force them to sell low or buy high, often after trading with informed traders (p.~278). ``All dealers face the same problems regardless of what they trade. They must set prices, they must market their services to acquire clients, they must manage their inventories, and they must be careful that they do not trade with better-informed traders'' (p.~278). Their quotes \citep[same chap., sec.~13.2, pp.~280--281]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \emph{two-sided} or \emph{one-sided}: most dealers quote both sides but ``usually aggressively price only the side on which they would prefer to trade'', a dealer who wants to buy quoting a high bid and an uncompetitive ask (p.~280);
\item \emph{firm} or \emph{soft}: firm quotes must be honoured ``only up to some maximum quantity'', the bid and ask sizes; a soft quote ``is simply an indication of interest'' (pp.~280--281);
\item \emph{continuous} or \emph{on request}: dealers in most corporate bond and some foreign exchange markets quote only on request, and such quotes are good for a limited time, shorter in active markets with volatile prices or narrow spreads, while organised quote-driven stock markets such as Nasdaq required continuous firm two-sided quotes (p.~281);
\item the \emph{inside spread}, best bid against best offer across dealers, ``can be no wider than the narrowest individual dealer spread'': five dealers each quoting a 0.50 spread make a 0.10 inside spread in Harris's Table~13-1 (pp.~280--281).
\end{itemize}
Dealers attract order flow ``by quoting aggressive prices and large sizes, providing high-quality service at low prices, advertising, creating marketing relationships, and purchasing it'' \citep[same chap., sec.~13.4, pp.~282--283]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Trading with dealers} \citep[same chap., sec.~13.3, pp.~281--282]{alpha-harris}. Dealers often trade at better prices than they quote, especially with small, active and apparently uninformed customers, so traders negotiate; institutions dealing directly pay no commission but a net price. Brokers route small orders to dealers they expect to give the best combination of price and service and insist on the best quoted price; ``When experienced traders negotiate prices with dealers, they usually ask for a two-sided quote before they say whether they want to buy or sell'', which disciplines dealers who would otherwise quote high to known buyers and low to known sellers, most where dealers are few (p.~282).

\emph{Order flow} \citep[same chap., sec.~13.4, pp.~282--283]{alpha-harris}: where quotes are public, aggressive prices win orders; where they are not, or price improvement is common, reputation, service and published statistics on execution quality do; relationships with brokers and institutions are cultivated with information, research, routing and accounting systems and entertainment; and some dealers buy order flow, typically market orders only, at payments that then averaged ``less than 1 cent per share'' in U.S. markets (up to 3 cents in the past), or exchange it reciprocally (p.~283).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Preferencing.} A broker who sends an order to a specific dealer \emph{preferences} it to that dealer; wholesalers trade mainly with orders introduced by retail brokers, and ``By forming stable relationships, brokers and dealers often can lower the total costs of trading'' (p.~282); the regulatory problems are in \hyperref[def:broker-agency-conflicts]{broker agency conflicts} and \hyperref[def:best-execution]{best execution}. \emph{Position and spread traders.} Where competition among dealers is intense, dealers must also speculate successfully to stay in business (position traders); spread traders profit only from buying at the bid and selling at the ask (sec.~13.1, p.~279).

\emph{Reading a wide quote}. A dealer who suspects informed flow, carries an unwanted inventory or expects a costly unwind widens or skews the quote, so a wide two-sided quote signals one of these; Harris's tactics for inventory and suspected informed traders are in \hyperref[meth:dealer-quote-adjustment]{dealer quote adjustment} (Tables~13-2 and~13-3). The source tells the client only to seek another dealer or refuse to trade when the quoted spread is too wide (p.~282).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Dealers are not necessarily compensated directly for relationships or order-flow value; Harris names the spread (buying low, selling high) and, for some dealers, speculation, and treats relationships and purchased order flow as ways of acquiring flow, not as compensation (pp.~279, 282--283). Inferring value from trades, quotes, news and client behaviour goes beyond Harris's fourth problem, avoiding better-informed traders, but matches his account of dealers inferring values from order flow and quote adjustment (secs.~13.6--13.8). Preferencing can route orders toward economic arrangements rather than best all-in execution, the agency conflict of \hyperref[def:broker-agency-conflicts]{broker agency conflicts}; it is a possibility, not a finding. Harris's U.S. figures (payments under a cent, Nasdaq's continuous-quote obligation) are as of 2002; the EU ban on receiving payment for order flow is in \hyperref[rule:eu-trading-venue-transparency]{EU trading venues}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:market-intermediaries]{market intermediaries}; \hyperref[def:specialist-obligations-privileges]{specialists and designated market makers}; \hyperref[def:dealer-inventory-risk]{dealer inventory risk}; \hyperref[meth:dealer-quote-adjustment]{dealer quote adjustment}; \hyperref[def:outside-spread]{the outside spread}; \hyperref[def:fx-market-structure]{FX market structure} and \hyperref[def:fixed-income-market-structure]{fixed-income market structure} (quotes on request).

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chap.~13.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Specialists and designated market makers: affirmative and negative obligations, privileges, the participation option, tick size and participation, and the regulator's question (as of 2002)]\label{def:specialist-obligations-privileges}
\qffield{Statement} Exchanges register some dealers as specialists or designated market makers and regulate what they must and may not do: ``Affirmative obligations obligate specialists to offer liquidity in various circumstances. Negative obligations prevent them from trading in other circumstances. Specialists accept these obligations because they enjoy the various privileges that come with them'' \citep[chap.~24, sec.~24.2, p.~496]{alpha-harris}. As of 2002:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \emph{affirmative}: ensure a reasonable market, quoting meaningful two-sided markets when no one else will, as ``traders of last resort'', without having to quote block sizes or support falling prices; smooth prices against large reversals to create \emph{price continuity}, judged by quoted spreads and depths and the number and size of reversals (sec.~24.2.1, pp.~496--498);
\item \emph{negative}: the public order precedence rule (no trading at a price at which a public trader is willing to trade) and price priority, and the \emph{public liquidity preservation principle}, which discourages specialists from trading with standing limit orders, so that they can only offer liquidity to incoming marketable orders or step ahead with a better price (sec.~24.2.2, pp.~498--499);
\item \emph{auction}: conducting the opening single-price auction and orderly markets, without direct compensation (sec.~24.4, p.~501);
\item \emph{capital}: exchanges' capital adequacy standards ``also ensure that specialists will have adequate capital to provide liquidity if no one else will'' (sec.~24.7, p.~511).
\end{itemize}
The privileges are ``access to information about order flows, the right to make decisions after others make their decisions, the ability to create the market quote, the ability to create and exercise certain look-back timing options, and the right to collect brokerage commissions from executing system order flow'' (sec.~24.5, p.~501), with dealing profits in thin markets where they are always present (sec.~24.5.9, p.~508).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{The participation option} (Interpretation: a formalisation of Harris's argument). With $V_{\mathrm{step}}$ the expected value of stepping ahead of or trading with the book, $C_{\mathrm{obligation}}$ the expected loss from required liquidity provision and $K$ compliance and capital costs, an assignment is privately attractive when $V_{\mathrm{step}}>C_{\mathrm{obligation}}+K$. Harris states the qualitative version: the negative obligations ``must be costly'', and specialists accept them ``because the value of their special privileges more than compensates'' (p.~499). The public version is his: ``Regulators must consider whether the value specialists obtain from their privileges is commensurate with the value of the additional liquidity they provide'', which is hard because liquidity services are hard to measure and value (sec.~24.7, p.~510).

\emph{Tick size and participation.} With a one-tick spread, the precedence rule and the preservation principle leave a specialist who wants to buy ahead of the book only the option of buying at the ask from an incoming marketable sell order; a smaller tick relaxes the public order precedence rule ``by decreasing the cost of stepping in front of booked orders'', since the specialist can then step inside the public quotes, and when U.S. ticks fell to one-sixteenth in 1997 and to a penny in 2000 ``specialist participation rates and specialist profitability both increased substantially'' (box, p.~500).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Who pays and who gains.} The beneficiaries are mainly small uninformed market-order traders who get price improvement and momentum traders who get liquidity in volatile markets; the payers are the limit-order traders in front of whose orders specialists step, and the system transfers wealth between them (p.~510). The opening option: Harris's specialist who wants to sell 16 lots at the open chooses the clearing price after seeing the book, selling 15 at 35.2 rather than depress the price (box, p.~509).

The liquidity a specialist supplies in stress helps all markets, while the profits come only from trading at its own exchange. Exchanges with small market shares therefore ``cannot expect that their traders will provide much price continuity''. With many competing dealers, free riding makes price continuity scarce, which Nasdaq's 20-day rule for dealers who stop making a market only partly addressed (pp.~510--511).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Capital standards do not mean price support: specialists ``are not required to support prices when values are falling'' (p.~497), so the obligation is a meaningful market, not a price floor. Larger ticks protect queue rents but can widen quoted spreads (\hyperref[def:tick-size]{tick size}), and the value of queue position in large-tick markets is \hyperref[res:queue-position-value]{queue-position value}. Everything here describes 2002 U.S. exchange specialists (NYSE, AMEX, regional exchanges, CBOE's designated market makers, pp.~495--496).

\emph{One current venue}. NYSE Rule~104, in the rulebook compilation NYSE publishes (file last modified 9 May 2019), requires a DMM to maintain ``a continuous two-sided quote with a displayed size of at least one round lot'' and DMM units to be at the national best bid or offer at least 15 percent of the trading day in securities with consolidated average daily volume below one million shares and 10 percent above, executions crediting and hidden orders not counting.

For NMS stocks its quotes must be entered within a \emph{Designated Percentage} of the NBBO (8 percent for LULD Tier~1 stocks, 28 for Tier~2 at \$1 or more, 30 below \$1; 20, 28 and~30 near the open and close) and refreshed if the NBBO moves beyond a \emph{Defined Limit} (9.5, 29.5 and 31.5 percent; 21.5 for Tier~1 near the open and close); DMMs facilitate openings, reopenings, the Midday Auction and the close, and their algorithms see aggregate order information for that purpose \citep[Rule~104(a)(1)--(3) and~(b)]{nyse-rules-2019}.

Supplemental liquidity providers, by contrast, must average at least 10 percent of the day at the NBB or NBO in assigned securities in return for rebates (Rule~107B(a)). The Federal Register shows a 2023 proposal to restructure the DMM programme that NYSE withdrew in July 2024 \citep[88~FR 77625; 89~FR 56456; 84~FR 51205]{fr-nyse-dmm-2023-withdrawn}. \emph{Current text} (NYSE online rulebook, 16 October 2026). The current Rule~104 keeps the 15 and 10 percent inside-quote requirements for securities that are not ETPs and adds at least 25 percent of the trading day for ETPs; the Designated Percentages (8, 28 and~30; 20, 28 and~30 between 9:30 and 9:45 a.m.\ and from 3:35 p.m.\ to the close) and Defined Limits (9.5, 29.5 and 31.5; 21.5 for Tier~1 in those periods) are as in the 2019 compilation \citep[Rule~104(a)(1) and~(a)(1)(A), export pp.~1--2]{nyse-rule104-2026}.

Its amendment history runs to 22 November 2022 (SR-NYSE-2022-52), with amendments in January 2020, June 2021 and four in 2022 after those of 2019 (export p.~7). Rule~107B's 10 percent average for SLPs is unchanged, last amended 25 January 2023 (SR-NYSE-2023-05) \citep[Rule~107B(a), export pp.~1 and~9]{nyse-rule107b-2026}. The rule texts are rendered pages, not filings.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chap.~24.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Market structures and venues: forums and access, exchanges, OTC and alternative systems, market information systems and transparency, clearing and settlement, and brokered credit]\label{def:market-structures-venues}
\qffield{Statement} ``The trading rules and the trading systems used by a market define its market structure. They determine who can trade; what they can trade; and when, where, and how they can trade. They also determine what information traders can see about orders, quotations, and trades; when they can see it; and who can see it'' \citep[chap.~5, p.~89]{alpha-harris}. Harris classifies a structure by its trading sessions, its execution system and its market information systems (p.~89; sessions and execution systems are \hyperref[def:trading-sessions-execution-systems]{trading sessions and execution systems}), with a minimum price increment (p.~90; \hyperref[def:tick-size]{tick size}). Forums are \emph{physically convened} (pits, posts) or \emph{distributed access} (telephone or screen); some jurisdictions require all trading in an instrument at one exchange (p.~90).

\emph{Exchanges} provide forums where members trade; some only host negotiation, others match orders by rules; corporate ownership replaced member ownership at many exchanges around 2000 (chap.~3, sec.~3.2.1, pp.~34--35). In \emph{over-the-counter} markets dealers and brokers arrange trades away from exchanges (p.~35). Harris's 2002 terms for U.S.\ equities: ECNs are order-driven systems not regulated as exchanges; the \emph{third market} is dealers trading listed stocks away from the exchange; alternative trading systems, ECNs among them, trade in the \emph{fourth market} (pp.~35 and~49). Exchanges are search engines: they ``collect information about who wants to trade'' and organise it so that traders find the best opportunities \citep[chap.~19, box, p.~396]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Notation} \emph{Pre-trade} (ex ante) and \emph{post-trade} (ex post) transparency: quick reporting of quotes and orders, and of trades; \emph{top of book} against \emph{market by price} \citep[chap.~5, sec.~5.4.4, p.~101]{alpha-harris}. Open-book and closed-book markets (sec.~5.4.7, p.~109).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Market information systems} (sec.~5.4, pp.~97--109): collection (electronic systems collect data cheaply; floors need reporters; data were over 20 percent of revenues at many exchanges, p.~97), distribution (members often see more and sooner; real-time and delayed services price-discriminate; U.S.\ securities information processors consolidate quotes and trades, p.~98), order routing, order presentation and order books.

\emph{Transparency}: ``Traders are often ambivalent about transparency \dots\ those who know the least about market conditions most favor transparency. Those who know the most oppose transparency'' (p.~101); Lehalle and Laruelle call the same tension a paradox: more shared information should improve price formation, but ``each trader fears `information leakage' '' \citep[Introduction, pp.~19--20]{alpha-laruelle}. \emph{Post-trade infrastructure}: a clearing agent matches buyer and seller records (failures are out-trades or DKs); settlement agents exchange cash and securities, usually net; clearinghouses act ``as buyer for every seller and as seller for every buyer'', require margins and position limits, and are mutualised among clearing members; depositories and custodians hold assets; systems that automate clearing and settlement provide straight-through processing \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2, pp.~35--38]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Brokered credit.} Harris's sidebar \emph{Multiplying Credit Checks}: with $10^6$ traders and no brokers, each trader must be ready to check every other, $999{,}999{,}000{,}000$ potential relationships; with 100 brokers of 10{,}000 clients each who guarantee their clients' trades, brokers check clients, clients check brokers and brokers check each other: $2{,}009{,}900$, ``500{,}000 times smaller'' \citep[chap.~7, p.~142]{alpha-harris}. These are ordered counts, $n(n-1)$ and $2n+B(B-1)$. Counted as unordered pairs they are $n(n-1)/2$ against $n+B(B-1)/2$ ($499{,}999{,}500{,}000$ against $1{,}004{,}950$, the same ratio of about 497{,}500). Either form holds when each client uses exactly one broker that guarantees its trades, clients do not check one another or other brokers, every pair of brokers may trade, and $n$ counts clients only.

\emph{Reported volume} depends on architecture: a 100-share trade between public traders reports 100 shares at an order-matching market, 200 with one dealer in between and 300 with two, and the World Federation of Exchanges distinguishes trading-system-view from regulated-environment-view counts (chap.~3, box, p.~48). \emph{RFQ against CLOB.} In fixed income the dealing desk requests quotes from several market makers and takes the best, while the most liquid products trade on electronic books close to equity ones \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.6, pp.~190--191]{alpha-laruelle}; the protocols are \hyperref[def:fixed-income-market-structure]{fixed-income market structure}. Electronification has not produced one architecture: futures are almost unfragmented, which Lehalle and Laruelle attribute to post-trade complexity, and U.S.\ options are fragmented while European ones are not, because of fragmented clearing (pp.~189--190).

\emph{Which venue for which order}: small traders like wide exposure, while large traders want exposure only to traders who will commit to trade with them, through crossing networks, negotiated size or brokers (Harris, chap.~26, sec.~26.3.1, pp.~530--531).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Harris's venue names, regulatory classes and settlement cycle (T+3 in 2002, with T+1 planned for 2005, p.~36) are historical; current U.S.\ and EU venue types are in \hyperref[rule:us-equity-market-structure]{U.S.\ equity market structure rules} and \hyperref[rule:eu-trading-venue-transparency]{EU trading venues and transparency}. Consolidated, fragmented and segmented market centres are Harris's chap.~26 vocabulary, treated in \hyperref[mech:venue-competition-fragmentation]{venue competition and fragmentation}; whether dark pools, internalisers and crossing networks weaken public price discovery is in \hyperref[def:dark-pools-crossing]{dark pools and crossing networks}. Designated market makers follow Harris's chap.~24 \citep[sec.~24.1, pp.~495--496]{alpha-harris}: specialist systems impose obligations to supply liquidity, usually one specialist per stock; designated multiple-market-maker systems oblige several, obligations being ``best enforced in electronic markets''; third-market dealers' obligations are voluntary; a pure CLOB has none.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:floor-automated-trading-systems]{floor and automated systems}; \hyperref[mech:otc-clearing-collateral]{OTC clearing and collateral}; \hyperref[data:market-data-levels]{market data levels}; \hyperref[def:market-intermediaries]{market intermediaries}; \hyperref[def:trading-sessions-execution-systems]{trading sessions}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chaps.~3, 5, 7 and~24.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Trading sessions and execution systems: continuous and call markets, quote-driven, order-driven, brokered and hybrid markets, auctions and fixings, and specialists]\label{def:trading-sessions-execution-systems}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Sessions.} ``In continuous trading, traders can attempt to arrange their trades whenever the market is open. In call markets, all trades take place only when the market is called'' \citep[chap.~5, sec.~5.1, p.~89]{alpha-harris}. A call may cover all instruments at once or call them in \emph{rotation}, by 2002 rare; many continuous markets open with a call and restart with one after halts; governments sell debt in calls; some markets (Deutsche Börse, Euronext Paris in 2002) trade their least active stocks only in calls (sec.~5.2.2, pp.~90--91). Calls focus attention ``at the same time and place''; continuous markets serve impatient traders (sec.~5.2.3, p.~91). Some markets run after-hours sessions (sec.~5.2.4, p.~92). \emph{Execution systems}, ``the defining characteristic of a market'' (sec.~5.3, p.~92):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{quote-driven} (dealer) markets: in pure form dealers take part in every trade and choose their clients; interdealer brokers let dealers trade anonymously (sec.~5.3.1, pp.~92--93);
\item \emph{order-driven} markets: order precedence rules decide who trades with whom and pricing rules the price; most are auction markets, by single-price auction, continuous two-sided auction or crossing network; auctions are oral (open outcry) or rule-based; traders cannot choose counterparties, so these markets need strong credit arrangements (sec.~5.3.2, pp.~94--95);
\item \emph{brokered} markets: brokers search for \emph{concealed} traders, who will not expose orders, and \emph{latent} traders, who do not know they want to trade until shown an opportunity; typical for blocks, real estate and going concerns (sec.~5.3.3, pp.~95--96);
\item \emph{hybrid} markets mix these, as the 2002 NYSE (order-driven, with specialists obliged to supply liquidity) and Nasdaq (quote-driven, displaying public limit orders) did (sec.~5.3.4, p.~96).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Auctions and fixings.} A single-price double-sided auction ranks bids and offers and usually maximises feasible volume; securities with little natural interest trade mainly in periodic auctions, also called fixings; opening and closing auctions set reference prices used for margins, fund valuations, cash-settled derivatives and some mergers, which invites attempts to ``mark the close'' \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.4, pp.~17--18]{s6-hasbrouck}. Design details matter: random stopping times discourage last-instant orders (Euronext), deadlines may be extended after large indicated moves, and large or destabilising orders and the cancellation of stabilising orders may face earlier deadlines (p.~18).

Clearing rules and incidence are \hyperref[mech:order-matching-pricing]{order matching and trade pricing}; primary markets' hold on auctions and its effect on venue shares is \hyperref[meth:venue-market-share]{venue market share}. \emph{Specialists} play three roles, as dealers trading for their own account, brokers representing other brokers' orders, and exchange officials responsible for orderly markets, with the auctioneer's role under the third \citep[chap.~24, sec.~24.1, p.~496; secs.~24.2--24.4, pp.~496--501]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{The London gold price as a sequential call.} From 12 September 1919 a daily gold price was set by five founding members, chaired by N~M Rothschild \& Sons, by telephone for the first days and then at a daily meeting \citep[web page]{lbma-gold-centenary}; LBMA describes that arrangement, ``an exclusive membership of five trading houses agreeing the daily price'', as no longer valid, and on 20 March 2015 announced ICE Benchmark Administration as the new independent administrator of the auction (same page).

As of October 2026, IBA runs electronic auctions for gold at 10:30 and 15:00 London time in rounds of fixed duration (for example 30 seconds): it publishes a price, participants enter, change or cancel quantities, and the round ends the auction if the buy--sell imbalance is within a preset threshold; otherwise the price is adjusted and a new round starts; any residual imbalance is shared equally among direct participants \citep[web page]{iba-precious-metals-auctions}. The auction produces one price, not continuous immediacy.

\emph{Why calls suit large orders}: a call concentrates opposite interest at one price, and Harris notes that trading halts which reopen with a call switch the pricing rule from discriminatory to uniform, protecting standing limit orders \citep[chap.~28, pp.~572--573]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Session hours, call rotations and the hybrid examples are as of 2002 (Harris) and 2007 (Hasbrouck). Opening, intraday, closing and volatility calls are a classification; venue rules differ. More transparency improves price discovery only when participants cannot cheaply game the signal (Interpretation); Hasbrouck's deadline rules (p.~18) are the documented countermeasures. How floor and brokered systems weaken equal access is in \hyperref[def:floor-automated-trading-systems]{floor and automated trading systems}. The gold price's 1919--2015 procedure is from LBMA's history page; the IBA methodology and participant list change over time.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:market-structures-venues]{market structures and venues}; \hyperref[mech:order-matching-pricing]{order matching and trade pricing}; \hyperref[def:floor-automated-trading-systems]{floor and automated trading systems}; \hyperref[mech:circuit-breakers-trading-halts]{circuit breakers and trading halts}; \hyperref[def:dealer-inventory-risk]{dealer inventory risk}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chaps.~5, 6 and~24; Hasbrouck, chap.~2.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Liquidity and market quality: liquidity as bilateral search, immediacy, width, depth and resiliency, displayed and latent liquidity, the four observable variables, and liquidity risk]\label{def:liquidity-dimensions}
\qffield{Statement} Harris: ``Liquidity is the ability to trade large size quickly, at low cost, when you want to trade'' \citep[chap.~19, p.~394]{alpha-harris}. It ``is the object of a bilateral search'' in which buyers look for sellers and sellers for buyers, actively or passively (sec.~19.1, p.~395). Searching has inputs (time) and outputs (price and size), whose trade-offs define three dimensions (sec.~19.2, p.~398):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{immediacy}: ``how quickly trades of a given size can be arranged at a given cost'';
\item \emph{width}: ``the cost of doing a trade of a given size'', the spread for small trades plus commissions, the cost per unit of liquidity, also called breadth;
\item \emph{depth}: ``the size of a trade that can be arranged at a given cost'';
\end{itemize}
and a fourth, \emph{resiliency}: ``how quickly prices revert to former levels after they change in response to large order flow imbalances initiated by uninformed traders'' (p.~400). Both width and depth are stated per unit: width ``is the cost per unit of liquidity'', and depth ``is measured in units available at a given price of liquidity'' (p.~398). Width and depth are duals: minimising the cost of trading a given size and maximising the size traded at a given cost are ``essentially identical'' problems, so depth and breadth ``summarize essentially the same information'' (p.~399).

Formalised: on a size domain $\mathcal Q$ of positive sizes, let $w(q)$ be the per-unit cost of trading $q$ with a given search effort, measured from the midpoint with commissions included; depth at a per-unit cost $\bar w$ is $D(\bar w)=\max\{q\in\mathcal Q:w(q)\le\bar w\}$, and if $w$ is nondecreasing on $\mathcal Q$ and the maximum is attained, then for $q\in\mathcal Q$, $q\le D(\bar w)$ if and only if $w(q)\le\bar w$. A total cash budget is a different question: with total cost $C(q)=q\,w(q)$ for $w$ in currency per share (or $C(q)=q\,m\,w_{\mathrm{bp}}(q)/10{,}000$ for $w$ in basis points of the midpoint $m$), the size affordable within a budget $K$ is $\max\{q:C(q)\le K\}$, which is not Harris's depth. Lehalle and Laruelle's four observable liquidity variables are traded volume, the quantity at the best limits (the \emph{book size}), the bid--ask spread and volatility \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.2, pp.~143--148]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Notation} $q$ size in shares (or lots), on a stated domain $\mathcal Q$: a finite set of lot multiples $\{q_{\min},2q_{\min},\dots,\bar q\}$ or an interval $[q_{\min},\bar q]$ with $0<q_{\min}\le\bar q<\infty$ (a per-unit cost is undefined at zero size); $w(q)$ per-unit cost of size $q$ in currency per share, \emph{average} unless stated, or $w_{\mathrm{bp}}(q)=10{,}000\,w(q)/m$ in basis points of the midpoint $m$; $\bar w$ a per-unit cost level in the same units; $D(\bar w)$ depth at that level, in shares; $C(q)=q\,w(q)=q\,m\,w_{\mathrm{bp}}(q)/10{,}000$ total cost in currency; $K$ a cash budget in currency; $B$, $c$ and $L$ in the search rule below: expected improvement from one more search, its direct cost, and the expected loss if the current best match disappears.

\qffield{Conditions} Concepts timeless; Lehalle and Laruelle's data 2008--2016. \emph{Per unit against total}. With the midpoint at 99 and 100 shares offered at 100, buying $q\le100$ shares costs $w(q)=1$ per share, so depth at a per-unit cost of 1 is 100 shares; inverting the total cost $C(q)=q$ at a budget of 1 gives one share. The alternative definition $D(c)=\max\{q:C(q)\le c\}$ with $C$ an all-in cost and $c$ a budget answers the second question.

\emph{Average or marginal.} With 100 shares more offered at 101, the average per-unit cost of 200 shares is $(100\times1+100\times2)/200=1.5$, so depth at an average cost of 1.5 is 200 shares, while at a marginal cost (the cost of the last share) of 1.5 it is 100; say which is meant.

\emph{Monotonicity.} Sweeping a book makes the average per-unit cost nondecreasing in size; a fixed commission per order makes it fall with size ($F/q$ added to the price cost), so $w$ need not be monotone, the set $\{q:w(q)\le\bar w\}$ need not be an interval, and the equivalence can fail (with $F=50$ on the book above, $w(1)=51$ while $w(100)=1.5$).

\emph{Attainment.} On a finite domain the maximum exists whenever some size is feasible. On an interval domain it exists if and only if the feasible set is nonempty, bounded above and contains its supremum $q^\star$, that is, $w(q^\star)\le\bar w$. A compact domain with $w$ lower semicontinuous is sufficient, because the feasible set is then closed and bounded. Lower semicontinuity is not necessary: $w(q)=q$ on $[q_{\min},1]$, $2$ on $(1,2)$ and $5$ on $[2,3]$, which jumps up at 2 and is not lower semicontinuous there, attains the maximum 1 at $\bar w=1.5$. It is not enough without boundedness: on $[q_{\min},\infty)$ a constant $w$ below $\bar w$ is continuous and the feasible set has no maximum.

And with $w(q)=q$ for $q<1$ and $w(q)=2$ for $q\ge1$ at $\bar w=1.5$ the feasible sizes are $[q_{\min},1)$, the supremum 1 is infeasible, and the equivalence fails at $q=1$. \emph{Definitions differ.} Hasbrouck's ``depth, breadth, and resiliency'' uses breadth for a market with many participants, none with market power \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.2, pp.~4--5]{s6-hasbrouck}, not Harris's cost per unit; precise definitions ``only exist in the contexts of particular models'' (p.~4).

\emph{Search.} Continue an active search while the expected benefit of another inquiry exceeds its cost; in a bilateral search ``the cost of continuing to search must include the potential loss of your previous best match'' \citep[chap.~19, secs.~19.1.1--19.1.2, pp.~395--396]{alpha-harris}, hence the rule, search again if $B>c+L$. It is a one-step comparison; with recall and independent offers it coincides with the reservation-value rule (for uniform offers with cost 0.02: reservation 0.80, net value 0.8005 against 0.784 and 0.792 for earlier and later stopping), but it is not a general optimal-stopping theorem. Harris's camera example: one more search has expected gain below 2.50 (a gain of at most 10 with probability below 0.25) and costs more than 2.50 at 30 an hour for five minutes, so the buyer stops (box, p.~395).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Search strategies}: active searchers buy speed with exposure, passive searchers wait and risk not trading, and large traders search actively only among traders who post firm quotes or become passive and undisplayed (Harris, pp.~396--397); the ``selective search'' (trusted counterparties first, then wider) is how brokers use what they know about who wants to trade (pp.~397--398).

\emph{Displayed and undisclosed liquidity.} Harris's Table~19-1, after a classification by George Sofianos, lists displayed quotes and orders, liquidity not displayed at a venue, displayed indications, liquidity not displayed at broker-dealers or at the buy side, and latent liquidity, each with its search strategy (``take it'', ``offer to trade'', ``ask'', ``use Liquidnet'') (p.~397). \emph{Book size} averages the quantities at the best bid and ask, summing several limits for small-tick instruments; it is neither an upper nor a lower bound on traded volume, because books replenish (a best bid of 20 throughout and 100 trades of 19 in five minutes trade 1{,}900) and a large order sweeps several limits \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.2, pp.~143--144]{alpha-laruelle}.

\emph{What drives liquidity for an order}: why the trader trades (informed traders find less), what is traded (interest), how well values are known, when (closed markets, suspected news, holidays), what others are doing, displayed imbalances, and who is trading (Harris, sec.~19.2, p.~399; sec.~19.1.2, p.~398). \emph{Resiliency} comes from value traders when they are ``well capitalized, well informed, and willing to trade'' (sec.~19.3.4, p.~403).

\emph{Time of day}: volume, volatility, spread and book size share an intraday shape; high volume, volatility and spread after the open, a calm middle of the day, rising volume into the close with the spread at its minimum and the book larger (Royal Dutch Shell, 2011--2016, Lehalle and Laruelle, Figure~2.12, pp.~145--148). \emph{Flow reactivity}, Lehalle and Laruelle's ``correlation between the number of trades and high frequency volatility'', measures whether a venue keeps trading when volatility rises (sec.~2.3.3, pp.~156--158).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Measures see one face each: ``liquidity cannot be reduced to past traded volumes'' (Lehalle and Laruelle, p.~143), and the spread misses depth. Book size is not a lower bound on executable size: hidden, reserve and replenishing orders can make it exceed the display, and cancellations before an order arrives can make it smaller (\hyperref[def:order-exposure-decisions]{order exposure decisions}). Liquidity risk, uncertainty about the cost of turning a desired position into a trade, and price continuity, the absence of jumps and reversals for ordinary order flow, are working definitions, not Harris's. Forcing continuity against informed flow only transfers losses to liquidity suppliers, an interpretation consistent with adverse selection (\hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{adverse-selection component}) and with Harris's analysis of halts (\hyperref[mech:circuit-breakers-trading-halts]{circuit breakers}).

Market quality is often listed as liquidity, tightness, depth, resiliency, transparency, volatility control and informational efficiency, which adds transparency and volatility control to Harris's five characteristics (\hyperref[def:market-functions]{what markets do}); that these criteria conflict is Harris's point (chap.~9, p.~218).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants} (spread and book size when the tick binds; the bounce statistic); \hyperref[meth:spread-volatility-regression]{spread--volatility regression}; \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}; \hyperref[meth:intraday-deseasonalisation]{intraday deseasonalisation}; \hyperref[def:market-intermediaries]{market intermediaries}; \hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chap.~19; Hasbrouck, chap.~1; Lehalle and Laruelle, secs.~2.2--2.3.

\end{qfdefinition}
