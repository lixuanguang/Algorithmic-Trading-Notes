\section{Orders, limit order books and queues}

\begin{qfmechanism}[Order lifecycle and messages: order states, insert, modify, cancel and execute, private and public channels, and the instructions that change priority]\label{mech:order-lifecycle}
\qffield{Statement} Lehalle and Laruelle's account starts from a book that is ``most often empty'' at the start of the day; orders are stored while they do not match a resting order, and two orders match when their prices are compatible, each match producing one public trade message and two private messages, one to the owner of each order \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.3.1, p.~217]{alpha-laruelle}. Members usually reach the matching engine through two channels: a private one, on which they send their orders and receive the messages that concern them, and a public one, on which everyone connected sees the transactions and the state of the book; the private channel is generally the faster (p.~218). At any time a trader can insert, modify or cancel an order and attach tags to it, such as a time in force (immediate or cancel, good till day, good till time) or a displayed quantity, which creates an iceberg order (p.~218). An order then leaves the book by execution against an incoming order that crosses it, by cancellation, or by expiry; an iceberg's hidden part usually has lower time priority than displayed orders at the same price submitted later, and an immediate-or-cancel order that matches nothing leaves no visible trace \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2, p.~55]{catalogue-0528}. The states an order passes through are those a broker or venue reports for it. Harris calls an order \emph{pending} after submission and before the broker accepts it (for example while an account or a borrow is confirmed) and \emph{working} once accepted and until filled or cancelled \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.2, p.~71]{alpha-harris}. In the FIX~4.4 message standard, OrdStatus (tag~39) ``identifies current status of order'' with the values new, partially filled, filled, done for day, cancelled, pending cancel, stopped, rejected, suspended, pending new, calculated, expired, accepted for bidding and pending replace; there is no value for \emph{replaced}. ExecType (tag~150) ``describes the specific ExecutionRpt \dots\ while OrdStatus (39) will always identify the current order status'': a confirmed replacement is reported with ExecType~5 (Replace) and an OrdStatus such as new or partially filled, and a fill with ExecType~F (Trade) \citep[OrdStatus and ExecType code sets, FIX.4.4\_EP311]{fix44-orchestra}.

\qffield{Notation} \emph{Insert} adds displayed or hidden quantity at a price; \emph{modify} changes price, quantity or instructions; \emph{cancel} removes resting quantity; \emph{execute} is a match against an incoming marketable order. A replacement (cancel-replace) is reported by its execution type, not as an order state. \emph{Working} (resting) and \emph{part-filled} orders have quantity still governed by their instructions; filled, cancelled, expired and rejected orders are terminal.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Sessions.} Whether a book starts empty depends on the venue and on the orders' durations. A day order is valid for the trading day on which it is submitted and expires at the close if unfilled, and most brokers assume a day order when no instruction is given; a good-till-cancel order stays valid until cancelled (some brokers cancel such orders after a month or two), and good-until orders until a stated date \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.9, pp.~82--84, Table~4-3]{alpha-harris}. FIX~4.4's TimeInForce treats an absent value as ``Day (or session)'' and also defines good till cancel, good till date, at the opening, immediate or cancel, fill or kill, good till crossing and at the close \citep[TimeInForce code set]{fix44-orchestra}. Orders carried across sessions therefore rest in the book when it reopens, which a replay must load; the current rules of a named venue (for example how CME Globex carries good-till-cancel and good-till-date orders) are a backlog item, not stated here. Priority after a modification is a venue rule, not a general one. On CME Globex under FIFO matching an order loses its priority and is requeued if its quantity is increased, its price changed or its account changed \citep[page ``Supported Matching Algorithms'', updated 11 February 2025]{ex-cme-matching}; a reduction in quantity is not on that list. Under pro-rata matching, which Bouchaud et al.\ describe as common in short-rate futures, time does not break ties at a price, and each resting order receives a share of an incoming order in proportion to its size \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.1, p.~54]{catalogue-0528}. A rule that applies to one venue, order type or matching algorithm must be stated with that venue and its rule date. Lehalle and Laruelle note in passing that ``inserting a new order will have a better rank than a modified order'' on reaching a matching engine \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.2.1, p.~70]{alpha-laruelle}, without naming a venue.

\qffield{Practice} Limit and stop instructions let a trader who is absent take part: many traders use limit orders to tell their brokers the conditions under which they would trade, and brokers then watch the market on their behalf \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.4.5, pp.~77--78]{alpha-harris}. A stop order becomes an ordinary order once triggered and ``remains valid even if price crosses back over the stop price'', so it can fill well beyond its stop price in a fast market (Harris's cotton example: a stop at 70 cents sold at 66.90; sec.~4.5, p.~78). For a replay, an execution record therefore needs the acknowledgement, every state change and the channel timestamps of each order, not only its fills; the public feed alone cannot show which resting order was the replayer's own.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Slice~33 listed ``replaced'' among FIX~4.4's order states, from a vendor dictionary that itself marked the value as replaced in FIX~4.3; it is an execution type, not a state, and was removed in the slice-33 follow-up, as was an unqualified ``an order book is empty at the start of the day''. The legacy notes said a modification ``loses priority when price worsens or size increases''. On CME's FIFO algorithm any price change loses priority, an improvement included; the legacy rule matches no venue rule read here. Their five ``order states'' (pending, accepted, working, part-filled, terminal) are a simplification of the reported states above, and they named minimum-quantity and cancel-on-disconnect instructions, which were not checked against any venue's rules and are left as a backlog item.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Order instructions: what an order specifies, the standard order types, validity, quantity, display and handling instructions, and how they interact]\label{def:order-instructions}
\qffield{Statement} Orders are trade instructions: they always specify the instrument, quantity and side, and may add conditions, most often limits on price, and also how long the order is valid, when it can be executed, whether partial fills are acceptable, where to present it, how to search for the other side and even with whom to trade \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.1, pp.~68--69]{alpha-harris}. Traders who cannot watch the market must write contingencies into their orders, and lose when old orders trade before they can be cancelled (p.~69). Harris's standard instructions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{market}: trade at the best price currently available; small market orders pay the spread, may receive price improvement from a dealer, large ones have market impact, and all bear execution-price uncertainty from quote changes between submission and execution (sec.~4.3, pp.~71--73);
\item \emph{limit}, including the \emph{marketable} limit order: trade only at the limit price or better (sec.~4.4, pp.~73--78; \hyperref[def:limit-order-book]{limit order book});
\item \emph{stop} and \emph{stop limit}: a stop price activates the order when the market reaches or passes it, after which a stop order is an ordinary market order and a stop limit order an ordinary limit order; ``a stop instruction provides for the activation of an order'', whereas a limit price sets the terms of trade (sec.~4.5.1, p.~79); stop orders demand liquidity when it is least available and add momentum (sec.~4.5.2, p.~79);
\item \emph{market-if-touched}: a market order activated when price falls to the touch price for a buy (rises, for a sell), contrarian, and very rare (sec.~4.6, p.~80, Table~4-2);
\item \emph{tick-sensitive}: a buy downtick order can fill only on a downtick or zero downtick, a sell uptick order only on an uptick or zero uptick; they cannot have market impact, act as limit orders with a limit that follows the last different price, and are most attractive when the tick is large (sec.~4.7, p.~81);
\item \emph{market-not-held}: the broker may use discretion over timing and price and is not accountable for failing to trade, used mostly for large orders (sec.~4.8, p.~82);
\item \emph{validity}: day (the most common, and the default at most brokers), good-till-cancel, good-until a date (good this week, good this month), immediate-or-cancel, good-after, market-on-open and market-on-close (sec.~4.9, pp.~82--84, Table~4-3);
\item \emph{quantity}: all-or-none, which must fill at once, and minimum-or-none (minimum acceptable quantity), each of whose fills must exceed a stated size (sec.~4.10, pp.~83--84);
\item \emph{other}: spread orders, with a limit on the price difference stated as a premium to the buy or sell side; display instructions that cap the displayed size (undisclosed, hidden, reserve or iceberg orders); substitution orders that let the broker choose among securities; and special settlement instructions (sec.~4.11, pp.~84--86).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} FIX~4.4 carries these in separate fields: OrdType (40) for market, limit, stop, stop limit, market-if-touched, pegged and others; TimeInForce (59) for day, good till cancel, at the opening, immediate or cancel, fill or kill, good till crossing, good till date and at the close; and ExecInst (18) for handling instructions such as not held, all or none, do not increase or reduce, and the pegs (market, primary, mid-price, last and others) \citep[OrdType, TimeInForce and ExecInst code sets, FIX.4.4\_EP311]{fix44-orchestra}. A label such as ``hidden pegged IOC'' is shorthand for values in several fields.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Interactions.} An order type and its instructions combine, and the combinations are not all equivalent across sources. Harris lists immediate-or-cancel, good-on-sight and fill-or-kill together as orders valid only on submission, ``in some markets'' called by the other names, and in his Euronext example a fill-or-kill limit order for 60{,}000 shares, sent against a 10{,}000-share display, fills in full from the hidden size behind it, while he says that any unfilled remainder of that order would have been cancelled, which is immediate-or-cancel behaviour in FIX's terms (pp.~83--85); Bouchaud et al.\ and FIX distinguish them: an immediate-or-cancel order executes what it can and cancels the rest, while fill-or-kill (all-or-nothing) executes in full or not at all \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2, p.~55]{catalogue-0528}. A marketable limit order without such an instruction leaves its unfilled remainder resting at its limit, and ``traders who do not want their unfilled orders to stand in the book must attach a fill-or-kill or an immediate-or-cancel instruction'' \citep[chap.~6, sec.~6.4, p.~126]{alpha-harris}. Orders with size restrictions usually rank behind unrestricted orders at the same price (p.~117). A stop limit order activated by a gap through both prices does not trade, and a stop market order that does trade may fill far beyond its stop (pp.~78--80; \hyperref[mech:order-lifecycle]{order lifecycle}). Market-on-open and market-on-close orders participate in the opening or closing auction where there is one (p.~83; \hyperref[mech:order-matching-pricing]{matching and trade pricing}).

\qffield{Practice} The order type is the trader's choice of how to trade off price, certainty and exposure; which orders a venue accepts, and how each behaves at that venue, are its rules, to be read from its rulebook with a date (a backlog item for named venues). Interpretation: in a replay, each order's type, time in force and quantity and display instructions are needed to know whether its remainder rests, where it ranks and whether it could have traded.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy taxonomy treated ``IOC/FOK'' as one category with two meanings; Harris uses the names interchangeably and FIX does not, so the source of a definition must be named. Its ``pegged'' orders are not in Harris's 2002 list; they are FIX~4.4 ExecInst values, and pegging references are venue rules. Harris's special-settlement example assumes U.S.\ regular-way settlement of T+3 (p.~85), as of 2002; it is not current. The legacy rule on tick-sensitive orders is a description of an order instruction, not a market rule; short-sale tick rules are a different matter, not covered here.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Limit order book: best quotes, limit-price placement, visibility, the three event types and how prices change, and quote-driven requests for quotes]\label{def:limit-order-book}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Order type and aggressiveness are different things.} Harris's market order is ``an instruction to trade at the best price currently available'' \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.3, p.~71]{alpha-harris}; a limit order trades only at its limit price or better, and a \emph{marketable} limit order is one that can execute on arrival because its price reaches the opposite best quote, ``like market orders, except that they limit the price concessions'' (sec.~4.4.1, p.~73). Bouchaud et al.\ use a convention for event categories instead: ``orders that match upon arrival are called market orders'' and those that do not are limit orders, so in their counts a marketable limit order is a market order \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.1.3, p.~45]{catalogue-0528}. A crossing limit order can do both: the part that matches executes at once, and ``any portion of $x$ that does not match instead becomes active at the price $p_x$'' (sec.~3.1.2, p.~45), unless an instruction such as immediate or cancel removes the remainder (p.~55). In this entry ``market order'' follows Bouchaud et al.'s convention only where stated. A \emph{limit order book} is the set of all limit orders for an asset on one platform at a given time, its best buy price is the bid $b(t)$, its best sell price the ask $a(t)>b(t)$, with mid-price $m(t)=\tfrac12[a(t)+b(t)]$ and spread $s(t)=a(t)-b(t)$, and the book is a set of queues, one per price level \citep[chap.~3, secs.~3.1.3--3.1.4, pp.~45--47]{catalogue-0528}. In that convention, limit orders supply liquidity and market orders demand it: patient traders meet impatient ones (p.~43). Harris classifies a limit order by its price relative to the market \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.4.1, Table~4-1, pp.~73--74]{alpha-harris}: \emph{marketable} (a buy at or above the best offer), \emph{making a new market} (between the best bid and offer), \emph{at the market} (at the best bid or offer), and \emph{behind} or, further away, \emph{far from} the market; aggressiveness falls in that order. Open-book markets display their books to all traders, closed-book markets do not, and some let traders hide their orders \citep[chap.~5, p.~109]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} Book dynamics are driven by three kinds of event: insertions add liquidity, while cancellations and transactions (by market or marketable limit orders) remove it, and trades can occur only at the best limits \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.3.1, p.~219]{alpha-laruelle}. The bid rises when a buy limit order arrives inside the spread and falls when sells exhaust the volume at the bid; a sell market order larger than the bid queue walks down the book and trades at several prices \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.1.8, p.~51; Table~3.1, p.~53]{catalogue-0528}. Request-for-quote systems differ: a trader asks several market makers, each free to answer, and modern systems aggregate the answers into a book-like view; Lehalle and Laruelle note fixed-income markets' move towards all-to-all RFQs that look like central limit order books \citep[chap.~3, p.~216]{alpha-laruelle}. Interpretation: an aggregated RFQ screen shows conditional, request-specific prices, not firm resting orders, so its depth is not comparable with a book's.

\qffield{Practice} Bouchaud et al.\ measure prices relative to the same-side best quote, $d=b(t)-p_x$ for a buy and $p_x-a(t)$ for a sell, to aggregate order flow across traders (sec.~3.1.6, p.~50). The spread and mid-price are measured as in \hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}, which this entry does not repeat.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Slice~33 first wrote that an order matching on arrival ``is'' a market order; that is Bouchaud et al.'s event convention, not an order type, and was corrected in its follow-up. The legacy taxonomy's ``far from the market: low immediacy cost'' reverses Harris's point: such orders give a better price if they fill but rarely fill. Its ``top-of-book'' and ``depth book'' visibility levels are data products, defined in \hyperref[data:market-data-levels]{market data levels}. A displayed book is not all liquidity: in Bouchaud et al.'s 2015 NASDAQ sample, 3 to 5 percent of market orders matched a hidden order inside the spread (Table~4.1, p.~60).

\begin{qfmechanism}[Matching and trade pricing: order precedence, the matching procedure, pro-rata allocation and its remainders, uniform, discriminatory and derivative pricing, and call-auction uncrossing]\label{mech:order-matching-pricing}
\qffield{Statement} Rule-based order-matching systems arrange trades from submitted orders in the same sequence, in calls and in continuous trading: they rank orders by their \emph{order precedence rules}, match the highest-ranked buy with the highest-ranked sell, trade if the buyer will pay at least what the seller asks, carry the remainder of the larger order to the next match, and price each trade by their \emph{trade pricing rules}; in a call the system makes one attempt after the call, in continuous trading one whenever an order arrives \citep[chap.~6, secs.~6.2--6.2.2, pp.~116--119]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Precedence} is hierarchical: price priority first everywhere, market orders ranking highest; then at least one secondary rule: time precedence (floor time, giving only the first order at a price precedence, or strict time), display precedence (displayed before undisclosed orders at a price, the parts of a partly displayed order treated separately), size precedence (large first in some markets, small first in others) or pro-rata allocation among orders at parity; orders with size restrictions usually rank lower (sec.~6.2.1, p.~117). Some equity exchanges also apply \emph{public order precedence}, forbidding members to trade ahead of a public order at the same price (sec.~6.1.1.3, p.~115). \emph{Pricing}: single-price auctions use the \emph{uniform} rule, one clearing price for all trades; continuous two-sided auctions use the \emph{discriminatory} rule, each trade at the standing order's limit price; crossing networks use the \emph{derivative} rule, a price taken from another market (sec.~6.2.3, p.~120; secs.~6.3--6.5, pp.~120--136).

\qffield{Notation} For a candidate price $p$, supply $S(p)=\sum_iq^s_i\mathbf 1\{s_i\le p\}$, demand $D(p)=\sum_jq^b_j\mathbf 1\{b_j\ge p\}$ (market orders counted at every price), matched volume $\min\{S(p),D(p)\}$ and excess demand $D(p)-S(p)$; the single-price auction clears where the volume is greatest (Harris, sec.~6.3.2, pp.~121--122). Bouchaud et al.\ write the auction price as $\arg\max_pQ(p)$ \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.3, eq.~(3.8), p.~56]{catalogue-0528}. The \emph{surplus} at an auction price is the quantity left unmatched there \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.1.1, p.~122]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Call auctions in European markets.} Lehalle and Laruelle describe four successive rules (pp.~121--122): maximum executable volume; then minimum surplus; then market pressure (all surplus on the buy side: the highest candidate price; all on the sell side: the lowest); then the price nearest a reference price, generally the last traded price; if orders do not cross, no price is formed. Some venues end the call at a random time after its theoretical end; their Table~2.2 gives maxima of 0 seconds for the LSE and Euronext Paris, 30 for Deutsche Börse, 60 for Borsa Italiana and 300 for U.S.\ markets, with variation by day and stock (pp.~122--123); those are as of their study, not current rules. \emph{Surplus and price priority.} The total trader surplus of a trade is the buyer's valuation minus the seller's whatever the price; the price only divides it. If traders bid and offer their valuations, matching by price priority puts every buyer who values the asset above the clearing price with every seller who values it below, so no other arrangement of the same orders yields more total surplus (sec.~6.3.3, pp.~122--125). \emph{Incidence.} For a given set of orders, large impatient traders prefer discriminatory pricing, which fills the first parts of their orders at better prices, and standing limit-order traders prefer uniform pricing; but traders bid more aggressively under uniform pricing, so the comparison overstates the effect, and continuous markets cannot enforce uniform pricing because large traders split their orders (sec.~6.4.2, pp.~129--130). Continuous trading lets traders trade when they want to, a benefit the surplus does not measure (p.~131).

\qffield{Practice} Checked (\nolinkurl{slice34-checks.py}) on Harris's nine-order example (Table~6-1): demand and supply give matched volumes 1, 7, 6 and 4 at 19.8, 20.0, 20.1 and 20.2, so the call clears 7 at 20.0 by the first rule alone; total surplus is 1.6 in the call against 1.0 for the same orders traded continuously (11 units), Harris's Tables~6-7 and~6-9; Bif's market buy averages 20.15 under discriminatory pricing (p.~128). \emph{Pro-rata allocation} fills orders at parity in proportion to size: 18 against resting 10 and 20 gives 6 and 12 (p.~117), and POSIT's 3{,}000 to sell against 5{,}000 and 10{,}000 to buy gives 1{,}000 and 2{,}000 (p.~134). The executable quantity is first capped by the eligible resting liquidity; what an incoming order cannot fill is its own residual, which rests or cancels by its instructions, not a rounding remainder (10 against three resting orders of 3 fills 3, 3 and 3 and leaves 1 of the incoming order unexecuted). When proportional shares of the executable quantity are not whole lots, a rounding remainder must be allocated by a further rule: 10 against three resting orders of 4 gives $\tfrac{10}{3}$ each, floors of 3 and one lot to allocate, and 7 against 1, 1 and 100 floors the small orders to nothing (assertions on capacity and conservation for under-, exactly and oversubscribed cases in \nolinkurl{slice34-checks.py}); which rule applies (time, size, rounding, minimum allocation) is venue-specific and is a backlog item, together with CME's own allocation steps (\hyperref[conv:cme-globex-trading]{CME Globex trading conventions}). \emph{Matching curves.} In Lehalle and Laruelle's study of auction feeds for FTSE~100, FTSE~MIB, SBF~120, DAX and OMXS~30 stocks from 1 January to 30 March 2011, the indicative volume relative to the final volume rises late and convexly in opening calls and early and concavely in closing calls; in the German closing auction, whose book is not shown, the indicative volume exceeds the final one in about a quarter of cases, which they read as orders placed to test the book and then cancelled (pp.~123--127). Indicative prices and volumes are therefore signals, not commitments; CME publishes an indicative opening price in its pre-open (\hyperref[conv:cme-globex-trading]{CME Globex}). \emph{The open-outcry rule} (slice~35). In an oral auction traders ``must publicly express all bids and offers so that all traders can act on them'' and must announce acceptances publicly, which makes participation fair, informs traders of market conditions and protects customers from brokers arranging private trades for friends \citep[chap.~6, sec.~6.1, p.~113]{alpha-harris}. Price priority is self-enforcing; time precedence is not, and ``is meaningful only when the minimum price increment is not trivially small'' (pp.~113--114). The legacy ``rule stack'' (precedence, matching, pricing and access rules) restates Harris's order precedence and trade pricing rules (p.~112) with access added, and ``the same latent supply and demand can produce different prices \dots\ under different rules'' is this entry's incidence point.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes' frequent batch auctions (``within-batch time priority is muted, reducing pure latency value'') come from Budish, Cramton and Shim's proposal, whose batch-auction sections were not read for \hyperref[res:latency-arbitrage-arms-race]{latency arbitrage}; the claim is left as a backlog item of \texttt{hft-market-design}. Their surplus proposition, ``trade the largest $Q$ with $b_Q\ge s_Q$'', is Harris's result for truthful limit prices (an exchange argument), not a property of any auction whatever traders submit. Public order precedence is described for U.S.\ equity exchanges as of 2002, when Harris notes that decimalisation had greatly weakened it (p.~115); it is not stated as a current rule. ``Discriminatory pricing favours aggressive size'' holds only for a given set of orders.

\begin{qfresult}[Standing limit orders are trading options: what sets their value, the two risks of using them, and why fills are adversely selected]\label{res:limit-order-options}
\qffield{Statement} Standing limit orders are options to trade: a sell limit order is a call that lets others buy when they want to, a buy limit order a put that lets them sell, and the strike is the limit price \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.4.2, p.~75]{alpha-harris}. They are options but not option contracts: their writers grant them free to the market and can cancel them, and anyone can exercise one with a market or marketable limit order (p.~75). Their value to others rises with how close the limit price is to the market, how long the order will stand, and volatility; to limit that value, traders place orders further from the market when it is volatile, so spreads are wide in volatile markets (pp.~75--76). Bouchaud et al.\ make the same point, adding that limit orders ``typically earn profits when prices mean-revert but suffer losses when prices trend'' \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.1.3, p.~46]{catalogue-0528}. The legacy formalisation, derived here: for an order of size $q$ at limit $L$, a contra trader with value $V_t$ exercises only when that is worth while, so the exercise value is $q(V_t-L)^+$ for a sell order and $q(L-V_t)^+$ for a buy order, while the order is live.

\qffield{Conditions} The option view needs a contra trader whose value differs from the limit price and who can act before the writer cancels; the payoff is the exerciser's surplus, not the writer's loss on a market that later moves. Two risks follow \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.4.4, p.~77]{alpha-harris}: \emph{execution uncertainty}, when prices move away and the order fails to trade (Harris's example: a buyer who chased the price paid 9 cents more than a market order would have, p.~77), and \emph{ex post regret}, when prices move through the limit and the order fills just before the market moves against the new position. Adverse selection, trading with better-informed traders, is ``the most important cause of ex post regret''. Placing orders further away lowers regret and the fill probability; placing them closer does the reverse.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation, from the passages above: fills are not a random sample of the times an order was available, because informed or faster traders exercise it when it has become cheap, so an evaluation of a passive strategy must measure the price move after its own fills (as in the realised spread of \hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}), not only its fill rate. In a continuous order-driven market the equilibrium spread makes the marginal trader indifferent between limit and market orders; that result, with its conditions, is \hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants}, which this entry does not restate. \emph{Order choice.} Market orders trade at once and pay the spread; limit orders get good prices if they execute and risk failing to trade, after which a trader may pay more than a market order would have cost; ``if the spread is wide, market orders will be expensive \dots\ if the spread is narrow, market orders may be more attractive'' \citep[chap.~14, sec.~14.4, p.~304]{alpha-harris}. Information changes the comparison: Harris's Table~14-1 shows uninformed traders losing to informed ones whichever order they use, a limit order filling when informed traders want the other side and failing when they want the same side (p.~304). Interpretation, derived from explicit cash flows (corrected in the slice-34 follow-up): to buy one unit by a horizon $T$, crossing pays the ask $a_0$ now; posting pays the bid $b_0$ if it fills and otherwise buys at a chase price $c_T$ at the horizon; both strategies then hold the same unit, marked at the same terminal price $P_T$. Posting's gain over crossing is therefore $a_0-b_0=S$ on a fill and $a_0-c_T$ on a miss, and its expectation is
\[ pS-(1-p)M,\qquad M=E[c_T-a_0\mid\text{miss}], \]
with $p$ the fill probability. A price fall after a fill (buying at 99 instead of 100, marked at 97) costs both strategies the same 2 or 3, so it is not a cost of posting: posting still gains 1. Adverse selection enters through $p$ and $M$: fills are likelier when the price is about to fall, and misses when it is about to rise, which makes the chase dear. Checked pathwise (\nolinkurl{slice34-checks.py}): on 200{,}000 random-walk paths the mean gain equals $pS-(1-p)M$ exactly ($-0.046$ with $p=0.43$), while the slice-34 formula $p(S-A)-(1-p)M$, with $A$ the post-fill fall, gives $-0.313$ because it charges that fall to posting alone. A separate $A$ would be justified only as a cost that posting incurs and crossing does not, for example if the strategies end with different quantities. A rule based on spread width alone ignores the trader's own information about whether the quote is stale. Aggressive orders buy certainty and reveal urgency; passive and hidden orders reveal less and bear queue, non-execution and adverse-selection risk (\hyperref[def:order-exposure-decisions]{order exposure decisions}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy proposition gave the payoff ``to the market'' without saying it is exercised at the other trader's discretion and that the writer may cancel; its ``timing option'' mechanism added cancellation speed and monitoring speed, which match Harris's argument about volatility and time to cancel (\hyperref[res:spread-determinants]{spread determinants}) but were not stated as such in the pages read here.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Queue imbalance at the best quotes: two normalisations, its predictive power for the next price move, and its limits]\label{def:queue-imbalance}
\qffield{Statement} With $V_b$ and $V_a$ the volumes at the best bid and ask, Bouchaud et al.\ define the \emph{queue imbalance}
\[ I=\frac{V_b}{V_b+V_a}\in(0,1),\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad QI=\frac{V_b-V_a}{V_b+V_a}=2I-1\in(-1,1), \]
invariant to rescaling both volumes; $I$ near 1 (a much larger bid queue) suggests net buying pressure and an upward move \citep[chap.~7, sec.~7.2, eq.~(7.1), p.~119]{catalogue-0528}. For a large-tick stock whose spread is one tick, the price changes only when one best queue empties, so the next move is a race between the two queues (sec.~7.1, p.~118). Estimating from random times the share of races that end with an up move, they find ``a strong, monotonic correlation between the queue imbalance and the direction of the next price move'' (p.~120). Lehalle and Laruelle state the same fact in queue-reactive terms: ``when the first queue is smaller than its opposite queue, it will most probably be consumed'' \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.3.2, pp.~223--224]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Sample.} Bouchaud et al.'s figure uses two large-tick NASDAQ stocks, INTC and CSCO, in 2015 (chap.~4, p.~58; Table~4.1, p.~60); Lehalle and Laruelle's statistics are for a large-tick stock with an average spread of about 1.3 ticks (p.~227). \emph{Horizon.} The prediction is for the next mid-price move, and Lehalle and Laruelle add that ``the amplitude of this future price move is far lower than a tick'' in most cases, so that no rewarding arbitrage can be built on it alone (p.~224). \emph{Large and small ticks.} The race argument needs a spread pinned at one tick; for small-tick stocks the spread can be filled inside and the argument does not apply as stated.

\qffield{Practice} Checked (\nolinkurl{order-flow-checks.py}): $QI=2I-1$ to machine precision, and both are unchanged when both volumes are scaled. Interpretation: as a model feature it must be bound to a stated forecast horizon and validated on later days and other stocks, since hidden orders, replenishment after depletion and the next queue's size all enter after the next move.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes defined queue imbalance twice, once as $(B_t-A_t)/(B_t+A_t)$ and once in words, with a second claim (``a large opposite queue can make the next price move less likely to reverse immediately'') found in neither source; it is not adopted. Queue imbalance is a state of displayed volume, distinct from the event-based \hyperref[def:order-flow-imbalance]{order-flow imbalance} and from signed trade volume.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:queue-reactive-intensities]{queue-reactive intensities}; \hyperref[def:order-flow-imbalance]{order-flow imbalance}; \hyperref[def:tick-size]{tick size} (large and small ticks).

\qffield{Further reading} The pathway's readings on imbalance-based placement (Lehalle and Mounjid) and on why imbalance predicts prices (Pulido, Rosenbaum and Sfendourakis, an optional branch) are not read here; Bouchaud et al.'s models of the race (secs.~7.3--7.5) are not summarised. The legacy blocks are kept verbatim in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/} and \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/lob-deep-learning/}.

\begin{qfmodel}[Queue-reactive order-book intensities: the Poisson baseline and why it fails, intensities conditioned on queue sizes, the arrival--departure ratio, and the reset that limits mean reversion]\label{mod:queue-reactive-intensities}
\qffield{Statement} Conditioning a variable means specifying the contexts in which it is ``less random''; for an order book, the probability of an insertion at the first limit when it is large, rather than of any insertion anywhere \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.3.2, pp.~219--220]{alpha-laruelle}. The baseline models three intensities, of insertions $\lambda^+$, cancellations $\lambda^c$ and transactions $\lambda^x$, as homogeneous Poisson processes independent of the book; each is estimated by starting at many times $t$, recording the waiting time $\tau-t$ to the next event of that type and averaging, which estimates $1/\lambda$ (p.~221). Simulated with those constant intensities, the book's queue sizes are ``very far away from the reality'' (Figure~3.11, p.~222). Huang, Lehalle and Rosenbaum's \emph{Model~I} keeps one intensity per event type and per size of the queue concerned, measured in average event sizes, by filing each waiting time under the queue size at the start time; Models~II and~III also condition on the sizes of the neighbouring queues, for example $\lambda^c(4,2,1)$ for a first bid of 4 with a second bid of 2 and a best ask of 1 (pp.~222--223).

\qffield{Notation} The \emph{arrival--departure} (A/R) ratio of a queue is its insertion intensity over the sum of its cancellation and transaction intensities; Lehalle and Laruelle read a ratio above one as a queue that grows on average and below one as one that depletes (p.~224). AES: average event size, the average size of an insertion, cancellation or trade.

\qffield{Conditions} In their figures the first queue's ratio is below one at every size and the second's above one, and the book is stable only because, when the first queue empties, the second is promoted and starts to deplete (pp.~224--225; the book is ergodic, n.~13). Two regularities explain why conditioning helps: a first queue smaller than the opposite one is usually consumed, and a large first queue brings more cancellations to the second (pp.~223--224). The statistics are for a large-tick stock with a spread of about 1.3 ticks; with small ticks a queue carries less meaning (p.~227). Interpretation, checked on a birth--death toy (\nolinkurl{order-flow-checks.py}): with intensities that do not depend on size, a ratio of 1.2 gives an upward drift of $+0.09$ per event and 0.8 a drift of $-0.11$; with size-dependent intensities the ratio has to be read at each size.

\qffield{Practice} Liquidity dynamics fitted this way give simulated prices whose volatility is ``far too low'': a newly promoted first queue is large, which pulls the price back, so mean reversion is too strong (pp.~225--226). Lehalle and Laruelle suggest Hawkes processes to add memory, or a reset: with some probability $\theta$, when a first queue depletes, the book is redrawn from the database as if participants accepted the new price (p.~226). The \emph{starting time} of the waiting-time estimator matters: from every second, from just after any event, or just after a trade gives different estimates (p.~221, n.~12). Checked (\nolinkurl{order-flow-checks.py}): for a clustered (Hawkes) sample the mean wait from uniform start times is 0.594 against a mean inter-event time of 0.405. Interpretation, derived: for a stationary renewal process with finite second moment the mean wait from an arbitrary time is $E[X^2]/(2E[X])$, which equals $E[X]$ exactly when the coefficient of variation is 1; that does not single out exponential gaps (gaps of 0.5 with probability 0.8 and 3 with probability 0.2 have $E[X]=1$, $E[X^2]=2$, and a simulated mean wait of 1.0006). The Hawkes sample's gaps have a coefficient of variation of 1.37 and $E[X^2]/(2E[X])=0.579$, close to its measured wait.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The queue-reactive model is a conditional Markov model with exponential waiting times; the guide's catalogue notes that it misses clustered latency races and a trader's longer-lived impact, so a simulator built on it must reproduce both book statistics and policy costs before its fills are trusted. The legacy ``latency race boundary'' (latency matters most when a trader can change queue position, less with wide ticks, deep queues or orders not competing for priority) is interpretation; Lehalle and Laruelle say a small tick shifts attention ``from queuing issues to limit price issues'' (chap.~1, p.~89), and Bouchaud et al.\ that activity at the best quotes is faster for large-tick stocks, reflecting the importance of queue position (chap.~4, p.~59).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:queue-imbalance]{queue imbalance}; \hyperref[mod:hawkes-order-flow]{Hawkes processes for order flow}; \hyperref[def:order-event-point-processes]{point processes and intensities}; \hyperref[mod:static-limit-order-depth]{static limit-order depth}; \hyperref[meth:passive-order-split]{passive order split} (queue position across venues).

\qffield{Further reading} Huang, Lehalle and Rosenbaum (2015) and Cont, Stoikov and Talreja (2010), required readings of \texttt{market-making-lob}, are not read here: the account is Lehalle and Laruelle's. The legacy blocks and the order-flow checks are in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-making-lob/} and \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/market-simulation/}.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Order-flow imbalance: Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov's event-based measure at the best quotes, against signed trade volume, and the family of imbalance features]\label{def:order-flow-imbalance}
\qffield{Statement} For consecutive best-quote states $(P^B_n,q^B_n,P^A_n,q^A_n)$, Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov define the contribution of the $n$th event to supply and demand at the best quotes,
\[ e_n=\mathbf 1\{P^B_n\ge P^B_{n-1}\}q^B_n-\mathbf 1\{P^B_n\le P^B_{n-1}\}q^B_{n-1}-\mathbf 1\{P^A_n\le P^A_{n-1}\}q^A_n+\mathbf 1\{P^A_n\ge P^A_{n-1}\}q^A_{n-1}, \]
so that at an unchanged bid $e_n$ is the change in bid size, a higher bid contributes the new bid size and a lower bid minus the old one, and the ask side enters with opposite signs; the \emph{order-flow imbalance} over $[t_{k-1},t_k]$ is $\mathrm{OFI}_k=\sum_{n=N(t_{k-1})+1}^{N(t_k)}e_n$, which ``encompasses trades, limit orders and cancelations'' \citep[arXiv v3, sec.~2.1, p.~4]{catalogue-0503}. The \emph{trade imbalance} is $\mathrm{TI}_k=\sum(b_n-s_n)$, buyer- minus seller-initiated volume (sec.~4.1, p.~16).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Evidence.} On one month (April 2010) of consolidated NYSE TAQ quotes and trades for 50 S\&P~500 stocks drawn at random, with 10-second intervals and regressions per half-hour, $\Delta P_k=\beta\,\mathrm{OFI}_k+\epsilon_k$ is linear with an average $R^2$ of 65 percent, against 32 percent for $\mathrm{TI}_k$; with both, the trade imbalance is significant in only 31 percent of subsamples (pp.~8 and~16), and the slope is inversely proportional to depth (abstract, p.~1). Timestamps were rounded to the second and quotes aggregated into an NBBO; Level~1 data only (p.~8). \emph{Names.} Bouchaud et al.\ call signed market-order volume $\sum\varepsilon_n\upsilon_n$ the ``order-flow imbalance'' \citep[chap.~11, sec.~11.4, p.~222]{catalogue-0528}; that is Cont et al.'s trade imbalance. A paper's ``OFI'' must be read from its formula.

\qffield{Practice} Checked on a toy sequence (\nolinkurl{order-flow-checks.py}): a market sell of 200 that is replenished, a cancellation of 300 at the ask and a market buy of 100 that empties the ask give $\mathrm{OFI}=+400$ and $\mathrm{TI}=-100$; the two can differ in sign. The legacy list of imbalance features (trade imbalance, best-quote imbalance as in \hyperref[def:queue-imbalance]{queue imbalance}, multi-level depth imbalance, event imbalance by queue, and pressure aggregated across venues) is a menu of constructions, not results; \emph{Multi-level and cross-asset OFI} (read for a source gap of slice~36 in the second slice-37 follow-up). Cont, Cucuringu and Zhang compute, for each of the top $M=10$ levels $m$, a per-event flow $\mathrm{OF}^{m,b}_n$ equal to $q^{m,b}_n$ if the level-$m$ bid price rose, $q^{m,b}_n-q^{m,b}_{n-1}$ if unchanged and $-q^{m,b}_n$ if it fell (and symmetrically on the ask), sum $\mathrm{OF}^{m,b}-\mathrm{OF}^{m,a}$ over each minute, and scale by the average depth of the first ten levels; the \emph{integrated} OFI is the first principal component of the ten scaled OFIs, with weights normalised to sum to one, which explains on average 89.06 percent of their variance (secs.~2.1--2.2, pp.~5--8, Table~2) \citep[arXiv v4, 13 June 2023]{wide-cont-cross-impact-ofi}. Note that when a price moves away the printed per-level formula uses the \emph{new} size where Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov use the old one (a fall in the bid from 300 shares to 500 at the lower price contributes $-500$, not $-300$; second follow-up check script, section~5). Sample: Nasdaq ITCH data from LOBSTER for the 100 largest S\&P~500 stocks, 2017--2019, one-minute bins, 10:00 to 15:30, regressions per non-overlapping 30-minute window and out-of-sample $R^2$ on the next 30 minutes (p.~11). Contemporaneously, the best-level OFI explains 71.16 percent of returns in sample and 64.64 out of sample, the integrated OFI 87.14 and 83.83, and adding other stocks' OFIs (by LASSO) adds little: 73.87 and 66.03 for best-level, 87.85 and 83.62 for integrated (Tables~3 and~5, pp.~12 and~16). For forecasting one-minute-ahead returns, re-estimated on a rolling 30 minutes, every model has a \emph{negative} out-of-sample $R^2$ ($-0.37$ for own best-level OFIs, $-0.10$ with cross-asset terms), though forecast-implied portfolios, before trading costs, earn more with cross-asset terms, and the advantage decays within minutes (Table~8, p.~23; Table~11 and Fig.~10, pp.~26--27); integrated OFIs do not beat best-level ones for forecasting (p.~28). So ``multi-level'' OFI improves contemporaneous fit, not one-minute forecasts. Normalising by volume, average daily volume or depth before comparing stocks is interpretation; Cont et al.'s depth result suggests depth.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes defined ``order flow imbalance'' in words as net buyer- minus seller-initiated activity, then gave signed trade volume as its formula: that is trade imbalance, not the event-based OFI that the pathway's readings use. Their caveat that imbalance ``can predict the next quote move but fail over longer horizons'' was not found in these sources: Cont et al.\ report contemporaneous fits, not forecasts, and on a subsample of stocks the results held for intervals from 10 quote updates to 10 minutes, the fit improving with the interval (p.~8). Any OFI feature used to forecast must be computed only from events before the forecast time.
