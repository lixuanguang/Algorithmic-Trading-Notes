\section{Floor and automated trading systems}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Floor against automated trading systems: two technologies for the same order-driven auction, compared on fairness, access, capacity, speed, information and cost (as of 2002)]\label{def:floor-automated-trading-systems}
\qffield{Statement} Floor-based oral auctions and automated rule-based auctions are both order-driven markets that match buy and sell orders by very similar rules. They differ mainly in technology: in oral auctions traders exchange information in person, in automated markets computers arrange the trades \citep[chap.~``Floor Versus Automated Trading Systems'', p.~544]{alpha-harris}. This entry groups Harris's comparison under six headings; his own sections are fairness, the convenience of distributed access, system capacity, negotiation speed, exchange of information on trading floors, market data reporting and cost (secs.~27.1--27.7, pp.~544--552), and his Table~27-1 lists eleven issues (p.~552, page image):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{fairness}: automated systems apply their rules exactly and produce complete audit trails; oral auctions depend on traders' skill and honesty and need surveillance. Floor traders see and react first, and in pits even size and voice matter, while automated markets favour traders with computer-generated orders (pp.~544--546);
\item \emph{convenience}: distributed access from traders' desks (pp.~546--547);
\item \emph{capacity}: electronic matching scales; oral auctions become disorderly ``fast markets'' when too many traders take part (p.~547);
\item \emph{speed}: automated systems complete and report trades faster, but oral negotiation of size is faster than splitting firm orders, which undisclosed orders and negotiation messaging partly address (pp.~547--548);
\item \emph{information exchange}: floors win when traders need to know who the counterparty is and whether more size is coming, to avoid informed or price-discriminating traders, and when brokers know of latent interest (pp.~548--549). Automated systems report market data faster and more accurately (p.~549);
\item \emph{cost}: both have high initial creation costs; screen-based systems have low operating costs, their main operating costs being telecommunications and data backup. Floor systems are labour-intensive, needing floor brokers, reporters, officials and runners, and well-trained floor brokers are expensive (sec.~27.7, pp.~549--552).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Automated trading also needs heavy investment in reliability, security and speed (pp.~549--551; \hyperref[meth:automated-trading-operational-controls]{operational controls for automated trading}). Automated markets exploit economies of scale, which drives exchange mergers. Because of the order-flow externality, the survival of large floor markets does not prove that floor technology is better (p.~553). Harris expects automated systems to serve active markets and small traders, and floors large institutions that need to exchange information (pp.~552--553).

\qffield{Practice} Harris's examples of competition, such as the Paris Bourse against London and the DTB against LIFFE, did not decide the question, because other events, a repealed French stamp tax and German banks repatriating their market, also moved trading (p.~543). \emph{Technology and reporting} (slice~35). New communications technologies gave traders ``instantaneous presence in markets that they formerly could not attend'', and computing allowed order-matching features impossible by hand, such as orders shown only to committed traders, linked contingent orders and limit prices indexed to market conditions \citep[chap.~``Competition Within and Among Markets'', sec.~26.1, pp.~524--525]{alpha-harris}. Electronic systems collect market information cheaply because it is already electronic, while floors need market reporters \citep[chap.~5, sec.~5.4.1, p.~97]{alpha-harris}, and clearing ``is a trivial exercise when automated order-matching systems arrange all trades'' \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2.1, p.~35]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Capacity} (derived in slice~35). The legacy condition that $N$ traders sending messages at rate $\lambda$ need a processing rate $\mu>N\lambda$ is the stability condition of a single-server queue: necessary, not sufficient. In an M/M/1 queue the mean time in the system is $1/(\mu-N\lambda)$, 2, 10 and 100~ms at utilisations 0.5, 0.9 and 0.99 with $\mu=1{,}000$ messages a second; with average utilisation 0.8 but 10 percent of the time at 1.5 times capacity, the mean wait rises from 3.9 to 12.5~ms and the 99th percentile from 21 to 74~ms; at utilisation 1.05 the backlog grows without bound (\nolinkurl{slice35-checks.py}). Peak, not average, rates decide whether quotes and time precedence stay reliable.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Both systems have high initial creation costs in absolute terms (p.~549), and Table~27-1 rates the automated system's software costs as relatively lower than the cost of building a floor and training its staff (p.~552); the two statements are compatible. The legacy mechanism's ``high fixed costs for software, data networks, security, and recovery, but low marginal labour cost per order'' agrees with the text. Its claim that floors remain valuable ``when negotiation, discretion, and soft information dominate raw matching speed'' paraphrases sec.~27.5. The market facts (the NYSE and most U.S. options exchanges still floor-based, the firms and systems named) are as of 2002 and are not current.
