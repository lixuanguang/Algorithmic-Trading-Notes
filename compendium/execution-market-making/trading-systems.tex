\section{Floor and automated trading systems}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Floor against automated trading systems: two technologies for the same order-driven auction, compared on fairness, access, capacity, speed, information and cost (as of 2002)]\label{def:floor-automated-trading-systems}
\qffield{Statement} Floor-based oral auctions and automated rule-based auctions are both order-driven markets that match buy and sell orders by very similar rules. They differ mainly in technology: in oral auctions traders exchange information in person, in automated markets computers arrange the trades \citep[chap.~``Floor Versus Automated Trading Systems'', p.~544]{alpha-harris}. This entry groups Harris's comparison under six headings; his own sections are fairness, the convenience of distributed access, system capacity, negotiation speed, exchange of information on trading floors, market data reporting and cost (secs.~27.1--27.7, pp.~544--552), and his Table~27-1 lists eleven issues (p.~552):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{fairness}: automated systems apply their rules exactly and produce complete audit trails; oral auctions depend on traders' skill and honesty and need surveillance. Floor traders see and react first, and in pits even size and voice matter, while automated markets favour traders with computer-generated orders (pp.~544--546);
\item \emph{convenience}: distributed access from traders' desks (pp.~546--547);
\item \emph{capacity}: electronic matching scales; oral auctions become disorderly ``fast markets'' when too many traders take part (p.~547);
\item \emph{speed}: automated systems complete and report trades faster, but oral negotiation of size is faster than splitting firm orders, which undisclosed orders and negotiation messaging partly address (pp.~547--548);
\item \emph{information exchange}: floors win when traders need to know who the counterparty is and whether more size is coming, to avoid informed or price-discriminating traders, and when brokers know of latent interest (pp.~548--549). Automated systems report market data faster and more accurately (p.~549);
\item \emph{cost}: both have high initial creation costs; screen-based systems have low operating costs, their main operating costs being telecommunications and data backup. Floor systems are labour-intensive, needing floor brokers, reporters, officials and runners, and well-trained floor brokers are expensive (sec.~27.7, pp.~549--552).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Automated trading also needs heavy investment in reliability, security and speed (pp.~549--551; \hyperref[meth:automated-trading-operational-controls]{operational controls for automated trading}). Automated markets exploit economies of scale, which drives exchange mergers. Because of the order-flow externality, the survival of large floor markets does not prove that floor technology is better (p.~553). Harris expects automated systems to serve active markets and small traders, and floors large institutions that need to exchange information (pp.~552--553).

\qffield{Practice} Harris's examples of competition, such as the Paris Bourse against London and the DTB against LIFFE, did not decide the question, because other events, a repealed French stamp tax and German banks repatriating their market, also moved trading (p.~543). \emph{Technology and reporting}. New communications technologies gave traders ``instantaneous presence in markets that they formerly could not attend'', and computing allowed order-matching features impossible by hand, such as orders shown only to committed traders, linked contingent orders and limit prices indexed to market conditions \citep[chap.~``Competition Within and Among Markets'', sec.~26.1, pp.~524--525]{alpha-harris}. Electronic systems collect market information cheaply because it is already electronic, while floors need market reporters \citep[chap.~5, sec.~5.4.1, p.~97]{alpha-harris}, and clearing ``is a trivial exercise when automated order-matching systems arrange all trades'' \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2.1, p.~35]{alpha-harris}.

\emph{Capacity}. The condition that $N$ traders sending messages at rate $\lambda$ each need a processing rate $\mu>N\lambda$ is, for the M/M/1 queue it implicitly assumes (Poisson arrivals at total rate $N\lambda$, one server with exponential service at rate $\mu$, first in first out, unlimited buffer), necessary and sufficient for stability: the queue has a stationary distribution if and only if $\rho=N\lambda/\mu<1$. Stability says nothing about latency or deadlines. In the stationary M/M/1 queue the mean wait in the queue is $\rho/(\mu-N\lambda)$ and the mean time in the system, wait plus service, $1/(\mu-N\lambda)$; with $\mu=1{,}000$ messages a second these are 1 and 2~ms at $\rho=0.5$, 9 and 10~ms at 0.9, and 99 and 100~ms at 0.99; the time in the system is exponential, so a message misses a deadline $d$ with probability $e^{-(\mu-N\lambda)d}$, 0.135 for $d=10$~ms at $\rho=0.8$.

When the arrival rate varies over time, an average below $\mu$ keeps the backlog from growing from one cycle to the next, but a burst at a rate $\lambda_b>\mu$ lasting $T_b$ builds a backlog of about $(\lambda_b-\mu)T_b$ messages, adding up to $(\lambda_b-\mu)T_b/\mu$ to the delay, which then drains at the rate by which capacity exceeds the arrivals. Whether quotes and time precedence stay reliable therefore depends on how long bursts last, how large a backlog they build, how much buffer there is (a full finite buffer rejects or drops messages) and what deadline matters, not on the average or the peak rate alone.

Simulated with arrivals sampled exactly by thinning: at a constant 800 messages a second the mean wait is 3.94~ms and the mean time in the system 4.94~ms against 4.00 and 5.00 in theory, with 99th percentiles of 20.6 and 21.7~ms against 21.9 and 23.0; with the same average but 1.5 times capacity for the first 100~ms of each second (0.7222 times capacity otherwise; realised average 800.2 a second), the mean wait is 12.53~ms, the mean time in the system 13.53~ms, its 99th percentile 72.9~ms and the share of messages slower than 10~ms 0.343 against 0.131; the backlog peaks near the fluid estimate of 50 messages (a mean wait of 51.7~ms at the end of a burst).

Bursts of 20 and 5~ms at the same peak and average give 99th percentiles of 25.8 and 22.5~ms; a buffer of 20 messages rejects 4.8 percent of messages with the 100~ms bursts against 0.29 percent at a constant rate (0.23 percent in the M/M/1/20 formula). At utilisation 1.05 the wait grows by about 0.05~s per second, to 2.5 and 4.6~s after 50 and 100~s. Drawing each inter-arrival gap from the rate in force at the previous arrival, which is inexact across rate boundaries; its realised rate, 799.8 a second against a stated 798, happened to be close, and it did not separate the wait from the time in the system.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Both systems have high initial creation costs in absolute terms (p.~549), and Table~27-1 rates the automated system's software costs as relatively lower than the cost of building a floor and training its staff (p.~552); the two statements are compatible. Automated systems have high fixed costs for software, data networks, security and recovery but a low marginal labour cost per order, as the text says. Floors remain valuable when negotiation, discretion and soft information matter more than matching speed (sec.~27.5). The market facts (the NYSE and most U.S. options exchanges still floor-based, the firms and systems named) are as of 2002 and are not current.
