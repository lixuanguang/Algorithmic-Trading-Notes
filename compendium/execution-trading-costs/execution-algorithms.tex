\subsection{Execution algorithms, order exposure and portfolio trading}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Automated trading architecture: market data with real-time analytics, two-way venue connections, historical data with recorded messages, and models with an accuracy check]\label{def:automated-trading-architecture}
\qffield{Statement} Lehalle and Laruelle sketch the main components of a broker's automated trading service (Figure~3.1): tick-by-tick and context databases, a calendar of expiries, expected news and half-days, modelling and model selection, a trading framework with a strategic layer, local trading tactics and listeners at each venue, synchronisation, a trading station, a trading database and TCA \citep[sec.~3.1, pp.~193--194]{alpha-laruelle}. The inputs of such tools are four (sec.~3.1.1, pp.~194--197):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{market data}: real-time feeds, consolidated across venues but quick to disaggregate, because it matters which venue currently offers a price. They hold trades, each with a price, a quantity and a timestamp that is ``not always precise enough'', and order books, or the sequence of their modifications, from which the book must be rebuilt on the fly. They are enriched with analytics: the spread, the side that initiated a trade, intraday volatility, an efficient spread and estimates of hidden quantities. Since raw data and analytics are needed together, ``the sooner they are computed, the better'', ideally on hardware as soon as raw data arrive (pp.~194--195);
\item \emph{connections to venues}, which are not natively synchronised with market data, usually being faster, and are two-way. One direction sends new orders, cancellations and modifications; the other returns acknowledgements, rejections of ill-formed messages, cancellations and partial fills. Incoming messages also yield analytics: dark pools publish only trades, so a dark buy order filled in full at a price shows resting sell quantity at that price or better (pp.~195--196);
\item \emph{historical data}: tick-by-tick or order-by-order market data; the analytics already computed in real time; recordings of the messages the firm's algorithms sent and received, to study past performance; and context such as news, corporate events, circuit breakers and venue outages (p.~196);
\item \emph{models}, each with three components. A formula takes parameters and variables: in the impact formula $\kappa\sigma(v/V)^\gamma$ the parameters $\kappa,\gamma$ may be estimated once a day and stored, while the variables $\sigma$, $V$ and $v$ are, in the book's words, ``known in real time''. Interpretation: $V$, the market volume during the trading, is available only as it is observed; volume still to come requires a forecast (see the decision-time item in \hyperref[def:trading-envelope]{trading envelope}). An estimation procedure computes the parameters from historical data. An accuracy computation qualifies the model and its parameters on the immediate past or in real time, so that a model can be deactivated when it is unlikely to be adequate in the current context (pp.~196--197).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Lehalle and Laruelle's practitioner description as of the 2018 printing; Narang's account as of 2024, with his latency figures undated. The inputs feed the components of a trading algorithm: execution parameters, a risk-control layer that sets the trading envelope, and tactics such as smart order routers (\hyperref[def:trading-envelope]{trading envelope}). For latency, some autonomous tactics sit as close as possible to the venues they address, and the risk-control layer at equal distance from all its robots (sec.~3.1.2, p.~198). This describes a broker's execution service for fragmented European equities as of the 2018 printing; it is a design, not a standard or a legal requirement.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: the recorded messages and context are what later make it possible to replay an execution and explain its performance (\hyperref[meth:execution-decision-support]{decision support}, \hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}). The accuracy computation is a model-monitoring control: governance of models and of the systems that run them is in \hyperref[meth:automated-trading-operational-controls]{operational controls for automated trading}.

Narang's version of the same infrastructure: connectivity, a message protocol, and hardware and software, each built or bought \citep[chap.~7, ``Execution'', sec.~7.2, pp.~138--140]{s7-narang}. Most traders use brokers as trading agents, ``due to regulatory and other constraints'', and reach venues by direct market access through the broker's servers, which he says adds 10 to 30 milliseconds against a fraction of a millisecond from colocated servers (p.~139). The FIX protocol, begun in 1992 between Fidelity and Salomon Brothers, is the standard for order and execution messages; he calls the protocol ``free and open source'' and distinguishes it from the FIX engines that implement it, which differ in quality and which latency-sensitive traders often build themselves (pp.~139--140). Buying execution algorithms can cost ``five or more times as much per share'' as running one's own (pp.~140--141).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The FIX protocol is free and open, but FIX engines vary and need not be open source (Narang; \nolinkurl{s6-fix}). Narang's chapter does not cover sponsored access; for U.S. broker-dealers market access is governed by Rule 15c3-5 (\hyperref[meth:automated-trading-operational-controls]{operational controls}). Narang's latency figures are undated estimates. That the hard part is keeping the decision chain coherent under fragmented venues and live latency, rather than any one model, is a synthesis, not a statement in the source.

Several items are not in the source: holidays, auctions, index events and expected halt days in the calendar (the figure shows expiries, expected news and half-days); book imbalance and queue sizes among the fast analytics; and public book updates as part of the venue connection, where the source keeps the private order messages apart from the market-data feed and notes that the two are not synchronised. Venue names, data and latencies are as of the 2018 printing.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:trading-envelope]{trading envelope}; \hyperref[meth:execution-decision-support]{decision support}; \hyperref[def:smart-order-routing]{smart order routing} (dark probes, latency); \hyperref[meth:automated-trading-operational-controls]{operational controls for automated trading}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Trading algorithm: execution parameters, a risk-control layer that sets a trading envelope, and tactics inside it]\label{def:trading-envelope}
\qffield{Statement} Lehalle and Laruelle describe a trading algorithm, instantiated from a class such as VWAP, TWAP, implementation shortfall, target close or liquidity seeking, as three components \citep[sec.~3.1.2, pp.~197--198]{alpha-laruelle}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{execution parameters}: side and quantity, the benchmark (VWAP, TWAP, percent of volume, entry price, close price), start and end times, bounds on the trading rate (maximum and minimum percent), aggressiveness, access to dark pools;
\item a \emph{risk-control layer} that computes the \emph{trading curve}, a minimum and maximum trading rate, from models of market impact, market risk and alpha and from ``expected market contexts'', balancing impact against market risk and its adverse selection;
\item \emph{trading robots} or liquidity-seeking tactics, such as a smart order router, that navigate inside the envelope set by the risk-control layer using the immediate state of liquidity; the risk-control layer assigns them short-term goals.
\end{itemize}
Because the analytics behind the curve are noisy, the curve is thick: a \emph{trading envelope}, inside which no trajectory is a priori more efficient than another for risk control. An algorithm trading faster than its curve stops posting; one trading too slowly trades aggressively, paying the spread \citep[sec.~3.4.1, pp.~231--232]{alpha-laruelle}. Envelopes are shown for a VWAP and an implementation-shortfall algorithm at several risk levels (Figures~3.13--3.14, pp.~232--233).

\qffield{Notation} Interpretation: the envelope bounds the cumulative executed quantity, or equivalently its rate, as a function of time, as plotted in Figure~3.2 (p.~199) and Figure~3.13 (p.~232). It applies to the parent order; child-order size, price and venue are the tactics' decisions.

\qffield{Conditions} Practitioner descriptions as of 2014 and 2018; broker definitions vary. How each algorithm class pursues its benchmark:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Benchmark against policy.} VWAP and TWAP name both a price statistic and a policy (\hyperref[def:execution-benchmark]{execution benchmarks}). Kissell's VWAP policy trades in proportion to the intraday volume curve and his TWAP policy evenly in time; both are static schedules fixed for the day; his POV policy trades a set fraction of market volume, so it reacts to realised volume but does not guarantee completion, and some brokers add a minimum rate to finish by the end time \citep[chap.~1, pp.~16--17]{s7-kissellscience}. Arrival-price and implementation-shortfall algorithms solve a cost--risk optimisation, the latter adding real-time adaptation to volumes and prices; their meaning differs across brokers, as does the scale of the risk-aversion parameter \citep[same chap., pp.~17--19]{s7-kissellscience}.
\item \emph{Information at decision time.} Model parameters, such as $\kappa$ and $\gamma$ in the impact term $\kappa\sigma(v/V)^\gamma$, are estimated about once a day from history; the variables $\sigma$, $V$ and $v$ are fed in real time \citep[sec.~3.1.1, p.~197]{alpha-laruelle}. Interpretation: $V$, the market volume during the trading, is known only as it occurs, so a schedule fixed at the start uses a forecast of it, and a policy that adapts uses only the volume observed so far.
\item \emph{Participation.} Interpretation: neither source says whether the market volume a participation rate $\rho$ applies to includes the order's own fills. If it does, the order trades $\rho/(1-\rho)$ of other traders' volume (25 percent at $\rho=20$ percent); if not, $\rho$ of it, which is $\rho/(1+\rho)$ of the total (16.7 percent). Kissell's participation-weighted price, the volume-weighted price of the first (shares traded)$/\rho$ shares, counts the order's own fills in the window (p.~108). The measurement window, the denominator and auction volume (often skipped by TWAP, p.~17) need stating.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Practice} Benchmarks can be combined by constraints: minimum or maximum participation rates make an algorithm behave like PoV when volume is very high or low; a limit price and a ``would level'' make it behave like implementation shortfall; a smart order router makes it resemble a liquidity seeker. Such hard combinations are bang-bang controls, applied fully once a threshold is crossed; they can be gamed if the threshold is detected, or adversely selected, finishing too early because of the constraints, if the conditions are not chosen accurately.

Building the logic on envelopes avoids this. Customising then has two steps: inject the desired risk profile and any volume or price constraints into the envelope, and plug in tactics suited to the investment style under defined market conditions, such as ``go into dark pools if volatility increases'' \citep[sec.~3.4.1, pp.~231--233]{alpha-laruelle}. The equations for optimal envelopes are cited there to Bouchard et al.\ (2011), Lehalle (2012) and Guéant et al.\ (2012).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Lehalle and Laruelle refer to Appendix~A.6 for the trading curve (p.~197), but A.6 is the Harris tick-size model; the scheduling appendix is A.7 (Interpretation, from the table of contents and p.~266). The caption of Figure~3.14 reads ``for a VWAP (left) or a (right) trading algorithm'', with the second name missing; the text calls the pair a VWAP and an IS (p.~232). In the source a too-fast algorithm stops posting. In its examples the would level turns an algorithm into IS and the SOR into a liquidity seeker.

Orders sent to dark pools come back as probes of their hidden books, and a firm records the messages its algorithms send and receive, with market data and context, to study their performance \citep[sec.~3.1.1, p.~196]{alpha-laruelle}. Interpretation: the envelope is a risk-control construct; following it says nothing about the cost against a benchmark measured afterwards.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:almgren-chriss-execution]{mean--variance execution} (a model for the trading curve); \hyperref[meth:algorithm-benchmark-choice]{choosing an algorithm's benchmark}; \hyperref[def:execution-benchmark]{execution benchmarks}; \hyperref[meth:execution-decision-support]{pre-trade and monitoring tools}; \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact} (the models the risk-control layer uses, developed by \nolinkurl{price-impact-execution}).

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Decision support for trading algorithms: pre-trade analytics, monitoring indicators and what-if scenarios]\label{meth:execution-decision-support}
\qffield{Statement} Before launch, pre-trade analytics use historical data, models and parameters to estimate and compare expected cost components (bid--ask spread, usual traded volume, usual quantity on the book), expected trading profiles and expected context (news, volatility levels), broken down by sector, currency and country; on portfolios they check consistency and speed some lines up and slow others down from the expected balance of market risk and impact \citep[sec.~3.1.3, pp.~198--199]{alpha-laruelle}. During trading, monitoring indicators let a trader, who may watch several hundred algorithms, find where poor performance concentrates; performance indicators estimate the result if trading stopped now or continued under unchanged conditions, from a decomposition of the average price into immediate price, market moves and impact (pp.~200--201).

What-if scenarios give real-time estimates of changed execution conditions, such as finishing now by taking the visible liquidity or converting an implementation-shortfall algorithm to percentage of volume (p.~201). Pre-trade, monitoring and post-trade tools should be compatible: for an indicator such as the bid--ask spread, pre-trade gives its expected average, monitoring its current value, updated expectation and likelihood, and post-trade its value during trading and its influence on performance, with breakdowns by sector, currency, country and side (p.~202). Monitoring indicators are built for human decisions, such as changing execution parameters on the fly, and can also be used by models to identify causes of poor performance (p.~200).

Because users must share the indicators' values with the algorithms, the algorithms themselves publish them as \emph{indicator publishers}, publishing views on their own state in real time to users and companion algorithms (p.~202).

\qffield{Conditions} Practitioner description as of the 2018 printing. Some what-if outcomes are simple formulas, others are estimates (p.~201). Pre-trade figures are forecasts made before the order. The average-price decomposition ``follows'' the scheduling appendix's (A.26) (p.~201), but (A.26) is an ex-ante objective, $\mathbb E(W)+\lambda\operatorname{Var}(W)=v^\star S_0+\kappa\sum_n\sigma_nv_n^{\gamma+1}/V_n^\gamma+\lambda\sum_nx_n^2\sigma_n^2$, labelled immediate buy, market impact and market risk \citep[app.~A, sec.~A.7.2, p.~279]{alpha-laruelle}: its third term is a variance penalty, not a price move.

Interpretation: the realised counterpart, from the book's change of variable (p.~278), is the identity $W=v^\star S_0+\sum_nx_n\sigma_n\xi_n+\kappa\sum_n\sigma_nv_n^{\gamma+1}/V_n^\gamma$, dividing by $v^\star$ for the average price; it holds exactly inside the model, with $x_n$ the quantity remaining from slice $n$ on, in the book's timing. On real executions the impact term is a model value and the market-move term what is left, so the split is model-dependent. The refined version, adding planned price, unexpected market moves and trading efficiency, is given in words only (p.~201), so its additivity is not shown.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: pre-trade estimates belong with the expected cost and risk of a schedule (\hyperref[mod:almgren-chriss-execution]{mean--variance execution}); realised performance belongs with \hyperref[def:implementation-shortfall]{implementation shortfall}. The average-price decomposition is the post-trade side of \hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}; its accounting counterpart without a model is the split in \hyperref[def:transaction-costs]{transaction costs}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Synchronisation for portfolios with common constraints, such as the same end time or constant exposure to a factor, comes after the pre-trade analysis (p.~200). The pre-trade what-if examples are finishing now and switching from IS to PoV. The immediate price is $S_0$, the price of buying everything at once without impact, and its impact term is the model's value, not an observed causal effect. The pre-, live- and post-trade principle follows p.~202.

The book calls trading algorithms ``stateless agents'' with introspection (p.~202) while having them publish views on their own state (pp.~200--202): its point is introspection and shared indicators.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:trading-envelope]{trading envelope}; \hyperref[def:execution-benchmark]{execution benchmarks}; \hyperref[def:implementation-shortfall]{implementation shortfall}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Order exposure decisions: how a large trader shows, splits and routes an order, and the reactive--proactive continuum]\label{def:order-exposure-decisions}
\qffield{Statement} Order submission strategy is the most important determinant of execution quality that traders control: market against limit orders, where to place limits, and when and how to resubmit. Large traders must also decide whether to look for the other side or wait for it, whether to show their full interest or hide it, whether to split the order over time, whether to use one broker or several to hide their total interest, and whether to trade in one market or many; display decisions are the most important a large buy-side trader makes \citep[chap.~``Buy-Side Traders'', p.~380]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Reactive} traders trade in response to opportunities others present; \emph{proactive} traders articulate their interests. Proactive traders expose orders to attract reactive ones, and two reactive traders never trade with each other (same chap., sec.~18.3, pp.~383--384).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Benefits and costs of exposure.} Display makes buyers and sellers easy to find and attracts reactive traders with latent interest; its costs come from how others respond \citep[chap.~``Buy-Side Traders'', secs.~18.2--18.3, pp.~382--383]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Parasitic traders}, order anticipators, use the exposed information to profit at the exposed trader's expense; \emph{defensive liquidity suppliers} use it to step out of the way of a large trader who would otherwise price-discriminate against them, so that the large trader pays more for liquidity (p.~383). Exposure can reveal three things: why the trader trades, the price impact of future trades, and valuable trading options (sec.~18.4, p.~384). Small traders rarely need to care, unless many small orders on one side add up to a large one (p.~383).

Cost is the main reason to be reactive: proactive traders issue instructions before knowing whether an opportunity will arise, which is wasteful when opportunities are rare, but reactive traders risk that proactive ones act first (p.~383). The reactive--proactive continuum is the willingness to bear search costs; the aggressive--passive continuum is the eagerness to trade. They are similar, not the same: aggressive traders tend to be proactive, and traders who can search cheaply tend to be proactive (p.~384).

\qffield{Practice} Evasive strategies: brokers to preserve anonymity, several brokers so that none knows the full interest, anonymous electronic systems, careful choice of whom to show first (the art of block trading), showing only part of the order, undisplayed orders, order indications that carry no commitment, and waiting for another trader's firm order and taking it \citep[same chap., sec.~18.5.1, pp.~386--387]{alpha-harris}. Deceptive and offensive strategies are costly and may be illegal in some jurisdictions (pp.~387--389).

\emph{Narang's execution algorithms.} Narang frames an algorithm's decisions as how aggressive to be, which order type to use, what size to send and where to send it, with the goals of completeness and cheapness \citep[chap.~7, ``Execution'', sec.~7.1, pp.~129--130]{s7-narang}. Market orders and marketable limit orders (a buy at the best offer, a sell at the best bid) are aggressive; a buy at or below the best bid, or a sell at or above the best offer, is passive. A passive order controls its price but may not fill, or fill only in part, and suffers adverse selection when it does fill (pp.~130--133). Liquidity rebates sweeten a passive order only if it executes, and some venues invert the fees (pp.~132--133).

He finds aggressiveness ``generally'' higher for momentum strategies and lower for mean reversion, and higher for stronger, more confident signals; a limit order inside the spread, possible only when the spread exceeds the minimum tick, is a middle ground (p.~133). Algorithms may also price off a ``micro price'' that reflects the order book's imbalance (pp.~133--134). A hidden order ``provides no information to the market'', in Narang's words, meaning that its size and price are not displayed before it trades; it loses priority to displayed orders at the same price on most exchanges, and not all exchanges allow them; iceberging posts most of a large order as hidden pieces (pp.~131 and~134--135).

Harris describes display instructions (undisclosed, hidden, reserve or iceberg orders): the broker fills the displayed part first and then shows more, up to the maximum display; at Euronext in 2002 traders could discover the full size of an undisclosed order only by submitting large marketable fill-or-kill orders \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.11.2, pp.~84--85]{alpha-harris}. Under display precedence, displayed orders rank ahead of undisclosed ones at the same price, the displayed and undisclosed parts of one order being treated separately (chap.~6, p.~117); Bouchaud et al.\ say an iceberg's hidden part ``usually has lower time priority'' \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2, p.~55]{catalogue-0528}. Whether a refreshed slice keeps or loses time priority is a venue rule. Interpretation: hiding removes the pre-trade display, not all leakage.

Executions are still printed and can be read, and the SEC's 2010 release describes immediate-or-cancel ``pinging'' orders used to find undisplayed liquidity, including to detect and trade in front of large interest \citep[p.~47, n.~69]{sec-concept-release-2010}; that is the order-anticipation risk in \hyperref[def:order-anticipation]{order anticipation}. A hidden order lowers, rather than removes, what others can infer. Splitting a large order into pieces over time trades impact against the risk of an adverse move, with piece size set from the cost model (pp.~136--137). An algorithm's detectable ``footprint'' lets others predict it and raise its costs (pp.~129--130). Routing is in \hyperref[def:smart-order-routing]{smart order routing}.

\emph{How markets protect exposure}. Harris lists the rules: time precedence, with a large enough tick that front runners must pay to step ahead; undisclosed orders, which others discover only by committing to trade, searchable with fill-or-kill orders that reveal nothing if they fail; audit trails and, in some markets, prohibitions on dual trading against broker front running; and delayed trade reporting for traders building or unwinding positions, which ``can greatly increase transaction costs for many types of traders'' and is commoner in dealer markets \citep[chap.~``Buy-Side Traders'', sec.~18.6, p.~389]{alpha-harris}. His sidebar reports that at the Paris Bourse and the Toronto Stock Exchange in 1994--1995 traders submitted more undisclosed orders when the tick was a small fraction of price (p.~389).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Proactive execution is not the same as crossing the spread: Harris separates search (reactive--proactive) from eagerness (aggressive--passive), and a proactive trader may post a limit order. Reactive execution lowers information leakage in Harris's evasive strategy of not exposing until trading (p.~387), but he states it as a strategy, not a regularity. Discretion ranges, protection prices, pegging and minimum fills are not in the source. Systems and venues named (Autex, POSIT, Island and others) are as of 2002. Aggressiveness is side-dependent: a limit order at the current best is fairly aggressive and one beyond it passive, so for a buy the comparison is with the offer and the bid, with the mirror image for a sale.

Fee and rebate levels, hidden-order priority and the availability of order types are venue rules; Narang notes that order types are created and retired often (pp.~134--135). Narang's third edition treats cancellation as routine queue management and defends high cancellation rates (chap.~14, sec.~14.1.3, p.~270; Appendix, sec.~A.2.2, pp.~314--315). The chapter does not discuss placing orders without intending them to execute in order to move others' view of the market, such as small sell orders entered away from the market and cancelled by a trader who wants to buy.

For trading on or subject to the rules of a registered entity under the Commodity Exchange Act (in practice futures and swaps venues; this gloss is Interpretation), U.S. law makes unlawful any practice ``of the character of, or \dots\ commonly known to the trade as, `spoofing' (bidding or offering with the intent to cancel the bid or offer before execution)'' \citep[paragraph~(a)(5)(C), 2024 edition]{usc-7-6c}. Its status for securities and in other jurisdictions is not settled here; the passage is kept as a description of conduct, not a technique.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:block-trade]{block trades} (order exposure as one of the four problems; sunshine trading); \hyperref[def:dark-pools-crossing]{dark pools and crossing networks}; \hyperref[def:order-instructions]{order instructions} (display and quantity instructions); \hyperref[def:order-anticipation]{order anticipation} (the parasitic traders exposure attracts); \hyperref[def:trading-envelope]{trading envelope} (tactics inside an algorithm). Fragmentation and dark venues are developed by \nolinkurl{market-structure-execution-quality}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Portfolio trading: program trades, the firm bid/offer market for packages, basket algorithms and index-fund trading]\label{def:portfolio-trading}
\qffield{Statement} A \emph{program trade} is the simultaneous submission of many orders; for statistics the NYSE and SEC counted trades of 15 or more coordinated transactions worth \$1 million or more, about 27 percent of NYSE volume; index arbitrageurs did about 9 percent of the reported program-trade volume, about 2.4 percent of total NYSE volume \citep[chap.~``Index and Portfolio Markets'', p.~489]{alpha-harris}.

In the \emph{firm bid/offer market}, also called the package, basket or portfolio trading market, package dealers quote firm prices for a whole portfolio from a characterisation of it, usually relative to its end-of-day value (for example the close minus 15 cents a share), and the list is revealed only after the close \citep[same chap., sec.~23.4.1, p.~490]{alpha-harris}. A \emph{basket algorithm} manages cost against total basket risk, using correlations, rather than each stock's own risk; constraints include cash balancing, self-financing and minimum and maximum participation \citep[chap.~1, pp.~18--19]{s7-kissellscience}.

\qffield{Conditions} The characterisation states whether the portfolio is an index portfolio and, if not, summary statistics: quantities, firm sizes, betas, price levels, average trading volumes, volatilities, primary listings and index membership (p.~490). Dealers price portfolios better than single stocks because informed traders are less likely to trade portfolios; clients pay them to assume the trading problem, and dealers may place parts with other clients (pp.~490--491).

Index products (index futures, index options and shares in index funds) let traders trade index risk more cheaply than the components, for three reasons Harris gives: index dealers rarely face well-informed traders, because few have valuable insight into the whole market; index markets are very active, so counterparties are easy to find and dealers turn over inventory quickly; and a single transaction is arranged, cleared and settled instead of many \citep[same chap., p.~484 and sec.~23.4, p.~489]{alpha-harris}. Index prices generally lead the cash index, because index traders price only index risk (p.~489).

\qffield{Practice} Index funds rebalance a value-weighted portfolio only when the index list changes, and a price-weighted one also at splits \citep[same chap., sec.~23.2, p.~486]{alpha-harris}. They can improve returns by supplying rather than demanding liquidity, substituting non-index stocks for expensive components, rebalancing only with flows and dividends, and holding a subset of the index; each tactic raises tracking error, so many funds use them sparingly (p.~487). A basket algorithm may slow a stock despite good liquidity, or speed one despite poor liquidity, if that reduces residual basket risk (Kissell, pp.~18--19).

Narang describes the same service as a \emph{portfolio bid}: the trader describes a ``blind'' portfolio by its characteristics, such as the valuation ratios of longs and shorts, sector breakdown and market capitalisations; brokers quote a fee in basis points of the gross market value traded and guarantee the price, and the broker then bears the risk of trading out of the portfolio at future prices \citep[chap.~7, ``Execution'', p.~128]{s7-narang}. ``Human'' execution of quant portfolios ``often looks like'' a portfolio bid (p.~128).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Basket, package and portfolio trades are not different markets: Harris uses the three names for one market. Tight index-basket quotes come, in Harris, from less adverse selection; diversified residual risk may also help but is not in the passage (Interpretation). Harris names non-index stocks as substitutes; sector, currency, country, factor loadings and borrow constraints as characterisation inputs, and futures and ETFs as rebalance substitutes, go beyond him, and the timing around reconstitution is in \hyperref[ev:index-reconstitution-impact]{index reconstitution}. Index flow is often said to cost less to trade because it is less security-specific, more active, easier to net and easier to hedge with futures, ETFs, options or baskets.

The first two match Harris's adverse-selection and activity reasons; netting and hedging are not among his reasons, and his third is that a package is a single transaction to arrange and settle. ``Portfolio transition'' is named by Kissell (``transition management'', p.~16) but not defined. The NYSE figures are as of 2002.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:block-trade]{block trades} (single-stock size); \hyperref[ev:index-reconstitution-impact]{index reconstitution} (price pressure from forced index trading); \hyperref[mech:etf-creation-redemption]{ETF creation and redemption}; \hyperref[def:trading-envelope]{trading envelope}; \hyperref[meth:execution-decision-support]{pre-trade analytics} (portfolio consistency checks).

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmechanism}[Positive-feedback hedging: portfolio insurance sells into falls and buys into rises, and its trades reinforce the move]\label{mech:positive-feedback-hedging}
\qffield{Statement} Portfolio insurance is a dynamic trading strategy that replicates a portfolio plus a put option; the formula for how much to trade comes from the Black--Scholes model and depends on the portfolio's volatility, the protection period and the guaranteed minimum value. Insurers buy when prices rise and sell when they fall, the same effect as stop orders, which many used. It works when the money covered is small, but when a large fraction of the market is insured, sellers arrive together and prices can fall substantially; order anticipators sell at the first sign of a fall and buyers withdraw, so expected insurance selling can drive prices down even before it occurs \citep[chap.~``Bubbles, Crashes, and Circuit Breakers'', sec.~28.2.2, pp.~560--561]{alpha-harris}.

Interpretation: if a rule holds $q(S)$ shares with $q'(S)>0$, a fall $dS<0$ gives $dq=q'(S)\,dS<0$, a sale; if each trade moves the price by $\lambda\,dq$ with $\lambda>0$, the induced move has the sign of the original one.

\qffield{Conditions} Interpretation: if the induced move persists until the rule trades again and triggers the same response, the moves form a geometric series with ratio $\lambda q'(S)$, which is dimensionless ($\lambda$ in currency per share, $q'$ in shares per unit of currency); the total is $dS/(1-\lambda q'(S))$ for $\lambda q'<1$, for example $1.67\,dS$ at $\lambda q'=0.4$. Purely temporary impact that reverts before the next rebalance gives no such amplification. Harris notes that exchange-listed puts would have made the insured amount visible through open interest and reduced the uncertainty that kept buyers away (p.~561).

\qffield{Practice} Harris's rough sizing for 1987: about \$100 billion insured, a typical delta of about 0.4, so a 1 percent fall would require selling about 0.4 percent, roughly 10 million shares at \$40, against average daily volume of 160 million shares (box, p.~561). Insurers who raised the protected value as markets rose had to sell more on the way down (box, p.~560).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Interpretation: the size of a rebalancing trade depends on how much the hedge ratio changes, the option's gamma, not on its delta. In a Black--Scholes illustration with zero rates, 20 percent volatility and one year to expiry, a 1 percent fall requires selling about 1.0, 1.6 and 1.8 percent of the insured value for protection at 80, 90 and 100 percent of the current value. Harris's box multiplies the fall by the delta, so its 10 million shares is an order-of-magnitude guess on a different basis. The argument shows only the sign of the effect, not its size or stability.
