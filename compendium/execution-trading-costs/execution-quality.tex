\subsection{Best execution, broker conflicts and execution review}

Numbers in \hyperref[meth:trade-execution-evaluation-governance]{execution evaluation and governance} are checked in \texttt{notes/artefacts/trade-execution/trade-execution-checks.py}; market costs in its original examples are stated assumptions, not quotes.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Best execution: three standards, the dimensions of execution quality, and why retail clients see commissions but not prices]\label{def:best-execution}
\qffield{Statement} A broker taking an order assumes an agency responsibility to obtain best execution, which is not well defined. To unsophisticated clients it means the best possible price for a market order and the fastest execution for a limit order, an absolute standard, which is what the term generally means in U.S. equity markets; to more sophisticated traders, the execution they pay for; to the most sophisticated, the execution they can expect given what they pay and how well they can audit it \citep[chap.~``Brokers'', sec.~7.4.2, pp.~160--161]{alpha-harris}.

Price is one dimension of execution quality and speed another; market-order traders care most about speed, limit-order traders about price, and best execution for standing limit orders depends on whether and when they execute \citep[chap.~``Internalization, Preferencing, and Crossing'', sec.~25.1, p.~515]{alpha-harris}. Kissell defines best execution as the process of choosing the strategy most likely to achieve the fund's investment objective, judged on the information available when trading began, not by closeness to a benchmark price \citep[chap.~3, p.~88]{s7-kissellscience}.

\qffield{Conditions} In U.S. equities as Harris describes them in 2002, dealers claim best execution when they fill marketable orders at the NBBO, a standard that applies only to orders smaller than the displayed size; some markets also expect average price improvement for small market orders (p.~515). Brokers that internalise or accept payment for order flow have a conflict of interest and must regularly and rigorously examine execution quality; SEC Rules 11Ac1-5 and 11Ac1-6 required market centres to publish monthly execution-quality statistics and brokers quarterly routing reports (pp.~517--518, boxes).

Retail traders can audit commissions and response times easily but not price quality, which needs trade and quote data and norms for comparison; so brokers compete on commissions, and in competitive markets the total of spread and commission for small market orders does not depend on how best execution is defined (pp.~517--518). Lehalle and Laruelle contrast two regimes as of 2018: in the U.S. the trade-through rule, they write, makes venues reroute orders marketable at a better price elsewhere, which needs a consolidated pre-trade view; in Europe the executing broker chooses how to consolidate access to liquidity and must disclose and justify a best-execution policy, raising the cost of fragmentation for intermediaries \citep[sec.~3.4.2, p.~234]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Practice} Retail execution quality is measured by price against the NBBO, the midpoint or the effective spread (\hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}), speed, whether and when limit orders execute, and commissions; audit firms compare each night's trades with quotes and report exceptions (Harris, pp.~515--519). Interpretation: these measures depend on order type and size and on the quotes at the time; comparisons between venues need like orders.

\emph{Best execution after fragmentation}. In Lehalle and Laruelle's account MiFID left each broker to define best execution in its own policy, and best execution focused on ``immediate price improvement'' for aggressive orders from snapshots of visible books; after fragmentation they argue it must cover consolidated access for passive and aggressive orders, with adverse selection, passive splits and reversal as measures judged over the order originator's horizon, and must account for clearing and settlement costs that differ by venue \citep[Introduction, pp.~17--19]{alpha-laruelle}; in Europe, best-execution policies took the place of the U.S.\ trade-through rule \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.1.2, p.~43]{alpha-laruelle}. Execution quality therefore depends on fees, fill probability, latency, hidden liquidity, access, size and horizon as well as displayed price.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Three obligations should not be merged: \emph{quote protection}, \emph{best execution} and \emph{execution-quality reporting}. Quote protection in the U.S. is the order protection rule, 17 CFR 242.611 (eCFR text for 13 October 2026; adopted 70 FR 37620, 29 June 2005; last amended 89 FR 26616, 15 April 2024): it requires a \emph{trading center} to maintain written policies reasonably designed to prevent trade-throughs of protected quotations in NMS stocks, and to surveil them, with exceptions including system failure, non-regular-way trades, single-priced opening, reopening and closing transactions, crossed protected quotations, intermarket sweep orders (executed, or routed simultaneously against the full displayed size of protected quotations), orders whose price is not based on the quote, venues whose best quote within the previous second was equal to or worse than the trade-through price, and stopped customer orders (paragraphs (a)--(b)(9)).

It is a duty of the venue, not the broker's best-execution duty, and it does not oblige a venue to reroute: an intermarket sweep order lets the router take the protected quotations itself. Lehalle and Laruelle's claims that U.S. clearing and settlement fees must be equal across venues, and their description of the European regime, are as of their 2018 printing. U.S. routing leans on protected consolidated quotes, while European brokers choose the consolidation layer (their p.~234). Defining best execution as the best auditable outcome given the client's objective, limit, urgency, available venues and information risk merges Harris's third standard (auditability) with Kissell's process definition, which do not coincide.

Post-fill price movement as a retail adverse-selection measure is a realised-spread idea (\hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}), not part of Harris's best-execution discussion. Rules 11Ac1-5 and 11Ac1-6 are now Rules 605 and 606; the expanded Rule 605 reports required from August 2026, and the 2025 withdrawal of the SEC's best-execution proposal, postdate these statements, which are as of 2002 (\nolinkurl{ex-sec-rule605-2024}, \nolinkurl{ex-sec-withdrawal-2025}).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:broker-agency-conflicts]{broker conflicts}; \hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}; \hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Broker conflicts: the principal--agent problem in order handling, from payment for order flow to front running]\label{def:broker-agency-conflicts}
\qffield{Statement} Brokers are agents whose interests can differ from their clients'; clients manage the problem with rewards and penalties, which require measuring performance, and measuring execution quality is hard \citep[chap.~``Brokers'', secs.~7.4--7.4.1, pp.~159--160]{alpha-harris}. Harris's conflicts (same chap.):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{dual trading}: a broker-dealer that internalises a client order wants the opposite price to the client, and may trade ahead of clients on the same side (sec.~7.4.3, p.~161);
\item \emph{order preferencing}: routing to a preferred dealer, an own dealer subsidiary, or in exchange for reciprocal flow or payment for order flow, about 1 cent a share in U.S. stocks in 2002, independently of current quotes (sec.~7.4.4, pp.~161--162);
\item \emph{soft dollars and directed brokerage}: commissions that buy research or other services, and sponsors directing orders to particular brokers or recapturing commissions (pp.~153--155);
\item \emph{dishonest practices}: front running, inappropriate order exposure, fraudulent trade assignment of the best fills to favoured clients, prearranged trading and kickbacks, unauthorised trading and churning (sec.~7.5, pp.~163--167).
\end{itemize}
Kissell adds venue rebates: when brokers keep maker--taker rebates or pay the fees, routing may serve the broker's net fees rather than the client \citep[chap.~3, p.~90]{s7-kissellscience}. Interpretation: if routing to $v$ costs the client $C_v$ in expectation and earns the broker $h_v$, the client's choice is $\arg\min_vC_v$ and a broker maximising its own net would choose $\arg\min_v(C_v-h_v)$; they differ exactly when some $a,b$ have $C_a<C_b$ but $C_a-h_a>C_b-h_b$. This states when a conflict can arise; it is not evidence that brokers route that way, since reputation, regulation and competition for flow also enter the broker's objective.

\qffield{Conditions} Practices and figures as of 2002 and 2014. Payments for order flow resemble kickbacks but generally are not, since competing dealers pass the value of order flow to brokers and brokers to clients through lower commissions (Harris, pp.~166 and~516--517). Dishonesty is likeliest where it is hard to detect; audit trails, supervision and reputation deter it (pp.~163--165).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: transaction cost analysis and audit trails turn suspected conflicts into testable comparisons, such as fill quality by venue against the NBBO or price improvement by routing destination, but a comparison shows a conflict's effect only when orders are comparable. \emph{Order-flow arrangements}. Harris adds that brokers who cross client orders internally to earn two commissions and print them at a market with a thin book bypass standing limit orders with time precedence \citep[chap.~``Competition Within and Among Markets'', box ``Lost Time Precedence'', p.~537]{alpha-harris}. Since the 2024 MiFIR amendments, EU investment firms may not receive payment for forwarding or executing retail clients' orders, subject to a possible national exemption until 30 June 2026 (\hyperref[rule:eu-trading-venue-transparency]{EU trading venues and transparency}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Order preferencing for service, economics or reciprocal flow is Harris's; reviewing it means comparing all-in fills rather than routing intent, an interpretation consistent with his best-execution standards. Commission recapture is not a broker's activity incentive: Harris treats it as a sponsor's tool to reduce the cost of soft-dollar commissions. Figures and rules are as of 2002.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:best-execution]{best execution}; \hyperref[def:order-anticipation]{order anticipation} (front running); \hyperref[def:order-exposure-decisions]{order exposure decisions}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Evaluating trade execution and trading governance: measuring and attributing cost, judging an execution against benchmarks and peers with statistical care, and the policies, disclosures, reviews and records of best execution]\label{meth:trade-execution-evaluation-governance}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Measuring and determining cost.} Choose the benchmark before trading, measure implementation shortfall with its components, and adjust for the market's move (\hyperref[meth:execution-cost-measures]{cost measures}). Compare realised cost with a pre-trade estimate, as a $z$-score, and with peers trading similar orders (\hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}).

\emph{Evaluating.} Best execution ``is a prospective, statistical, and qualitative concept''. It can be measured ex post over many trades, but not trade by trade: one would not judge a card player on one hand \citep[chap.~10, sec.~6.1, pp.~679--680]{managing-investment-portfolios-2007}.

\emph{Governance.} The 2002 trade-management guidelines summarised in MIP call for processes, disclosures and record keeping. Current rules require the following (pp.~679--681).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{U.S. broker-dealers} must use reasonable diligence to find the best market so that the price to the customer is as favourable as possible under prevailing conditions, considering the character of the market, the size and type of transaction, the number of markets checked, the accessibility of the quotation and the order's terms. Firms that route or internalise order flow must review execution quality ``regular and rigorous'', at least quarterly, by security and order type: price improvement and disimprovement, fill likelihood, speed, size, cost, customer needs and payment-for-order-flow arrangements. They must change routing or justify not changing it \citep[Rule~5310(a)(1) and Supplementary Material~.09]{finra-rule-5310}.
\item \emph{EU investment firms} must take ``all sufficient steps to obtain \dots\ the best possible result'', considering price, costs, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size and nature. For retail clients this is total consideration. Firms must have an order execution policy naming venues, disclose it and obtain consent, monitor it regularly and correct deficiencies, and be able to demonstrate compliance on request \citep[art.~27(1), (4)--(5), (7)--(8)]{eu-2014-65-mifid2}.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Comparing brokers.} Broker B's average shortfall is 7 basis points worse than A's: 32 against 25, with a standard deviation of 40, over 100 and 120 orders. The difference gives $t=1.29$ ($p=0.20$), not significant. Detecting a 7 basis point difference at the 5 percent level with this dispersion needs about 250 orders per broker, or a matched comparison of similar orders.
\item \emph{Governance decision.} Keep both brokers, route comparable orders to each to build the sample, and record the review and its rationale for the quarterly best-execution file.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} U.S. rule text as retrieved from FINRA on 19~October~2026 (last amended 2014). MiFID~II article~27 as originally enacted in 2014; later amendments to the reporting paragraphs, 27(3) and (6), were not reviewed.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Ranking brokers on raw averages without regard to order difficulty or sample size. Treating a policy document as compliance without the reviews and records behind it. Letting soft-dollar arrangements influence routing (\hyperref[def:broker-agency-conflicts]{broker conflicts}).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:best-execution]{best execution}; \hyperref[meth:execution-cost-measures]{cost measures}.

\end{qfmethod}
