\section{Futures: venue conventions, off-exchange trades, margin and price discovery}

\begin{qfconvention}[CME Globex trading conventions: a centralised market with permitted exceptions, matching algorithms, the pre-open and opening match, and the E-mini S\&P 500 daily settlement window]\label{conv:cme-globex-trading}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Centralisation.} Harris reported in 2002 that most futures markets require all trades in their contracts to be arranged within their trading systems, floor or screen. The rules prevent brokers from directing orders to confederates at prices worse than the exchange's, and futures markets had historically had no block-trading procedures or cumbersome ones \citep[chap.~``Block Traders'', sec.~15.6.2, p.~334]{alpha-harris}. CME's current rulebook keeps the principle and names the exceptions: block trades, and exchanges for related positions, which are ``permitted to be executed outside of the Exchange's centralized market in accordance with the requirements of this rule'' \citep[Rules 526 and 538]{cme-rulebook-ch5}; see \hyperref[conv:futures-block-efrp]{futures block trades and EFRPs}.

\emph{Matching.} On CME Globex a matching algorithm allocates quantity when an aggressor order meets one or more resting orders. The supported algorithms, identified by tag 1142, are Allocation (A), FIFO (F), FIFO with lead market maker (T), FIFO with top order and lead market maker (S), Pro-Rata (C), Configurable (K), Threshold Pro-Rata (O), Threshold Pro-Rata with lead market maker (Q) and an Institutional Prioritization algorithm (V). Under FIFO, price and time are the only criteria; an order loses its priority and is requeued if its quantity is increased, its price changed or its account changed. As one named example, SOFR futures (but not SOFR packs and bundles, which match FIFO) use a pro-rata allocation. A ``top'' order that betters the market has priority, then remaining quantity is allocated pro rata to resting orders at the price; orders entered in the pre-open or at the indicative opening price are matched by price and time \citep[page ``Supported Matching Algorithms'', updated 11 February 2025]{ex-cme-matching}.

\emph{Opening.} In the Pre-Open and Reserve states orders can be entered or modified, but no matching occurs, so the book can lock or cross. The trading engine computes and publishes an indicative opening price and quantity, ``a probable price and quantity at which the market will open or re-open'', for outright futures, futures spreads, options spreads and options series \citep[updated 11 February 2025]{cme-iop-wiki}.

\emph{Daily settlement, one contract, as of 2020.} In the procedure filed for trade dates from 26 October 2020, the E-mini S\&P 500 (ES) and the Micro E-mini settle to the daily settlement price of the full-sized S\&P 500 futures (SP), rounded to the nearest tradable tick. For SP's lead month, Tier~1 is the volume-weighted average price of trades in the E-mini contract on Globex from 14:59:30 to 15:00:00 Central Time, rounded to the nearest 0.10 index point. The amendment struck the floor trades in the full-sized contract, the multiplier of five and the word ``combined''. If no lead-month trades occur in the window, Tier~2 is the midpoint of the bid and ask over it; if no two-sided market is available, Tier~3 derives the price from the cash index by a carry calculation. The second month is derived from the lead month through the calendar spread. Tier~1 settles it to the VWAP of lead--second spread trades in the window ``using the same methodology''; Tier~2, if no spread trades occur, applies the last spread trade price to the lead-month settlement to derive the second-month settlement, using the nearer of the spread's bid or ask if the last trade lies outside them; Tier~3, with no spread market information, uses the carry calculation. Interpretation: in Tier~1, too, the spread price is applied to the lead-month settlement to give the second-month outright, as Tier~2 states explicitly (Exhibit~B, PDF p.~6). Staff may determine an alternative price if the calculations cannot be made or activity is anomalous \citep[Exhibit~B, blackline, page images]{cme-es-settlement-2020}.

\qffield{Conditions} The matching and opening pages are living documentation, checked on 15 October 2026; the settlement procedure is stated as filed in 2020, because the current procedure page could not be retrieved. Which algorithm applies to a given contract is set per product and was checked here only for SOFR futures. Other exchanges, and other CME contracts' settlement procedures, were not checked.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: the pathway's checkpoint needs a contract's algorithm before estimating passive fill probability. Under FIFO a resting order's fill depends on its queue position; under pro-rata allocation it depends on its size relative to the quantity at the price, which rewards larger posted size (\hyperref[meth:passive-order-split]{passive order split} for the FIFO queue model). When proportional shares are not whole contracts, a remainder must be allocated by a further rule; CME's own allocation steps for each algorithm were not read (slice~34; \hyperref[mech:order-matching-pricing]{matching and trade pricing} works examples of remainders).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy caveat \emph{No Call Auction Norm} said many futures markets do not rely on equity-style opening and closing call auctions. For CME Globex this is wrong at the open: the pre-open with an indicative opening price, followed by the opening match, is a call-auction-like uncross. For the E-mini S\&P 500 close it is right only in the narrower sense that, under the 2020 procedure, the daily settlement is a 30-second VWAP of continuous Globex trading, with midpoint and carry fallbacks, not a closing auction. Its claim that participants instead ``agree on liquid trading hours'' is not in the sources checked. The legacy principle \emph{Centralised Futures Trading} gave margining, clearing, delivery rules and fungibility as the reasons fragmentation is limited. The sources checked give exchange rules requiring on-exchange trading, with defined exceptions; the economic reasons were not checked. The legacy notes' ``highly electronic'' describes CME Globex today; Harris's 2002 account still includes floor trading.

\begin{qfconvention}[Futures block trades and exchanges for related positions at CME Group: eligibility, the related position, reporting, and the prohibition of transitory EFRPs]\label{conv:futures-block-efrp}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Block trades} (CME Rule 526, current rulebook \citep[Rule~526]{cme-rulebook-ch5}).
\begin{itemize}
\item The exchange designates the products in which blocks are permitted and their minimum quantities, and orders may not be aggregated to reach them except by eligible advisers (526.A).
\item Each party must be an Eligible Contract Participant under the Commodity Exchange Act; registered or exempt commodity trading advisers and investment advisers with more than \$25 million under management, and similar foreign persons, may act for their customers (526.B, I, J).
\item A customer must have specified that its order be executed as a block (526.C).
\item The price must be ``fair and reasonable'' given the block's size, other trades in the contract, related markets and the circumstances (526.D).
\item Blocks do not trigger conditional orders such as stops or otherwise affect orders in the regular market (526.E).
\item The seller or broker reports the block within the period and in the manner the exchange specifies, and the exchange publishes blocks separately from regular-market trades (526.F); blocks are reported to the clearing house, and records are kept (526.G--H).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Exchanges for related positions} (EFRPs; CME Rule 538 \citep[Rule~538]{cme-rulebook-ch5}).
\begin{itemize}
\item An EFRP is a privately negotiated off-exchange execution of a futures or options contract together with the simultaneous, opposite execution of an equivalent quantity of the related cash product, by-product, related product or OTC derivative. The types are the exchange of futures for physical (EFP), for risk (EFR, against an OTC swap or other derivative) and of option for option (EOO).
\item Each party is buyer on one side and seller on the other, and each side's two legs must be for accounts with the same beneficial ownership (538.A). A third party may facilitate the related position as principal for a customer but must show that it was passed through to the customer, except for immediately offsetting FX EFPs under 538.K: when CTAs, account controllers or others act for a customer in those, the initiating and offsetting cash legs need not be passed through, though the customer bears the profit or loss on the offsetting leg if the futures leg fails to clear (538.A). Opposing accounts must be independently controlled (538.B).
\item The related position must be the underlying cash commodity, a by-product, a related product or an OTC derivative with a reasonable degree of price correlation, never a futures or option on futures. It requires a bona fide transfer of ownership or a binding contract (538.C).
\item Quantities must be approximately equivalent, with hedge ratios allowed (538.E). Prices must be on the contract's increments and commercially reasonable (538.F).
\item Each EFRP is submitted to the clearing house within the specified time, with the time its terms were agreed, and is accepted only once it has cleared and the first settlement variation and performance bond are confirmed (538.I). Records are kept (538.H).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Transitory EFRPs.} Rule 538.C forbids an EFRP whose execution is contingent on another EFRP or related-position transaction between the parties, where the transactions offset the related positions without market risk that is material in context. CME Group's advisory notice in force from 7 August 2026 defines these as \emph{transitory EFRPs}. It answers ``No'' to whether they are permitted in any CME Group product: the time between the transactions is a factor, but the test is whether they ``have integrity as independent transactions exposed to market risk'' \citep[pp.~4 and~10, Q14]{cme-ra2607-5}. Rule 538.K, as amended from 7 August 2026, expressly permits EFRPs in foreign currency futures whose parties immediately offset the related position, but only as EFPs whose related position is a cash transaction or EFRs whose related position is a non-deliverable forward (NDF), in foreign currencies only and never as EOOs; the cash-side confirmations must identify the counterparty, or for a CTA's client its carrying clearing member and account \citep[Rule~538.K, page image]{cme-rulebook-ch5}. The exchanges' filing describes these as physical FX cash and FX NDF related positions; the amendment changed only 538.K, leaving 538.A--J unchanged \citep[pp.~1--3, Exhibit~A blackline]{cme-ra2607-5}. They are not treated as transitory, because the offsetting cash or NDF transaction is not contingent on the EFRP (Q28).

\qffield{Conditions} Product thresholds and reporting deadlines are set in separate exchange notices, not checked here. The rules are those of CME Group's U.S. exchanges (CME, CBOT, NYMEX and COMEX); other venues were not checked. History, kept apart from current rules: Harris described futures block rules in 2002 as limited to eligible sophisticated traders at fair and reasonable prices. He described EFPs as originally developed for agricultural hedgers, and as used at CME in currency futures immediately after the close, where exchanges called them ``transitory EFPs'' because the floor traders bought or sold the cash currency at the same time \citep[chap.~``Block Traders'', sec.~15.6.2, p.~334]{alpha-harris}. CME Group's 2019 notice records that transitory EFRPs had been permitted in CME foreign currency, NYMEX energy and COMEX and NYMEX metals products until 4 August 2014 and have been prohibited since \citep[p.~1]{cme-ra1911-5}.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: an equity block cross interacts with the exchange book under precedence rules (\hyperref[def:block-trade]{block trades}, Harris's 2002 equity rules). A futures block is negotiated away from the book, does not trigger the book's conditional orders and is published separately. The two regimes should not be mixed.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy taxonomy listed a ``transitory EFP: short-lived EFP used when the book is closed or thin''. The term is verified, but only as history: it matches Harris's 2002 account, and at CME Group such EFRPs have been prohibited since August 2014. The immediately offsetting FX EFRP of Rule 538.K is a different, permitted structure. Its EFP definition (``exchange cash or physical exposure for offsetting futures'') matches Rule 538 in substance; its closing sentence (``move large risk off-book, subject to eligibility and reporting rules'') fits Rules 526 and 538.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:block-trade]{block trades} (owned by \texttt{execution-trading-costs}); \hyperref[conv:cme-globex-trading]{CME Globex trading conventions}; \hyperref[mech:futures-margin-settlement]{futures margin and settlement}.

\begin{qfmechanism}[Futures margin and settlement at CME Clearing: performance bond, settlement variation each cycle, and the default waterfall]\label{mech:futures-margin-settlement}
\qffield{Statement} At CME Clearing, settlement for outstanding exposure is made with the clearing house in settlement cycles held ``at least once each Business Day'', at times the clearing house sets and possibly different by contract. Each clearing member, for its own and its customers' positions, pays or collects \emph{settlement variation}: the difference between this cycle's settlement price and the previous cycle's, or the trade price for a new position. Payments are final once the clearing house's bank account is debited or credited \citep[Rule~814]{cme-rulebook-ch8}. Separately, clearing members post \emph{performance bond}, initial and additional, as clearing-house staff set it, gross for each omnibus customer account and in approved collateral. The clearing house retains it ``in whole or in part, as Clearing House staff may deem necessary'', until the trades it covers have been offset, cash settled, delivered or otherwise closed out; if a clearing member defaults on performance bond, the collateral is taken over and sold, customer collateral being applied to customer requirements and house collateral to house requirements \citep[Rule~820]{cme-rulebook-ch8}.

On a clearing member's default the Base waterfall runs as follows \citep[Rules~802.A--802.B]{cme-rulebook-ch8}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the defaulting member's own resources, with default management run separately for each account class (futures, foreign futures, cleared swaps). A loss in a customer class is met first from that class's customer collateral (performance bond, excess bond and settlement gains). In a defaulted customer futures class this includes the collateral of customers who did not cause the default (802.A.2, 802.G.1). The member's own assets (its Base Guaranty Fund requirement, performance bond, CME shares or cash in lieu and other assets) are applied across account classes with priority to customer classes (802.A.5.i, iii). Collateral left after liquidating the proprietary account may be applied to customer-class losses, pro rata among defaulted customer classes (802.G.6). The restriction runs one way: segregated customer collateral may not be used for the member's proprietary obligations or for another customer account class (802.A.5.iii, 802.G);
\item CME's corporate contribution of \$100 million (802.B.1.i);
\item the Base Guaranty Fund, that is, the prefunded contributions of the non-defaulting members, applied in the rule's order of priority: the subordinated amounts of members subordinated after a default-management auction, then the remaining contributions of the other members pro rata, then the seniorized amounts of winning bidders (802.B.1.ii--iii; the fund consists of members' contributions, 802.A.4, so it is counted once here);
\item assessments on the non-defaulting members, capped at 275 percent of each member's Base Guaranty Fund requirement for losses from a single defaulter and 550 percent for all defaults in a cooling-off period, divided pro rata (802.B.1.iv).
\end{enumerate}

\qffield{Conditions} Interpretation, keeping apart the items the legacy notes merged:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{contract standardisation}, set in each contract's chapter (not read here);
\item \emph{initial margin}, the performance bond that covers moves between settlements and the time to close out;
\item \emph{variation settlement}, the realised gain or loss each cycle;
\item \emph{intraday calls}, which Rule 814 permits through more than one cycle a day, though the actual cycle times were not checked;
\item the \emph{FCM's} arrangements with its customers, governed by its customer agreements and by rules not read here, while Rules 814 and 820 bind clearing members;
\item \emph{residual default risk}: prices move between cycles, performance bond may fall short, and losses then pass along the waterfall of Rule 802 to CME's contribution, the other members' prefunded contributions and, within the caps, assessments on them.
\end{itemize}
Daily settlement bounds the exposure that can build between cycles; it does not eliminate credit risk continuously. Harris notes that futures are popular with hedgers and speculators because positions can be taken without posting large margins \citep[chap.~``Index and Portfolio Markets'', sec.~23.5, p.~492]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Practice} The legacy statement that the broker or FCM ``manages client margin calls, liquidation rights, and cash balances'' describes the customer side, which was not checked; the clearing-house rules above are the member side. \emph{A textbook account, generic rather than CME's rules (slice~31).} Hull illustrates the customer side with a broker's margin account \citep[chap.~2, ``Futures Markets and Central Counterparties'', sec.~2.4, pp.~51--54]{s7-hulloptions}: an initial margin is deposited, the account is settled to the day's gain or loss each day (daily settlement or marking to market), and the daily flow between longs and shorts is variation margin. Below a maintenance margin, ``somewhat lower than the initial margin'' and usually about 75 percent of it, the trader receives a margin call to restore the initial level or is closed out; excess over the initial margin can be withdrawn; most brokers pay interest on balances, and Treasury bills (at about 90 percent of value) or shares (about 50 percent) can usually meet initial but not later calls. Clearing members, by contrast, face a maintenance margin equal to the initial margin, margin computed on members' net positions, possible intraday calls, and a guaranty fund; margin is usually set so that the clearing house is ``about 99\% certain'' it covers losses on a member's default (pp.~53--54). Hull's gold example is checked: two contracts of 100 ounces at \$1{,}750 lose $200\times9=\$1{,}800$ on a \$9 fall, and the day-7 balance of \$7{,}980 prompts a call of \$4{,}020 back to \$12{,}000 (p.~51; Table~2.1, p.~52; p.~53). These levels, ratios and haircuts are Hull's illustrations, not rules of any clearing house; minimum margins are set by each clearing house and brokers may demand more (p.~53).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy mechanism concluded that credit risk ``is therefore controlled continuously rather than only at final settlement''. It is controlled at each settlement cycle, at least daily, with performance bond for the moves in between and a mutualised default waterfall for what remains; ``continuously'' overstates it. The legacy taxonomy's items were checked as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``fungible by expiry and specification'': contract terms were not read;
\item ``margin and default management built into the market'': Rules 802, 814 and 820;
\item ``leverage'': Harris p.~492;
\item ``hedging link to cash baskets, ETFs, options and swaps'': only partly, since Rule 538 allows cash and OTC-derivative related positions; ETFs were not checked.
\end{itemize}

\begin{qfevidence}[Price discovery between index futures and their cash and ETF markets: the E-mini's leadership in 2000, and what explains leadership]\label{ev:index-futures-price-discovery}
\qffield{Statement} Hasbrouck studies price discovery across U.S. equity index markets at up to one-second resolution, using vector error-correction models and information shares, for 1 March to 31 May 2000. He finds:
\begin{itemize}
\item for the S\&P 500 and Nasdaq-100, whose index markets comprised an ETF, a floor-traded futures contract and an electronic E-mini, most price discovery occurred in the E-mini, whose prices generally led the regular futures and the ETFs; the ETFs' contributions were statistically significant but small;
\item for the S\&P 400 MidCap, with only an ETF and regular futures, both contributed substantially and the ETF appeared to dominate;
\item the S\&P 500 sector ETFs contributed little to price discovery in the index.
\end{itemize}
\citep[NYU working paper FIN-00-046, draft of 16 November 2000, abstract and pp.~4--5, 13 and~34]{ex-hasbrouck-2003}. The earlier literature he reviews found that the dominant influence ran from futures to cash, with weaker but measurable effects in the reverse direction (p.~5).

\qffield{Conditions} The evidence is for three U.S. indexes in three months of 2000, when the regular contracts traded on the CME floor during regular hours and on Globex otherwise, and the E-minis on Globex (p.~6). It is from a preliminary working-paper draft. The catalogue describes the published 2003 version's S\&P 400 result as shared between the regular futures and the ETF; the published version was not read, so the difference is recorded, not resolved. The theoretical treatment of futures--cash price discovery the pathway assigns, Garbade and Silber (1983, \texttt{w5-garbade-silber}), was not available and is unread.

\qffield{Practice} Harris's reason for index-market leadership is that index traders price only index risk, while traders of the components must price every risk of their securities, so index markets discover the price of index risk faster (\hyperref[def:portfolio-trading]{portfolio trading}, p.~489).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy mechanism said futures lead ``when they are cheaper to trade, more leveraged, or open during information arrival'', and cash markets lead ``when constituent-level information dominates''. Harris's argument supports only the general direction for index risk. The specific conditions are plausible hypotheses not tested in the passages read, and the working paper does not attribute the E-mini's leadership to cost or leverage. Leadership in information shares is a statistical measure of where innovations first appear, not a causal claim about who is informed.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:portfolio-trading]{portfolio trading}, on why index markets discover the price of index risk first, owned by the section overview \texttt{execution-trading-costs}; \hyperref[mech:etf-creation-redemption]{ETF creation and redemption}; \hyperref[def:arbitrage]{arbitrage} (the basis and delivery arbitrage).

\qffield{Further reading} The legacy block it replaces, verbatim, is in \nolinkurl{derivations.tex} in the folder \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/futures-execution/}. Garbade and Silber (1983) for the theory.

Stability: draft. Currency: evidence for March--May 2000.

\begin{qfconvention}[Government bond futures as Gliner describes them in 2013--2014: U.S.\ Treasury contract sizes and ticks, physical delivery and cheapest-to-deliver, the German Schatz, Bobl and Bund, and why futures trade below the bond]\label{conv:government-bond-futures}
\qffield{Statement} U.S.\ Treasury note and bond futures are physically settled, ``which lends to the concept of cheapest-to-deliver'', and most traders roll before delivery \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``U.S.\ Fixed Income Futures'', PDF p.~264]{s7-gliner}. Gliner's specifications (pp.~265--266): \$200,000 face for the 2-year (TU) and \$100,000 for the 5-year (FV), 10-year (TY), bond (US, his ``15+Year'') and ultra bond (WN, ``25+Year''); ticks of a quarter of a 32nd (\$15.625) for TU, a quarter (\$7.8125) for FV, a half (\$15.625) for TY and a full 32nd (\$31.25) for US and WN; March, June, September and December months. German futures: Schatz about 2 years (DU), Bobl 4.5 to 5.5 years (OE) and Bund 8.5 to 10.5 years (RX) to maturity (p.~266).

\qffield{Notation} Tick value $=$ face $\times$ tick in points $/100$: $200{,}000\times(1/128)/100=\$15.625$; $100{,}000\times(1/128)/100=\$7.8125$; $100{,}000\times(1/64)/100=\$15.625$; $100{,}000\times(1/32)/100=\$31.25$ (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/slice37-checks.py}, section~7).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Why the curve slopes down.} Gliner says Treasuries ``have term premium, which implies a positive sloping yield curve, which means that the futures curve on Treasuries is usually in backwardation'', unusually among futures (p.~264), and adds that underlying instruments ``generally have a higher interest rate than short-term financing costs''. The second clause is the reason: with coupon income above the repo rate the forward price is below spot (positive carry). A 4 percent bond at 100 financed at 1 percent for three months has a forward of 99.25 (checks, section~8). Term premium affects the slope of yields, not the futures discount directly; a futures curve in ``backwardation'' here means lower prices for later contracts.

\qffield{Practice} Contract specifications change, and contracts and tick sizes may have been added or altered since 2014; the specifications above are Gliner's and should be checked against CME's and Eurex's current contract terms before use (backlog). \emph{Eurex terms} (slice-37 follow-up). Eurex's fixed-income futures factsheet gives remaining terms of 1.75 to 2.25 years (Euro-Schatz, FGBS), 4.5 to 5.5 (Euro-Bobl, FGBM) and 8.5 to 10.5 (Euro-Bund, FGBL) on a notional 6 percent coupon, and 24.0 to 35.0 years (Euro-Buxl, FGBX) on a notional 4 percent coupon, with a contract value of EUR 100,000, delivery on the tenth calendar day of the delivery month (or the next exchange day) and last trading two exchange days before \citep[pp.~1--2]{eurex-fi-factsheet}; Eurex's contract specifications as of 10 September 2025 define the subject matter of these contracts in the same way \citep[sec.~1.2.1, p.~1]{eurex-cs-2025}. The legacy's Schatz and Buxl bands are therefore current Eurex terms; the factsheet's date was not shown in the text read. CME's Treasury contract specifications were not read: CME's site refused automated access with a terms-of-use message (backlog narrowed to CME's rulebook chapters for TU, FV, TY, US and UB).
