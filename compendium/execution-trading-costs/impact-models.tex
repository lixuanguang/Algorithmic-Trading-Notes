\subsection{Price impact}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Price impact: unperturbed, observed and trade prices; reaction, prediction, transient and permanent impact]\label{def:price-impact}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Price impact} is the effect of trading on prices: trades cause price moves that would not otherwise have happened \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.1.2, p.~5]{wide-webster-price-impact}. Webster separates three prices for a trader whose trades $Q$ are part of all trades $L$ \citep[chap.~2, Definition~2.2.6, pp.~53--54]{wide-webster-price-impact}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{unperturbed price} $S_t$, the price without the trades $L$ and their impact;
\item the \emph{perturbed} or observed price $P_t=S_t+I_t$, where $I_t$ is the impact of $L$;
\item the \emph{trade price} $\widetilde P_{t-}=S_{t-}+I_{t-}+s_{t-}$, where $s$ is the instantaneous transaction (spread) cost of the trader's own fill, and the subscript $t-$ marks that a fill at $t$ executes against the state just before $t$.
\end{itemize}
Bouchaud, Bonart, Donier and Gould separate three impacts of a market order executed at $t$, at horizon $\ell$, with $m$ the mid-price and $\mathcal F_t$ everything that may have prompted the order \citep[chap.~11, sec.~11.2, eqs.~(11.1)--(11.4), pp.~210--211]{catalogue-0528}:
\[ I^{\mathrm{react}}=\mathbb E[m_{t+\ell}\mid\mathrm{exec}_t,\mathcal F_t]-\mathbb E[m_{t+\ell}\mid\text{no exec}_t,\mathcal F_t],\qquad I^{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathbb E[m_{t+\ell}\mid\mathrm{exec}_t]-m_t, \]
\[ I^{\mathrm{pred}}=\mathbb E[m_{t+\ell}\mid\text{no exec}_t,\mathcal F_t]-m_t,\qquad I^{\mathrm{obs}}=I^{\mathrm{react}}+I^{\mathrm{pred}}. \]
Over time, impact has a \emph{transient} part that decays and a \emph{permanent} part. The permanent part combines reaction and prediction: it is higher for traders with predictive power than for noise trades \citep[chap.~12, sec.~12.4, p.~240]{catalogue-0528}.

\qffield{Notation} $I^{\mathrm{react}}$, $I^{\mathrm{obs}}$, $I^{\mathrm{pred}}$ are the reaction, observed and prediction impacts. The source writes $I^{\mathrm{react.}}_{t+\ell}(\mathrm{exec}_t\mid\mathcal F_t)$ and so on; the time and conditioning arguments are dropped here. The identity $I^{\mathrm{obs}}=I^{\mathrm{react}}+I^{\mathrm{pred}}$ needs every expectation conditioned on $\mathcal F_t$; as printed, (11.2) conditions on execution alone, and the authors state that the conditioning on $\mathcal F_t$ is always implicit \citep[same sec., p.~210]{catalogue-0528} (Interpretation: without it the two sides differ by $\mathbb E[m_{t+\ell}\mid\mathrm{exec}_t]-\mathbb E[m_{t+\ell}\mid\mathrm{exec}_t,\mathcal F_t]$). Webster's $P$ is a price, not the $P$ of other entries.

\qffield{Conditions} Timeless (concepts); the empirical statements as of 2018. Two readings of impact frame the literature \citep[chap.~11, sec.~11.1, pp.~208--210]{catalogue-0528}. In the efficient-market reading, traders forecast prices and trade accordingly, so trades and later price changes correlate even if trades move nothing; in Hasbrouck's words, quoted there, orders forecast prices. In the order-flow reading, trading itself moves prices, and impact exists even in a model with no information at all, such as the Santa Fe model. Reality probably lies between. If the mid-price were a martingale, observed and reaction impact would coincide; they do not, because the information behind the order raises the expected price even without the trade (p.~211). Studies with random or cash-driven trades find that, over short horizons, mechanical impact is about the same for random and allegedly informed trades; prediction shows only at longer horizons (p.~212).

After a metaorder, the impact path splits into a transient part that vanishes and a permanent part $I_\infty(Q)$, which collects both the prediction behind the order and any permanent reaction to all trades \citep[chap.~12, sec.~12.2, eq.~(12.5), p.~233]{catalogue-0528}. Bacry, Iuga, Lasnier and Lehalle describe the same divide for permanent impact: a mechanical view, common among econophysicists, in which buying and selling pressure moves prices, and an informational view, favoured by economists after Kyle, in which trades reveal information.

They hold the two as a dual picture rather than choosing one \citep[arXiv preprint of 9 Dec.\ 2014, sec.~7.1, pp.~19--20]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}. Within the mechanical view they report two positions, a ``fair pricing'' view in which permanent impact is about two-thirds of the temporary impact, and Bouchaud's view that there is no permanent impact, only the long memory of the order flow; both are cited there.

\qffield{Practice} Reaction impact can be estimated by trading at random, by randomising execution of signals, or by conditioning on the rest of the market's order-sign imbalance; prediction impact is very hard to estimate \citep[chap.~11, sec.~11.2, pp.~211--212]{catalogue-0528}. The counterfactual cannot be observed: a trader never sees both outcomes of one trade, which Webster calls the causality challenge \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.1.3, pp.~6--7]{wide-webster-price-impact}. Webster traces impact modelling through four communities: economists' stylised models (Kyle), mathematical finance's control problems (Almgren--Chriss, Obizhaeva--Wang, Gatheral), econophysics' empirical response functions and propagators, and books from quantitative trading desks \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.1.5, pp.~9--15]{wide-webster-price-impact}. The model families in use differ in what they describe:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Almgren--Chriss}: the price follows an arithmetic random walk shifted by a permanent impact $g(v)$ of the trading rate $v$; each sale executes at the previous price less a temporary impact $h(v)$ that does not enter the next market price \citep[author preprint of Dec.\ 2000, secs.~1.2--1.3, eqs.~(1)--(2), pp.~8--9]{alpha-almgren}. In the linear case $g(v)=\gamma v$ and $h(n_k/\tau)=\epsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}(n_k)+(\eta/\tau)n_k$, with $\gamma$ in (\$/share)/share, $\epsilon$ in \$/share (half the spread plus fees) and $\eta$ in (\$/share)/(share/time) \citep[author preprint of Dec.\ 2000, sec.~1.5, eqs.~(6)--(7), pp.~10--11]{alpha-almgren}. The schedule minimises expected cost plus $\lambda$ times its variance \citep[author preprint of Dec.\ 2000, sec.~2, pp.~12--13]{alpha-almgren}.
\item \emph{Linear impact}: cost or impact proportional to the trading rate (Almgren--Chriss) or to signed size (\hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle}).
\item \emph{Propagator}: each signed trade moves the price by a decaying kernel (\hyperref[mod:propagator]{propagator model}).
\item \emph{Square-root}: metaorder impact concave in size relative to volume (\hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{square-root impact law}).
\end{itemize}
Impact during execution is measured from the arrival price, signed by the order's side: in bid--ask spreads by Lehalle and Laruelle \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.1, p.~204]{alpha-laruelle}, relative to volatility by Almgren et al.\ (\hyperref[meth:almgren-impact-estimation]{direct estimation}); its path is in \hyperref[ev:intraday-impact-profile]{intraday impact profile}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} ``Temporary impact'' has three meanings. In Almgren and Chriss it is $h(v)$, which lowers the execution price of the trader's own sale in that period but does not enter the next market price \citep[author preprint of Dec.\ 2000, sec.~1.3, eq.~(2), p.~9]{alpha-almgren}; their prose describes a short-lived imbalance after which liquidity returns each period (p.~8). Webster accordingly reads it as the instantaneous transaction cost \citep[chap.~2, n.~3, p.~53]{wide-webster-price-impact}.

Bacry et al.\ use ``temporary market impact'' for the impact at the end of a metaorder's execution, distinct from the transient curve during execution and the decay after it \citep[arXiv preprint of 9 Dec.\ 2014, sec.~2.2, p.~3]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}. Neither is a transient displacement that decays over many later trades. Defining temporary impact as the price concession needed to obtain immediacy, expected to decay as liquidity replenishes, mixes the two; say which is meant (Interpretation). Calling a long-run move permanent impact needs care.

Long-horizon impact is hard to measure because price variance grows with the horizon. Correlated metaorders, the same decision continued over days, can look like permanent impact. Separating mechanical from informational impact is difficult \citep[chap.~12, sec.~12.4, p.~240]{catalogue-0528}. Bacry et al.\ remove moves of each stock's reference index (see \hyperref[meth:post-execution-impact-profile]{post-execution impact profile}); sector or factor moves are not removed, and Bouchaud et al.\ suggest conditioning on the rest of the market's order-sign imbalance (p.~212). Removing the information effect before calling the residual permanent impact presumes the effect can be measured; Bacry et al., without the traders' signals, remove the market component and read it as aggregated information (p.~20). Theoretical, reduced-form and practitioner views of impact simplify Webster's four communities.

Orders of the same size and participation can have different impact because of their timing: following a trend, reverting to a mean, or following an index rebalancing \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2, p.~204]{alpha-laruelle}. Separating execution mechanics from the signal behind the trade is the split between reaction and prediction impact above. The mechanism (temporary impact decays when other traders disagree with the move; permanent impact remains when the order flow reveals information) is the information-based reading of the distinction above, with the caveats on the terms that this entry gives; Harris's resiliency, how quickly prices revert after imbalances initiated by uninformed traders, ties the decay to value traders \citep[chap.~``Liquidity'', sec.~19.2, p.~400; sec.~19.3.4, p.~403]{alpha-harris} (\hyperref[def:liquidity-dimensions]{liquidity and market quality}).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:propagator]{propagator model}; \hyperref[res:no-price-manipulation]{absence of price manipulation}; \hyperref[def:price-formation-process]{price formation process} (temporary and permanent impact by consensus, Lehalle and Laruelle); \hyperref[res:spread-component-identification]{spread component identification} (transitory and permanent components of the spread); \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle single-auction model} (permanent impact from information); \hyperref[mod:grossman-miller]{Grossman--Miller model} (a temporary concession for immediacy).

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmodel}[Propagator model: transient impact of past trades]\label{mod:propagator}
\qffield{Statement} In market-order time, with $\varepsilon_n=\pm1$ the sign of the $n$th market order and $\xi_n$ price changes not induced by trades, the mid-price is \citep[chap.~13, sec.~13.2, eq.~(13.7), p.~252]{catalogue-0528}
\[ m_t=m_{t_0}+\sum_{t_0\le n<t}G(t-n)\,\varepsilon_n+\sum_{t_0\le n<t}\xi_n. \]
The \emph{propagator} $G(\ell)$ is the impact at lag $\ell$ of one trade, expected to decrease with $\ell$; $G_\infty=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}G(\ell)$ is the permanent reaction impact. Returns then split into immediate impact, the decay of past impacts and noise, $r_t=G_1\varepsilon_t+\sum_{n<t}K(t-n)\varepsilon_n+\xi_t$ with $K(\ell)=G(\ell+1)-G(\ell)$ (same sec., eq.~(13.8)). Equation~(13.7) defines the family of linear transient impact models (TIM).

\qffield{Notation} $m_t$ mid-price just before event $t$; $r_t=m_{t+1}-m_t$; $G_1=G(1)$; $C(\ell)=\mathbb E[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t+\ell}]$ the sign autocorrelation; $R(\ell)=\mathbb E[\varepsilon_t(m_{t+\ell}-m_t)]$ the response function (observed impact).

\qffield{Conditions} Timeless (model); the empirical motivation as of 2018. Why impact must be transient: if each trade had constant permanent impact $G_1$, then $\mathbb E[r_tr_{t+\ell}]=G_1^2C(\ell)$, and the long memory of order signs would make returns strongly autocorrelated. That contradicts the nearly flat signature plots of real markets, so much of each trade's impact must relax \citep[chap.~13, secs.~13.1--13.2, pp.~249--251]{catalogue-0528}. The sign memory comes mainly from order splitting, investors working large orders in pieces because available liquidity is small; on the time scales of the power-law decay of $C(\ell)$, herding is not a relevant factor \citep[chap.~10, secs.~10.4--10.5, p.~198]{catalogue-0528}.

For a power-law propagator $G(\ell)\approx\Gamma_\infty\ell^{-\beta}$ and signs with $C(\ell)\approx c_\infty\ell^{-\gamma}$, $0<\gamma<1$, prices are diffusive at long lags only at the fine balance $\beta=\beta_c=(1-\gamma)/2$; permanent impact ($\beta=0$) gives trending prices, faster decay ($\beta>\beta_c$) sub-diffusive ones \citep[chap.~13, secs.~13.2.2--13.2.3, eq.~(13.17), pp.~256--257]{catalogue-0528}. The simple model assumes impact independent of trade volume, time and market conditions, which the authors call gross oversimplifications, relaxed in their chap.~14 \citep[same chap., p.~252]{catalogue-0528}.

\qffield{Practice} The observed response and the bare propagator differ when signs are correlated: $R(\ell)=G(\ell)+\sum_{0<n<\ell}G(\ell-n)C(n)+\sum_{n>0}[G(\ell+n)-G(n)]C(n)$, which reduces to $G(\ell)$ only for uncorrelated signs. Estimating $G$ from $R$ and $C$ through this relation is sensitive to finite-size effects \citep[same chap., sec.~13.2.1, eq.~(13.10), p.~253]{catalogue-0528}. Webster calls the Obizhaeva--Wang model a simple form of the propagator model \citep[chap.~1, p.~10]{wide-webster-price-impact}; in its generalized form impact follows $dI_t=-\beta_tI_t\,dt+\lambda_t\,dQ_t$ \citep[chap.~2, Definition~2.8.1, p.~97]{wide-webster-price-impact}. Interpretation: that is an exponentially decaying kernel in calendar time, $G\propto e^{-\beta\ell}$ for constant $\beta$.

Kernel shapes in use: \emph{non-parametric}, from the lagged sign--return correlation $S(\ell)=R(\ell+1)-R(\ell)=\sum_{n\ge0}C(|n-\ell|)K(n)$ inverted as $K=\mathbf C^{-1}S$ up to a maximum lag \citep[chap.~13, eqs.~(13.13)--(13.15), p.~254]{catalogue-0528}; \emph{power law}, which the estimates show at long lags, $G(\ell)\approx\Gamma_\infty\ell^{-\beta}$ with $\beta<1$, decaying to zero but with divergent sum (``long-range resilience'', same p.~254); and \emph{exponential}, as in the Obizhaeva--Wang model above.

The original paper by Bouchaud, Gefen, Potters and Wyart writes the price as a sum over past trades with a ``bare'' propagator $G_0$ weighting each sign by the logarithm of the trade volume, $p_n=\sum_{n'<n}G_0(n-n')\,\varepsilon_{n'}\ln V_{n'}+\sum_{n'<n}\eta_{n'}$, with $\eta$ independent of the signs and uncorrelated in time \citep[arXiv cond-mat/0307332v2, sec.~3.1, eq.~(12), p.~14]{wide-bouchaud-propagator}. It argues that with power-law sign correlations a constant $G_0$ would make the response grow like $\ell^{1-\gamma}$, so $G_0$ must decay \citep[same, sec.~3.1, p.~16]{wide-bouchaud-propagator}; with a power-law ansatz the Hurst exponent satisfies $2H=2-2\beta-\gamma$ \citep[same, sec.~3.2, eq.~(22), pp.~16--17]{wide-bouchaud-propagator}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} In the simple model every trade shares one propagator $G$; trade-dependent decay profiles need the generalised models of chap.~14, which generalise by event type, not by agent. Reading the measured response $R(\ell)$ as the impact of a single trade ignores the sign autocorrelation. Timing and accumulation conventions matter: (a) $m_k$ is the mid-price just \emph{before} trade $k$ when summing over $k'<k$; (b) the noise is the cumulative $\sum\eta$, which makes the price diffusive apart from trading, not a single $\eta_k$, which would make it stationary (Interpretation); (c) the 2004 paper weights trades by $\ln V$, which the book's simple model omits; (d) $R$ and $C$ are measured, and the kernel is estimated from them.

Interpretation: Hasbrouck's generalized Roll model, in \hyperref[res:spread-component-identification]{spread component identification}, is the constant-propagator case applied to the efficient price with independent signs: the trade moves it permanently by $\lambda$.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}; \hyperref[res:no-price-manipulation]{absence of price manipulation} (restrictions on $G$ and on the Obizhaeva--Wang parameters); \hyperref[def:intraday-overnight-returns]{returns by time scale}; \hyperref[def:volatility-components]{fundamental and transitory volatility} (the variance ratio and transient components).

\qffield{Further reading} The original paper \citep{wide-bouchaud-propagator}, arXiv version.

\end{qfmodel}

\begin{qfresult}[Absence of price manipulation in impact models]\label{res:no-price-manipulation}
\qffield{Statement} In an impact model, \emph{price manipulation} is a round trip, a strategy that starts and ends with no position, whose expected cost is negative. Webster quotes this definition from Gatheral (2010)\citep[chap.~1, n.~3, p.~7]{wide-webster-price-impact}. A usable model must exclude it. Bouchaud, Bonart, Donier and Gould write the expected cost of a closed trajectory with trading rate $j$ and price $x^*$ as $\mathcal C=\int_0^Tj(u)\,x^*(u)\,du$ with $\int_0^Tj(u)\,du=0$. They show that in their latent-liquidity model it is a non-negative quadratic form, so $\mathcal C\ge0$ for every schedule, generalising a result for the linear propagator model \citep[chap.~19, sec.~19.5, pp.~361--362]{catalogue-0528}. In Webster's generalized Obizhaeva--Wang model, buying $\Delta$ in one jump at $t$ and selling it at $t'>t$, without a spread, earns
\[ (S_{t'}-S_t)\Delta-\tfrac12\lambda_t(2\beta_t+\gamma'_t)(t'-t)\Delta^2+o(t'-t), \]
so if $2\beta+\gamma'<0$, large, quick round trips earn arbitrary expected profits from the trader's own impact. The no-manipulation condition is $2\beta+\gamma'>0$, after Fruth, Schöneborn and Urusov (2013)\citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.7.1, Example~2.7.1, p.~94]{wide-webster-price-impact}.

\qffield{Notation} $S$ unperturbed price; $\Delta$ shares; impact $I$ with $dI=-\beta I\,dt+\lambda\,dQ$, decay $\beta$ per unit time, push $\lambda=e^\gamma$ in price per share, $\gamma'$ its rate of change. As Webster's profit expression implies, the trader's buy executes at $S_t+\tfrac12\lambda_t\Delta$ on average, the sell at $S_{t'}+e^{-\int_t^{t'}\beta_s\,ds}\lambda_t\Delta-\tfrac12\lambda_{t'}\Delta$.

\qffield{Conditions} Webster's example assumes no spread, no prior impact, jump trades and exact unwinding; the expected profit from $S$ is zero only if $S$ is a martingale. The condition $2\beta+\gamma'>0$ rules out only the paradoxes that yield arbitrary profits. It does not rule out transaction-triggered manipulation, such as selling first to depress the price for later buying \citep[same sec., pp.~94--95]{wide-webster-price-impact}. In a square-root model it bounds forecast volume growth, $v'_t/v_t<4\beta_t$: trading activity cannot double in less than a quarter of the impact half-life, so rising volume curves can create manipulation opportunities while falling ones cannot \citep[same chap., sec.~2.7.3, Example~2.7.2, p.~96]{wide-webster-price-impact}.

\qffield{Practice} Using an impact model that admits profitable closed trajectories in execution algorithms leads to instabilities or counter-intuitive trades \citep[chap.~19, p.~362]{catalogue-0528}, and Webster asks that a model rule out manipulation paradoxes \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.1.4, p.~8]{wide-webster-price-impact}. Interpretation, the accounting behind any check: a trader who buys and sells $Q$ shares at average execution prices $\bar P_B$ and $\bar P_S$, impact included, and pays explicit costs $F$ in total, ends with $Q(\bar P_S-\bar P_B)-F$ and no position, hence no mark-to-market term. With a per-share cost $c$ on each side, $F=2Qc$; with $c$ quoted per round-trip share, $F=Qc$. Spread counted in $\bar P$ must not be counted again in $c$.

\qffield{Pitfalls} A realised profitable round trip is not price manipulation: the profit may come from $S_{t'}-S_t$, from information, or from luck. Manipulation concerns expected profit under a model, with no information (Interpretation). Impact that treats buys and sells alike per unit is not by itself a no-manipulation guarantee. Webster's example is linear and symmetric trade by trade, yet admits manipulation when liquidity changes faster than impact decays (Interpretation). That a zero-net sequence cannot mechanically create a higher average sale price holds in Harris's instantaneous, permanent-impact example but is not a general result. Stating the identity as a test needs $c$ defined per side or per round trip. Strategic bluffing, which fools other traders through their inferences, is a different mechanism from manipulation through one's own impact in a model, where nobody is fooled (\hyperref[def:bluffing-manipulation]{bluffing and manipulation}).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:propagator]{propagator model}; \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}; \hyperref[def:bluffing-manipulation]{bluffing and manipulation} (Harris's asymmetric-impact bluff).

\qffield{Further reading} Gatheral, ``No-Dynamic-Arbitrage and Market Impact'' \citep{catalogue-0501}.

\end{qfresult}

\subsection{Metaorder impact}

\begin{qfevidence}[Square-root impact law: metaorder impact concave in size relative to volume]\label{ev:square-root-impact}
\qffield{Statement} The peak impact of a metaorder of volume $Q$ executed over time $T$ is well described by \citep[chap.~12, sec.~12.3.1, eq.~(12.6), p.~234]{catalogue-0528}
\[ \mathcal I^{\mathrm{peak}}(Q,T)\cong Y\sigma_T\left(\frac{Q}{V_T}\right)^{\delta},\qquad Q\ll V_T, \]
with $Y$ of order one ($Y\cong0.5$ for US stocks), $\delta$ between 0.4 and 0.7, $\sigma_T$ the volatility and $V_T$ the volume traded over the execution horizon. Both sides are in percent of price. In the Capital Fund Management sample of about 500{,}000 futures metaorders, June 2007 to December 2010, $\delta\simeq0.5$ for small-tick and $\simeq0.6$ for large-tick contracts, for $Q/V_T$ from about $10^{-5}$ to a few percent \citep[same sec., Figure~12.2, p.~235]{catalogue-0528}. Bacry, Iuga, Lasnier and Lehalle confirm a square-root temporary impact in daily participation on about 400{,}000 metaorders traded on European markets by one broker in 2010 \citep[arXiv preprint of 9 Dec.\ 2014, abstract, p.~1]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}.

\qffield{Conditions} The law is reported across equities, futures, FX, options and Bitcoin, before and after 2005, for small- and large-tick instruments and for different strategies and execution styles \citep[same sec., p.~234]{catalogue-0528}. It concerns \emph{peak} impact at the end of execution, not permanent impact; the price reverts after execution (Figure~12.1, p.~234). Impact is not additive: the second half of a metaorder moves the price less than the first, and $Q$ enters relative to the volume traded during execution, not to market capitalisation \citep[same chap., sec.~12.3.2, pp.~235--236]{catalogue-0528}. Linear models such as Kyle's predict the opposite \citep[same chap., pp.~233 and~241]{catalogue-0528}.

Almgren et al., on US orders of 2001--2003, reject an exponent of one-half for temporary impact against size relative to volume during execution at the 95 percent level, estimating $0.600\pm0.038$ (\hyperref[meth:almgren-impact-estimation]{direct estimation}); Bacry et al.\ find that adding a duration factor $1/T^{\gamma}$ with $\gamma\simeq0.25$ reinforces the square-root shape in daily participation \citep[arXiv preprint of 9 Dec.\ 2014, abstract, p.~1]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}. The exponents differ with the regressor (daily participation, trading rate, or size relative to volume during execution) and with the fitting method.

\qffield{Practice} If the impact of the first fraction $\phi$ of a metaorder follows the same law, the execution shortfall per unit volume is two-thirds of the peak impact under square-root impact, against one-half under linear impact \citep[same chap., sec.~12.5, eqs.~(12.10)--(12.11), p.~241]{catalogue-0528}. Bacry et al.\ debias post-execution prices with a square-root model of daily participation and report that powers from 0.4 to 0.7 give similar results \citep[arXiv preprint, sec.~7.2, n.~3, p.~21]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Describing impact only as concave in order size relative to market volume omits the volatility scale $\sigma_T$, the small-participation condition $Q\ll V_T$ and the exponent range; ``square-root'' names exponents from 0.4 to 0.7. The fitted exponent depends on the estimation method: Bacry et al.\ find smaller exponents with robust direct regressions than with log-log regressions (sec.~8, p.~22). The primary study of the futures sample, Tóth et al.\ (2011), is cited through Bouchaud et al.\ (\nolinkurl{wide-toth-square-root}).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}; \hyperref[mod:propagator]{propagator model}; \hyperref[meth:post-execution-impact-profile]{post-execution impact profile}; \hyperref[mod:kyle-single-auction]{Kyle single-auction model} (linear impact, the contrast).

\qffield{Further reading} Tóth et al., the futures study \citep{wide-toth-square-root}.

\end{qfevidence}

\begin{qfmethod}[Post-execution impact profile: debiasing later metaorders and removing index moves]\label{meth:post-execution-impact-profile}
\qffield{Statement} To estimate how much of a metaorder's impact remains days after execution, Bacry, Iuga, Lasnier and Lehalle proceed as follows \citep[arXiv preprint of 9 Dec.\ 2014, sec.~7.2, pp.~20--22]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For each metaorder executed on day $D$, take daily closes over the 41 days $d=D-20,\dots,D+20$.
\item Debias for later flow: from the price moves after day $D$, subtract the temporary impact of the metaorders executed on the following days, using a square-root model of their aggregated daily participation fitted to the data, following Waelbroeck and Gomes (2013), cited there.
\item Split each daily log return into a systematic part and a residual,
\[ \log P_d-\log P_{d-1}=\beta(\omega)\,(\log\mathcal I_d-\log\mathcal I_{d-1})+\Delta W_d, \]
with $P_d$ the stock's close, $\mathcal I_d$ its reference index and $\beta(\omega)$ the stock's beta over $D-20$ to $D+20$ (eq.~(21), p.~21).
\item Cumulate from the close of $D-1$: the idiosyncratic profile $W_d-W_{D-1}$ and the systematic profile $\beta(\log\mathcal I_d-\log\mathcal I_{D-1})$. Their sum, the post-execution profile, is an estimate of permanent impact (p.~22).
\end{enumerate}

\qffield{Notation} $\mathcal I_d$ is an index level, not impact; $P_d$ a close price, not Webster's perturbed price. Impact itself is defined through $\epsilon\,\Delta P_t=\eta_t+\epsilon\,\Delta W_t$ for a metaorder of sign $\epsilon$, with $\Delta W$ the move that would have occurred without it \citep[arXiv preprint, sec.~2.2, eq.~(1), p.~3]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}; estimation assumes $\mathbb E[\epsilon\,\Delta W]=0$, not $\mathbb E[\Delta W]=0$ (p.~4).

\qffield{Conditions} As of the 2010 European sample. Lehalle and Laruelle restate the procedure, citing the 2015 published paper: each stock's reference index is its home index (CAC~40, FTSE~100, DAX), and $\beta$ is estimated by least squares over $D-20$ to $D+20$ \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.2, eq.~(3.2), pp.~210 and~213--215]{alpha-laruelle}.

Interpretation: this matches the preprint's window, so the in-sample estimation of $\beta$ remains an open methodological question rather than a preprint artefact; a restatement by one of the authors is not independent confirmation. The broker's data do not say why each metaorder was placed. The authors therefore assume the clients' aggregate portfolio is close to the market portfolio, with beta one, and treat the systematic component as the information the issuers used in aggregate (p.~20). The participation of metaorders is autocorrelated beyond 20 days, at about 0.3 to 0.4 \citep[same sec., Figure~10, p.~21]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}, so later executions in the same direction would otherwise be attributed to the first.

\qffield{Practice} Without debiasing, prices keep trending in the direction of the execution-day jump for 20 days, with no reversal. After debiasing, the idiosyncratic profile returns to its initial level within 20 days, and the impact remaining after 20 days is entirely systematic; the authors conclude that no permanent effect is due to the metaorder itself (sec.~7.3, p.~22). They report this as compatible with two studies cited there. Waelbroeck and Gomes (2013) found permanent impact for informed trades but none for cash-driven ones over 60 days. Brokmann et al.\ (2014) found that, once the issuer's alpha was removed, impact vanished after about a dozen days (p.~20).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Bacry et al.\ could not classify trades as information- or liquidity-driven, lacking the triggers; that split is Waelbroeck and Gomes's design, reported through Bacry et al. Their method removes only the reference index, not market and sector moves from a factor model. Interpretation:
\begin{itemize}
\item The beta is estimated over a window that includes the execution and the days after it, so it is in-sample and can absorb part of the event.
\item Removing the systematic part removes any impact the aggregate flow had on the index itself, which the authors read as information.
\item The debiasing inherits the error of the square-root model for later flow.
\end{itemize}
Not charging the execution for a market move is one reading of the decomposition; the authors' own reading is that the systematic part carries the information behind the trades.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact} (permanent against finite-horizon impact; correlated metaorders); \hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{square-root impact law} (the debiasing model); \hyperref[mod:propagator]{propagator model} (long memory of order flow).

\qffield{Further reading} Waelbroeck and Gomes (2013) and Brokmann et al.\ (2014), cited by Bacry et al.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfevidence}[Intraday metaorder impact: its path during and after execution, and its dependence on participation and duration]\label{ev:intraday-impact-profile}
\qffield{Statement} Measured as the price move from the arrival price, signed by the order's side and expressed in bid--ask spreads, the average impact of Crédit Agricole Cheuvreux executions on CAC~40 stocks in 2010 rises concavely over the execution: a slice executed early moves the price more than one executed late. After the execution it reverts, to a level below the one reached at the end \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2.1, Figure~3.3, pp.~204--206]{alpha-laruelle}. Lehalle and Laruelle summarise how impact depends on the participation rate and the duration by
\[ \text{Market impact}\propto\text{Duration}^{\alpha}\times\text{Participation}^{\gamma}, \]
fitted to averages over buckets of duration (in 5-minute intervals) and participation rate. The fitted surface is concave in each variable separately, with $\gamma\approx0.5$ \citep[same sec., eq.~(3.1), Figure~3.6, pp.~207--209]{alpha-laruelle}. Impact rises with both participation and duration, even separately: executions of 25 to 50 minutes lie above those shorter than 25 minutes at every participation rate (Figure~3.5, pp.~206--207).

\qffield{Conditions} The fit is descriptive and the authors do not report $\alpha$ or uncertainty for either exponent. ``Participation'' there is the period liquidity ratio, the order's share of market volume during its execution. Bacry, Iuga, Lasnier and Lehalle measure the same dependence on another broker sample (European metaorders, 2010) by direct regression of the signed price move at the end of execution \citep[arXiv preprint of 9 Dec.\ 2014, sec.~3.2, Table~2, p.~8]{wide-bacry-life-cycle}:
\begin{itemize}
\item on daily participation $R$ with duration, the exponents are 0.54--0.59 for $R$ and $-0.23$ to $-0.35$ for duration;
\item on the trading rate $r$ with duration, they are 0.37--0.56 for $r$ and $+0.15$ to $+0.24$ for duration.
\end{itemize}
The sign of the duration effect thus depends on what is held fixed. At fixed daily participation a longer execution trades more slowly and costs less; at a fixed participation rate a longer execution trades more and costs more. Interpretation: with impact $\propto R^aT^b$ and $R=rT$, impact $\propto r^aT^{a+b}$, and $a+b\approx0.2$--0.36 from the first rows, consistent with the second and with Lehalle and Laruelle's $\alpha>0$.

\qffield{Practice} Lehalle and Laruelle list stock characteristics (liquidity, volatility, spread) and order characteristics (aggressiveness, quantity, expected duration) as drivers, and timing as a further one. The same quantity has different impact depending on whether the order follows a trend, reverts to a mean, or follows an index rebalancing, and on whether high-frequency traders can detect it \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.2, pp.~203--204 and~206]{alpha-laruelle}. After one day they take the temporary impact to have vanished, so later moves count as permanent \citep[same sec., p.~209]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Tick size, typical depth, order side, auctions and venue access (lit and dark liquidity, queue priority, routing) are not drivers in the source. On the spread the sources disagree. Bacry et al.\ find a spread term with exponent 0.28 in a log-log fit (Table~2, (R.3)), while Almgren et al.\ find no consistent dependence of permanent impact on the spread (\hyperref[meth:almgren-impact-estimation]{direct estimation}). In the terms of \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact} the decayed part is the decay of the transient displacement, not temporary impact, which Bacry et al.\ reserve for the value at the end of execution. Only one fitted participation exponent, $\gamma\approx0.5$, is reported, so this fit does not establish concavity across datasets.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{square-root impact law}; \hyperref[meth:almgren-impact-estimation]{direct estimation of impact} (a different design); \hyperref[meth:post-execution-impact-profile]{post-execution impact profile} (the daily horizon); \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}.

\qffield{Further reading} Moro et al.\ (2009), cited by Lehalle and Laruelle for the concave path.

\end{qfevidence}

\begin{qfmethod}[Direct estimation of permanent and temporary impact from institutional orders (Almgren, Thum, Hauptmann and Li)]\label{meth:almgren-impact-estimation}
\qffield{Statement} From completed orders, Almgren, Thum, Hauptmann and Li estimate two impact functions \citep[authors' preprint of 10 May 2005, secs.~2.2--4.3, pp.~8--21]{wide-almgren-direct-impact}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For each order measure three prices (p.~9): the pre-trade midpoint $S_0$, from the last quote before the first transaction; the realised average price $\bar S$; and the post-trade midpoint $S_{\mathrm{post}}$, from the first quote at least half an hour after the last execution, carried to the next morning if that falls after the close.
\item Define the permanent impact $I=(S_{\mathrm{post}}-S_0)/S_0$ and the realised impact $J=(\bar S-S_0)/S_0$, both signed so that a cost has the sign of the order (p.~10).
\item Measure time in volume time, the fraction of an average day's volume executed so far (p.~10). The order of $X$ shares is executed at a constant rate over $T$, with $V$ the 10-day average daily volume, $\sigma$ an intraday estimate of daily volatility, and $\Theta$ the shares outstanding (pp.~11--13 and~19).
\item Fit, with heteroskedastic weights from the model's error terms (eqs.~(7)--(8), p.~19)
\[ \frac{I}{\sigma}=\gamma\,T\,\mathrm{sgn}(X)\left|\frac{X}{VT}\right|^{\alpha}\left(\frac{\Theta}{V}\right)^{\delta}+\text{noise},\qquad \frac1\sigma\left(J-\frac I2\right)=\eta\,\mathrm{sgn}(X)\left|\frac{X}{VT}\right|^{\beta}+\text{noise}. \]
\end{enumerate}
The term $I/2$ removes the effect of the order's own permanent impact on its later fills, so $J-I/2$ is the temporary impact (eq.~(2), p.~15).

\qffield{Notation} Here $I$ and $J$ are the paper's impact variables, not the impact process $I_t$ of other entries; $\gamma$, $\eta$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\delta$ are its coefficients and exponents, not the Almgren--Chriss $\gamma$ and $\eta$ of \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}.

\qffield{Conditions} The sample is Citigroup US equity orders from December 2001 to June 2003: 682{,}562 orders before filtering, 29{,}509 after (pp.~5--8). The filters keep S\&P~500 stocks with daily volatility below 12.5 percent. They exclude market-on-open, market-on-close and VWAP orders and orders with executions after 4:10~pm, and keep orders of at least two transactions, 1{,}000 shares and 0.25 percent of average daily volume. The median order lasts about half an hour; most are a few percent of daily volume or less (pp.~6--8). The model assumes a constant trading rate in volume time and no cross-impact. It makes permanent impact independent of the schedule, so stealth trading is excluded by construction (p.~13).

\qffield{Practice} The estimates are $\alpha=0.891\pm0.10$, $\delta=0.267\pm0.22$, $\beta=0.600\pm0.038$, one standard deviation (p.~20). The authors fix $\alpha=1$, which they cannot reject, $\delta=1/4$ and $\beta=3/5$, rejecting the square root at the 95 percent level. They then find $\gamma=0.314\pm0.041$ and $\eta=0.142\pm0.0062$ \citep[same preprint, secs.~4.2--4.3, pp.~20--21]{wide-almgren-direct-impact}. The fitted model is $I=\gamma\sigma(X/V)(\Theta/V)^{1/4}$ and $J=I/2+\mathrm{sgn}(X)\,\eta\sigma|X/VT|^{3/5}$ (p.~21). In their example of a purchase of 10 percent of daily volume in IBM ($\sigma=1.57\%$, $\Theta/V=263$), permanent impact is 20~bp, and realised cost is 32, 25 and 18~bp for durations of 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5 days (Table~3, p.~22).

Interpretation: recomputing from the fitted coefficients gives 19.9~bp and 32.2, 24.6 and 18.4~bp, matching the table to its rounding; DRI likewise. Temporary impact needs no stock-specific factor beyond volume and volatility, and no consistent dependence on the bid--ask spread was found (p.~19).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The ``permanent'' impact $I$ is the move to half an hour after the last fill, chosen because the regressed values stop changing with the lag at about that point (p.~9). It is a finite-horizon measurement that can include later flow and information; compare the 20-day profile of the \hyperref[meth:post-execution-impact-profile]{post-execution method}. $R^2$ is typically below one percent, because volatility dominates the price moves of individual orders (p.~21). Filling only partly, and the exclusion of block-desk orders, could bias the sample; the authors report nearly identical coefficients with and without partly filled orders (p.~8). The fit reflects US large caps over 2001--2003; the authors expect coefficients, exponents and forms to be re-estimated (pp.~21--22).

They prefer linear permanent impact because, citing Huberman and Stanzl (2004), it is the only form free of arbitrage, while nonlinear temporary impact introduces none (p.~16); see \hyperref[res:no-price-manipulation]{absence of price manipulation} for the conditions in other models.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact} (the Almgren--Chriss framework behind the model); \hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{square-root impact law} (an exponent of 0.6 here); \hyperref[ev:intraday-impact-profile]{intraday impact profile}; \hyperref[meth:post-execution-impact-profile]{post-execution impact profile}.

\qffield{Further reading} The paper's published version, \emph{Risk} 18(7), 2005.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Order-flow impact regression: the Glosten--Harris decomposition into permanent and transitory components]\label{meth:order-flow-impact-regression}
\qffield{Statement} Harris presents the Glosten--Harris model as an order-flow transaction-cost estimator \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', box, p.~435]{alpha-harris}. The trade price is fundamental value plus a transitory component, $P_t=V_t+\theta_t$ with $\theta_t=aq_t+bq_tS_t$. Value changes by a permanent effect proportional to signed size plus other information, $\Delta V_t=\lambda q_tS_t+\varepsilon_t$. Hence
\[ \Delta P_t=\lambda\,q_tS_t+a\,\Delta q_t+b\,\Delta(q_tS_t)+\varepsilon_t, \]
estimated by regressing the price change on signed size, the change in sign and the change in signed size. Hasbrouck states the model with a size-independent permanent term as well: $m_t=m_{t-1}+u_t+q_t(\lambda_0+\lambda_1V_t)$, $p_t=m_t+q_t(c_0+c_1V_t)$ \citep[sec.~9.8.1, eq.~(9.26), p.~91]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Notation} $q_t=\pm1$ the trade sign (house convention); $S_t$ (Harris's Size) or $V_t$ (Hasbrouck) the unsigned size. Interpretation: $a$ is half the fixed transitory spread per side and $\lambda$ the permanent effect per share, as in the spread-component entries.

\qffield{Conditions} Interpretation: differencing Hasbrouck's form gives $\Delta p_t=\lambda_0q_t+\lambda_1q_tV_t+c_0\Delta q_t+c_1\Delta(q_tV_t)+u_t$. Harris's regression, and the simplified one, are the special case $\lambda_0=0$. That follows Harris's statement that information theory suggests a permanent effect proportional to size (p.~435), but it drops a term the general model allows. Glosten and Harris's own estimation treated prices as rounded to the tick and trade signs as latent, using a nonlinear filter, and assumed size and sign exogenous with normal public news \citep[sec.~9.8.1, pp.~91--92]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Practice} Estimated size effects are generally concave, flattening at large sizes, and signed size often adds little beyond the sign. One explanation is time-varying liquidity: traders trade large when impact is low. The index can be trades (event time) or fixed intervals (clock time): event time favours stationarity for one security, clock time comparability across securities. Trade prices suit execution-cost questions; quote midpoints suit long-run impact and are less noisy \citep[secs.~9.7.2--9.7.4, pp.~89--91]{s6-hasbrouck}. Conditioning on an index return, or better on recent index-futures returns, matters when attributing price changes to a stock's own trades \citep[sec.~9.6, p.~87]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Harris writes that ``the statistical properties of standard regression methods ensure that the regression error term is uncorrelated with the independent regressor variables'' (p.~435). Interpretation: least squares makes the fitted residuals orthogonal to the regressors by construction, but the coefficients estimate the permanent and transitory effects only if the true $\varepsilon_t$ is uncorrelated with them. That is an assumption, Glosten and Harris's exogeneity, not a consequence of the method. Signed order flow is chosen by traders who may trade on information or on the same news.

Coefficients then mix reaction with prediction, as in \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}. Of the usual controls (market moves, time of day, volatility, spread, order splitting), the sources support an index return in the conditioning set (Hasbrouck, p.~87), volume time (\hyperref[meth:almgren-impact-estimation]{Almgren et al.}) and volatility and spread terms (\hyperref[ev:intraday-impact-profile]{Bacry et al.}). Controls reduce omitted-variable bias but do not by themselves make the coefficients causal (Interpretation); random trading is the design that does (\hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}, Practice).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:spread-component-identification]{spread component identification} (the generalized Roll model, the size-free special case); \hyperref[res:adverse-selection-spread-component]{spread components}; \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}; \hyperref[mod:propagator]{propagator model} (impact that decays over many trades rather than one).

\qffield{Further reading} Glosten and Harris (1988).

\end{qfmethod}
