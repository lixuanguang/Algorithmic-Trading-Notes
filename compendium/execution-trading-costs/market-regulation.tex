\section{Market regulation, venue competition and circuit breakers}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Regulation as market design: who regulates, what market-design questions are, how to evaluate a rule, and the political economy of rule-making]\label{def:market-regulation-design}
\qffield{Statement} ``Regulators create and enforce rules that facilitate trading''; good rules help traders communicate, prevent fraud and make ``all things generally \dots\ as they appear'', while other rules give favoured traders, brokers or exchanges protection from competition, protect domestic or incumbent markets, express ideology or raise revenue \citep[chap.~3, sec.~3.5, pp.~59--60]{alpha-harris}. ``The stated purpose of a regulation often is not its true objective'' (p.~60).

\emph{Who regulates.} Legislatures set the framework; government agencies (in the United States the SEC and CFTC, with the Federal Reserve setting speculative margins) write and enforce regulations; self-regulatory organisations (exchanges, clearinghouses and trader associations) regulate their members under rules approved by the agency; private bodies set accounting and performance standards; international organisations (IOSCO, the World Federation of Exchanges) coordinate (sec.~3.5.1--3.5.2, pp.~60--66). Harris's summary: ``Legislatures provide the regulatory framework, the SROs provide the details, and governmental regulatory agencies provide oversight'' (p.~66).

\emph{Design questions} debated around 2002 included a central limit order book, quote- against order-driven systems, internalisation and preferencing, price limits and halts, floor against screen, linkages, trading hours, who sees the book, who owns market data and what may trade \citep[chap.~9, p.~202]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Evaluating a rule.} Harris's normative procedure has four parts: identify all reasonable alternative policies; specify the criteria, a social welfare function with its acceptable trade-offs; evaluate each alternative by positive analysis; and choose the one with the greatest welfare; ``When policy becomes the objective rather than the means to the objective, poor results often follow'' (chap.~9, box, p.~204). His own objectives, stated as opinion, rank the private interests of utilitarian traders first, the public benefits of liquid, informative markets second, support for profit-motivated traders only when needed for those, and hostility to strategies that exploit other traders, among them manipulators, bluffers, front runners and very fast traders picking off slow dealers (sec.~9.6, pp.~216--217).

The SEC's statutory direction to consider ``efficiency, competition, and capital formation'' as well as investor protection gives no guidance on trade-offs among them (chap.~3, box, p.~61). \emph{Public and private services.} Markets charge for private services such as order routing; public services such as price continuity and the regulation of insider trading, manipulation and capital structures benefit everyone, so ``unregulated competition among market centers produces fewer public services than would be socially desirable'' (chap.~26, sec.~26.5.3, p.~538).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Political economy.} After the 1987 crash, Harris argues, regulators facing public demand for action were better off adopting circuit breakers whether or not another crash followed, and mild rather than severe ones (\emph{regulatory risk}, Tables~28-1 and~28-2); the NYSE's Rule~80A collar on index arbitrage benefited specialists, who outnumbered arbitrageurs among members (470 of 1{,}366 members were full-time specialists in November 1997, while 20 members did 95 percent of program trades), an example of rent seeking (\emph{regulatory capture}) \citep[chap.~28, sec.~28.4, pp.~578--581]{alpha-harris}. ``After a crisis, regulators often adopt regulations that have little economic value simply to respond to public demands for action'' (p.~582).

\emph{Unintended consequences}: Brazil's 0.38 percent tax on financial transactions of 1997 moved institutional trading in Brazilian stocks to ADRs in New York; Bovespa volume fell from 1.2 billion reais a day in 1997 to 350 million in 2001, partly because of the 2001 crisis, and in September 2001 the government announced an exemption for stock trades (chap.~3, box, p.~66). Exposure protection is a design choice with its own trade-offs: time precedence with a meaningful tick, undisclosed orders, audit trails and delayed reporting (\hyperref[def:order-exposure-decisions]{order exposure decisions}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The institutions named are as of 2002 (NASD has since become part of FINRA; national regulators listed in Harris's Table~3-11 have been renamed or merged). The ``measurement risk'', policy adopted before transitory and fundamental volatility can be separated, matches Harris's remark that empirical studies of circuit breakers ``generally have been inconclusive'' because extreme events are rare (chap.~28, p.~577), not a named category of his. A welfare ranking is opinion; the four-step procedure only makes the criteria explicit.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:market-functions]{what markets do}; \hyperref[mech:venue-competition-fragmentation]{venue competition and fragmentation}; \hyperref[mech:circuit-breakers-trading-halts]{circuit breakers and trading halts}; \hyperref[rule:us-equity-market-structure]{U.S.\ equity market structure rules}; \hyperref[rule:eu-trading-venue-transparency]{EU trading venues and transparency}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chaps.~3, 9, 26 and~28.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmechanism}[Venue competition and fragmentation: the order-flow externality, why markets segment, cross-market precedence, the post-MiFID cycle of entry, convergence and consolidation, and technology fragmentation]\label{mech:venue-competition-fragmentation}
\qffield{Statement} A market is \emph{fragmented} when essentially the same thing trades in different market centres and \emph{consolidated} when all traders trade in one; competition among traders for the best price works best in a consolidated market, while competition among market centres to offer services implies fragmentation, ``The two competitions therefore are inconsistent'' \citep[chap.~26, p.~524]{alpha-harris}. \emph{Consolidating force}: each trader who joins a market adds liquidity that attracts others, the \emph{order-flow externality}, a network externality that makes trading winner-take-all and new entry hard (secs.~26.2 and~26.5.1, pp.~526 and~535--536); Hasbrouck likewise calls the liquidity externality a network externality that ``favors market consolidation'' \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.2, p.~5]{s6-hasbrouck}.

\emph{Fragmenting force}: ``Markets fragment because traders are not all identical'' (sec.~26.3, p.~530): by size (large traders control exposure and value strict time precedence with an economically significant tick; small traders want exposure and small ticks), information (informed traders prefer anonymous consolidated markets; uninformed traders prefer to be identified), patience (impatient traders value dealers; patient traders value order-driven systems with time precedence), access, and creditworthiness (a single standard makes creditworthy traders subsidise others) (secs.~26.3.1--26.3.5, pp.~530--533).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Order-flow externality as an inequality.} If a trader's utility at venue $i$ is $U_i=s_i-c_i+\ell(F_i)$ (service, cost, and an increasing function of order flow), an entrant better by $(s_e-c_e)-(s_m-c_m)>0$ still loses the trader when $\ell(F_m)-\ell(F_e)$ exceeds that margin; this restatement of the proposition is a rearrangement, true whenever the trader chooses the higher utility, and its content lies in the specification (one trader, flows taken as given). With $\ell=\log$, flows of 1{,}000 against 50 and a 0.25 service advantage, the incumbent wins by 2.75 (\nolinkurl{liquidity-market-design-checks.py}).

\emph{Cross-market precedence.} A market cannot meaningfully enforce time and public-order precedence when other markets trading the same instrument do not: quote matchers trade elsewhere, small markets offer small ticks to attract them, and larger ones follow; Harris's \emph{Race to the Bottom} sidebar reads the U.S.\ tick reductions of the 1990s, which ``culminated in the full decimalization of the markets in 2001'', this way (sec.~26.5.2, pp.~536--538); his sidebar \emph{Lost Time Precedence} gives the 2002 U.S.\ example of regional specialists and third-market dealers filling orders at prices first quoted on the NYSE (p.~537). The U.S.\ answer was a trade-through rule (\hyperref[rule:us-equity-market-structure]{U.S.\ equity market structure rules}); in Europe, best-execution policies took its place \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.1.2, p.~43]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Post-MiFID Europe, as Lehalle and Laruelle describe it.} MiFID, in force from 1 November 2007, abolished concentration rules and introduced brokers' best-execution policies; instead of many venues a few appeared, each running several pools, and ``the agents \dots\ inherited the crucial role of consolidating the liquidity'' \citep[Introduction, p.~17]{alpha-laruelle}. Their phases (chap.~1, secs.~1.1.2--1.1.4, pp.~39--62): first attempts (Chi-X, Turquoise, BATS Europe and NASDAQ OMX Europe, opened country by country, Table~1.1, p.~40); convergence towards pan-European offers and a few groups (the LSE Group with Turquoise; BATS and Chi-X; Euronext), which then segmented liquidity into niche pools, ``natural'' against ``opportunistic'' (pp.~41--42); and broker crossing networks and MTF dark pools (pp.~54--56).

Their two cautionary cases: Turquoise, founded by nine banks with a market-making agreement whose end made its share drop (pp.~47--48), and NASDAQ OMX's MTF, which ``almost never crossed 1\% of the market share'' and closed (pp.~49--50). Consolidation followed: BATS acquired Chi-X Europe in November 2011, a Deutsche Börse--NYSE Euronext merger was rejected by the European Commission, ICE bought NYSE Euronext in 2013, and Cboe acquired Bats in February 2017 (pp.~60--62); their summary: challengers first offer new services at lower cost, practices converge, then mergers come while ``the offer continues to expand'' (p.~62). Harris's two consolidation waves, after the telegraph and telephone and from the early 1990s, have the same drivers: the order-flow externality, a falling ratio of variable to fixed costs, and loosened cross-border restrictions (sec.~26.6, pp.~538--539).

\emph{Technology fragmentation}: application protocols (XDP, ITCH, EBS, Pitch, EMDI, RWF), multicast UDP or TCP with venue-specific recovery, and FAST, ZLIB or no compression made a common feed handler hard to build (Lehalle and Laruelle, pp.~53--54). \emph{Shared references}: in the United States the consolidated tape lets participants decide on the same information; Lehalle and Laruelle argue that Europe needs a consolidated post-trade tape rather than reliance on primary markets, citing the Euronext outage of October 2010, during which MTF prices diverged from Euronext's by about 2 percent for some stocks (Introduction, p.~7; chap.~2, sec.~2.5.2, pp.~183--186).

\emph{Price formation across fragmented venues}. With several venues trading one security, Hasbrouck models their prices as cointegrated around a common efficient price (a vector error-correction model) and attributes price discovery by \emph{information shares}, each venue's share of the efficient price's innovation variance, reported as bounds over orderings when innovations are correlated; the venue with the largest share ``is in some ways the most important. In a statistical sense, it is the price leader'' \citep[chap.~10, secs.~10.2--10.3, pp.~99--102]{s6-hasbrouck}. Wider time intervals make events look simultaneous and widen the bounds, and the timestamps and sequencing of different markets' reports are often unreliable (pp.~104--105).

The mechanism (fragmentation improves prices while venues stay synchronised by routing, arbitrage and consolidated information, and damages price formation when they lose a common reference, primary quotes go stale or participants pause routing under stress) is consistent with Harris's consolidation mechanisms in this entry and with Lehalle and Laruelle's outage account (\hyperref[ev:european-fragmentation-evidence]{European fragmentation evidence}); as a general claim it is the interpretation's synthesis and was not tested.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Lehalle and Laruelle's dates are not all consistent: the end of Turquoise's market-making agreement is March 2009 in Table~1.1 and on p.~47 but March 2008 on p.~53; NASDAQ OMX Europe runs ``from September 2008 to May 2010'' on p.~49 but launches in May 2009 and closes in July 2010 in Table~1.1; the LSE's acquisition of Turquoise is placed in February 2011 (pp.~16 and~60); and the Euronext outage is dated 13 October 2010 in the text and 10 October 2010, a Sunday, in the figure captions. These were not checked against primary sources and are reported as the book gives them. The statement that segmentation separates ``natural'' from ``opportunistic'' liquidity is the authors' interpretation, with Menkveld cited for opportunistic liquidity (p.~42).

Their merger-wave chronology is to 2017; current ownership was not checked. Harris's examples (ITS, SOES) are U.S.\ institutions of 2002. The caveat \emph{Systemic Risk Link} says fragmentation is not systemic risk by itself but becomes dangerous when participants react to stress in the same direction, related venues desynchronise, or a liquidity withdrawal spreads to hedges, ETFs, futures and correlated stocks. Lehalle and Laruelle frame the question the same way, as a debate between supporters of high-frequency trading, who say it lowers volatility by adding liquidity, and opponents, who cite the Flash Crash, and conclude that the Flash Crash ``is not an obvious evidence of an increase in systemic risk'' \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.5, pp.~169 and~177]{alpha-laruelle}; the 2010 staff report's lessons on simultaneous withdrawal and interconnected markets are in \hyperref[ev:may-2010-flash-crash]{the May 2010 Flash Crash}. The conditions in the caveat are plausible mechanisms, not established findings.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[ev:european-fragmentation-evidence]{European fragmentation evidence}; \hyperref[meth:fragmentation-concentration-metrics]{fragmentation and concentration metrics}; \hyperref[def:smart-order-routing]{smart order routing}; \hyperref[def:dark-pools-crossing]{dark pools and crossing networks}; \hyperref[def:market-structures-venues]{market structures and venues}; \hyperref[def:fixed-income-market-structure]{fixed-income market structure}.

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chap.~26; Lehalle and Laruelle, introduction and chap.~1, sec.~1.1.

\end{qfmechanism}

\begin{qfrule}[U.S.\ equity market structure rules: decimal pricing, Regulation NMS and the minimum pricing increment, single-stock and market-wide circuit breakers, stub quotes, market access controls and the consolidated audit trail (status as of 16 October 2026)]\label{rule:us-equity-market-structure}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Decimal pricing.} On 8 June 2000 the SEC ordered the exchanges and the NASD to submit a plan phasing in decimal pricing for stocks and options, starting no later than 5 September 2000 \citep[Release 34-42914]{sec-34-42914}; the conversion of consolidated quotations ``was successfully completed on April 9, 2001'', narrowing the minimum price variation from $1/16$ of a dollar to a penny \citep[Release 34-44568, p.~38390]{sec-34-44568}.

\emph{Regulation NMS} (Release 34-51808, effective 29 August 2005) adopted the Order Protection Rule (policies and procedures to prevent trades at prices inferior to protected quotations, which must be immediately and automatically accessible), the Access Rule (fair access, a cap on access fees, and a ban on patterns of locking or crossing quotations), the Sub-Penny Rule (no orders, quotes or indications in increments below a penny except when priced below \$1.00) and amended market data rules \citep[p.~37496]{sec-34-51808}; its phased compliance dates were not read.

\emph{Minimum pricing increment, current text.} As amended in 2024 \citep[Release 34-101070, catalogue entry]{ex-sec-tick-access-2024}, 17 CFR 242.612 sets, for orders priced at \$1.00 or more, an increment of \$0.01 if a stock's time-weighted average quoted spread over the evaluation period (January--March or July--September) exceeded \$0.015 and \$0.005 if it did not, operative from the first business day of May and of November; \$0.0001 below \$1.00 \citep[text for 14 October 2026]{cfr-242-612}.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Adoption, stay and compliance of the 2024 amendments.} Adopted 18 September 2024 (Release 34-101070), the amendments also cut the access-fee caps of Rule 610(c) to \$0.001 per share for quotations at \$1.00 or more and 0.1 percent of the price below, required exchange fees and rebates to be determinable at execution (Rule 610(d)), and accelerated the round-lot and odd-lot information definitions. On 12 December 2024 the Commission stayed the amendments to Rules 600(b)(89)(i)(F), 610(c) and 612 pending judicial review, not those to 610(d), 603(b) or the round-lot and odd-lot definitions; the D.C.\ Circuit denied the petitions on 14 October 2025; on 31 October 2025 the Commission exempted compliance with the three stayed rules until the first business day of November 2026; and on 11 June 2026 it extended the exemption from compliance with Rules 600(b)(89)(i)(F) and 612 (the amended minimum pricing increment) and 610(c) (the amended fee caps) to ``the first business day of November 2027'' \citep[91~FR 36022--36024]{sec-34-105656}.

\emph{Effective text against compliance relief}. The amendments took effect on 9 December 2024 \citep[document record]{fr-2024-21867}, and the amended rule text is in the CFR \citep[text for 14 October 2026]{cfr-242-612}; the 2024 stay suspended the effect of three of them pending judicial review, and the October 2025 relief was granted ``in anticipation of an end to the partial stay upon the completion of judicial review''. What the 2025 and 2026 orders grant is exemptive relief from \emph{compliance}, not a change to the rule text: as of 16 October 2026 the amended Rules 600(b)(89)(i)(F), 610(c) and 612 are effective rules whose compliance is exempted until the first business day of November 2027 \citep[pp.~36022--36024]{sec-34-105656}. The June 2026 order cites the competing 2026 initiatives (23/5 trading, Rule 605 compliance) and the systems and rule-filing work the amendments need.

\emph{Odd-lot information.} The adopting release set the first business day of May 2026 as the compliance date for odd-lot information. On 15 January 2026 the Commission exempted the participants of the consolidated-data plans from Rule 600(b)(69)(ii), odd lots priced at or between the national best bid and offer ``aggregated at each price level at each national securities exchange and national securities association'' (aggregate odd-lot depth), until the first business day of May 2028. The exemption does not cover Rule 600(b)(69)(iii), the best odd-lot order (the highest odd-lot bid above the national best bid and the lowest odd-lot offer below the national best offer), which the request letter said the exclusive SIPs would begin disseminating in May 2026 \citep[pp.~1--5]{sec-34-104612}.

Whether that dissemination began on schedule, and the request letter's statement that the round-lot definition was implemented in November 2025, were not checked against the plans' own records. \emph{Circuit breakers and quoting after 6 May 2010.} Single-stock circuit breakers (five-minute pauses after a 10 percent move in five minutes) were adopted in June 2010 for S\&P 500 stocks and extended to all NMS securities in June 2011, per Lehalle and Laruelle \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.5.2, pp.~181--182]{alpha-laruelle}. On 31 May 2012 the Commission approved, as a one-year pilot, the NMS Plan to Address Extraordinary Market Volatility (\emph{limit up--limit down}), which prevents trades outside price bands around a reference price, with bands, for stocks priced above \$3.00, of 5 percent for Tier~1 stocks (S\&P 500, Russell 1000, certain ETPs) and 10 percent for Tier~2 stocks, 20 percent from \$0.75 to \$3.00, and the lesser of \$0.15 or 75 percent below \$0.75, with a proposed implementation date of 4 February 2013 \citep[Release 34-67091]{sec-34-67091}; on 11 April 2019 it approved the plan's operation ``on a permanent basis'' \citep[Release 34-85623]{sec-34-85623}.

On the same day in 2012 it approved, as a pilot, revised \emph{market-wide circuit breakers}: the S\&P 500 replaced the Dow Jones Industrial Average, triggers recalculated daily fell from 10, 20 and 30 percent to 7, 13 and 20 percent, Level~1 and~2 declines halt trading for 15 minutes before 3:25 p.m.\ and not after, and Level~3 halts trading for the day \citep[Release 34-67090]{sec-34-67090}; exchange filings extended that pilot in 2019 and 2020 (to 18 October 2021 in one filing) \citep[MEMX filing, sec.~II.A.1]{sec-fr-2020-23011}. On 16 March 2022 the Commission approved, on an accelerated basis, NYSE's proposal, as modified by Amendment No.~1, to make its market-wide circuit-breaker rule (Rule~7.12) permanent ``in their current form'', after a working group's study of the four Level~1 halts of March 2020; the pilot period was to expire on 18 March 2022.

Amendment No.~1 requires participation in industry-wide testing, a report to the Commission after any Level~1, 2 or~3 halt, and a review after 5 percent market declines or changes to reopening processes \citep[Release 34-94441, pp.~16286--16296]{sec-34-94441}. \emph{Other venues and later changes} (read 2026-10-16 in the Federal Register). Nasdaq filed on 12 April 2022 ``to adopt the same change to make permanent the MWCB pilot program in Equity~4, Rule~4121'', and FINRA on 13 April 2022 for Rule~6121.02 (market-wide circuit breakers in NMS stocks), both immediately effective \citep[87~FR 23296 and 23291]{fr-2022-mwcb-permanent}; the API search returned similar April 2022 filings by the other NYSE, Cboe, Nasdaq, IEX, MEMX and LTSE exchanges, which were listed, not read.

The triggers remain 7, 13 and 20 percent of the prior S\&P~500 close, with 15-minute Level~1 and~2 halts only between 9:30 a.m.\ and 3:25 p.m.\ and a Level~3 halt for the rest of the day; on 4 September 2026 NYSE filed, immediately effective, an amendment to Rule~7.12 so that after a Level~3 halt no SRO resumes before 4:00 a.m.\ ET the next trading day once 23-hour trading begins (several exchanges planned overnight sessions from 9:00 p.m.\ to 4:00 a.m.\ from 6 December 2026), ``in coordination with the other SROs'' \citep[91~FR 59278]{fr-2026-19125}; Nasdaq filed the same change to Rule~4121 \citep[91~FR 64439]{fr-2026-20605}.

\emph{LULD Plan after 2019.} The Commission's list of plan actions runs from the Eighteenth Amendment (permanent operation, 11 April 2019) through the Twentieth (approved 21 April 2020, clarifying the quarterly data the participants report) and amendments of 2020, 2025 and 2026; it disapproved the Twenty-Third Amendment on 16 September 2024, which would have put all ETPs except single-stock ETPs in Tier~1 with tighter bands \citep[89~FR 77203]{fr-2024-21508}; on 5 August 2026 it approved the Twenty-Seventh, a first phase of ``Overnight Price Bands'' for 9:00 p.m.\ to 4:00 a.m.\ ET, set by each stock's primary listing exchange, with trading centres open then required to prevent trades outside them \citep[91~FR 51515]{fr-2026-16201}; and on 7 October 2026 it put the Twenty-Ninth into effect for up to 120 days, moving the time of the overnight consolidated reference price from 7:45 to 7:30 p.m. \citep[91~FR 65016]{fr-2026-20799}.

The Twenty-First, Twenty-Second, Twenty-Fifth, Twenty-Sixth and Twenty-Eighth amendments were seen only as release numbers in that list and not read. \emph{Stub quotes.} The Commission approved SRO rules, implemented 6 December 2010, requiring registered market makers to quote within 8 percent of the NBBO for securities under the single-stock circuit breakers (readjusting at 9.5 percent), 20 percent outside the breakers' hours and 30 percent for other securities \citep[Release 34-63255]{sec-34-63255}.

\emph{Market access.} Rule 15c3-5 (Release 34-63241, dated 3 November 2010; effective 14 January 2011, compliance 14 July 2011) requires brokers with market access to control pre-set credit and capital thresholds and regulatory requirements under their own direct control \citep[paras.~(b)--(c)]{cfr-240-15c3-5}. \emph{Consolidated audit trail.} Rule 613 (Release 34-67457, effective 1 October 2012) required the SROs to submit an NMS plan for a consolidated order-tracking system from order inception through routing, cancellation, modification or execution \citep[p.~45722, summary and dates]{sec-34-67457}; the CAT NMS Plan was approved on 15 November 2016 \citep[Release 34-79318]{sec-34-79318}.

\emph{Since 2016} (read 2026-10-16 in the Federal Register). On 25 July 2025 the Eleventh Circuit vacated the Commission's 2023 order approving the ``Executed Share Model'' for funding CAT, as arbitrary and capricious because the Commission had not explained why it did not bar SROs from passing their CAT costs through to broker-dealers and had not incorporated actual CAT costs in its economic analysis; the court did not question the model's allocation and stayed its judgment for sixty days after its mandate \citep[secs.~I--II]{fr-2026-05350}. CAT LLC refiled the model on 5 September 2025 with a promise by each participant not to file pass-through fees, and the Commission approved it, as modified, on 16 March 2026 \citep[sec.~I, 91~FR 13410]{fr-2026-05350}.

On 13 January 2026 it approved, as modified by two participant amendments and by the Commission, an amendment that proposed to codify earlier exemptions and to end the reporting of names, addresses and years of birth for all customers, keeping the anonymised customer identifiers (CCIDs), with the customer database renamed the ``Reference Database'' in Amendment No.~1 (the Commission's modifications were not read in detail) \citep[sec.~I and nn.~25--38]{fr-2026-00762}; on 27 March 2026 it approved further cost reductions, noting that operating costs had ``far exceeded'' the \$55.8 million a year estimated in 2016 \citep[secs.~I--II, 91~FR 16284]{fr-2026-06255}; and on 20 April 2026 it published a concept release on a ``comprehensive review'' of CAT and other audit trails, including their funding, with comments due by 22 June 2026 \citep[91~FR 20945]{fr-2026-07651}. The current technical specifications \citep[catalogue entry only]{wide-sec-cat} and the court's opinion itself were not read; the litigation is known here from the Commission's and CAT LLC's accounts.

\qffield{Practice} Historical claims to keep apart: Harris's account of trade-through and time-precedence problems before Regulation NMS (\hyperref[mech:venue-competition-fragmentation]{venue competition and fragmentation}) and his description of market-wide halts at 10, 20 and 30 percent of the DJIA after 1998 \citep[chap.~28, box, p.~573]{alpha-harris} predate the 2012 rules. Lehalle and Laruelle date the stub-quote approval 8 November 2010; the approval order in the Federal Register is dated 5 November 2010. They date the vote on Rule 613 11 July 2012, which the Commission's 2026 order also gives as the adoption date; the adopting release is dated 18 July 2012 \citep[sec.~II]{fr-2026-05350} \citep[n.~3]{fr-2026-00762}. The marketrule ``Decimalization \dots\ narrowed quoted spreads but reduced displayed depth and queue stability'' is evidence, summarised in \hyperref[ev:tick-size-market-quality]{tick size and market quality}; ``Reg NMS links U.S.\ equity venues through protected quotes'' omits that protection covers only automated top-of-book quotations (Rule 611, \hyperref[def:best-execution]{best execution}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Keep effective rule text and compliance relief apart: the CFR shows the amended Rule 612 and fee caps, but compliance with them is exempted until November 2027; and the odd-lot exemption to May 2028 covers aggregate odd-lot depth, not the best odd-lot order. The compliance status of the 2024 tick and access-fee amendments has changed three times; recheck the SEC's exemptive orders before relying on a date. Lehalle and Laruelle's single-stock circuit-breaker parameters were not checked against the 2010 approval orders. Rule texts are from the eCFR point-in-time edition for 14 October 2026; SEC releases were read in the Federal Register, except Release 34-104612, read in the SEC's PDF; Release 34-105656 was read in both.

\qffield{Further reading} Lehalle and Laruelle, chap.~2, sec.~2.5.2.

\begin{qfrule}[EU trading venues and pre-trade transparency: MiFID and MiFID II dates, venue types, waivers, and the double volume cap replaced by a single volume cap (status as of 16 October 2026)]\label{rule:eu-trading-venue-transparency}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Dates.} MiFID (Directive 2004/39/EC of 21 April 2004) applied from 1 November 2007, after Directive 2006/31/EC postponed its transposition deadline to 31 January 2007 \citep[Art.~1, points (4)--(5)]{eu-dir-2006-31}. MiFID~II (Directive 2014/65/EU) and MiFIR (Regulation (EU) No 600/2014) were to apply from 3 January 2017 \citep[Art.~93]{eu-dir-2014-65} \citep[Art.~55]{eu-reg-600-2014}; Directive (EU) 2016/1034 and Regulation (EU) 2016/1033, both of 23 June 2016, deferred them by twelve months to 3 January 2018 \citep[Art.~1, points (7)--(8)]{eu-dir-2016-1034} \citep[recital and Art.~1]{eu-reg-2016-1033}.

\emph{Venue types} under MiFID~II: a \emph{regulated market} and an \emph{MTF} are multilateral systems bringing together third-party interests under non-discretionary rules; an \emph{OTF} is a multilateral system that is neither, for bonds, structured finance products, emission allowances or derivatives; a \emph{trading venue} is any of the three \citep[Art.~4(1), points (21)--(24)]{eu-dir-2014-65}. \emph{Systematic internaliser, historical wording.} As adopted in 2014 and applied from 3 January 2018, MiFID~II defined a systematic internaliser as an investment firm which, ``on an organised, frequent systematic and substantial basis'', deals on own account when executing client orders outside a regulated market, an MTF or an OTF without operating a multilateral system.

The frequent and systematic basis was measured by the number of OTC trades executed on own account for clients, the substantial basis by the size of that OTC trading relative to the firm's own or the Union's total trading in the instrument, and the definition applied ``only where the pre-set limits \dots\ are both crossed or where an investment firm chooses to opt-in'' \citep[Art.~4(1), point (20)]{eu-dir-2014-65}.

\emph{Current wording.} Directive (EU) 2024/790 replaced point (20): a systematic internaliser ``means an investment firm which, on an organised, frequent and systematic basis, deals on own account in equity instruments by executing client orders outside a regulated market, an MTF or an OTF, without operating a multilateral system, or which opts in to the status of systematic internaliser'' \citep[Art.~1, point (3)(b)]{eu-dir-2024-790}. Three things changed. The \emph{substantial-basis} criterion is gone. The status that follows from a firm's own trading covers \emph{equity instruments} only, and the pre-set quantitative limits give way to a qualitative assessment, because the quantitative criteria imposed ``an excessive burden'' on firms and on ESMA and because Regulation (EU) 2024/791 removes systematic internalisers from the pre-trade transparency requirements for non-equity instruments.

The \emph{opt-in}, already in the 2014 text, remains, and for non-equity instruments it is now the only route: ``It should, however, be possible for an investment firm to opt in to become a systematic internaliser for non-equity instruments'' \citep[recital (7)]{eu-dir-2024-790}. Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/392 reads the amendment the same way (it ``replaced the quantitative criteria \dots\ with a qualitative assessment and limited that assessment to equity instruments only, while leaving the possibility \dots\ to opt in'') and gives that as the reason to delete from Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/577 the obligation on trading venues, APAs and CTPs to supply the data for the quantitative calculations of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/565, Articles~12--15 \citep[recital (1)]{eu-reg-del-2026-392}; the deleted provisions of Regulation 2017/577 themselves were not read.

\emph{Dark trading} uses the \emph{reference price waiver} and the \emph{negotiated trade waiver} of MiFIR Article 4(1)(a) and (b)(i) \citep[recital on the volume cap]{ex-eu-mifir-review-2024}. \emph{The four equity waivers and their thresholds} (read 2026-10-16). MiFIR Article~4(1) lets competent authorities waive pre-trade transparency for (a) systems matching at a reference price derived from the first-admission venue or the most relevant market, which must be that venue's current midpoint, or its opening or closing price outside continuous trading; (b) negotiated transactions within the current volume-weighted spread, in illiquid instruments within a set percentage of a reference price, or subject to conditions other than the current market price; (c) orders large in scale; and (d) orders held in an order management facility pending disclosure \citep[Art.~4(1)--(2)]{eu-reg-600-2014}; Regulation (EU) 2024/791 removed the volume-cap condition from (b)(i) \citep[Art.~1(3)]{ex-eu-mifir-review-2024}.

Large in scale for shares and depositary receipts is a minimum order size by the instrument's EU average daily turnover (ADT): \texteuro15{,}000 below \texteuro50{,}000 ADT, then 30{,}000, 60{,}000, 100{,}000, 200{,}000, 300{,}000, 400{,}000 and 500{,}000, and \texteuro650{,}000 at \texteuro100 million ADT or more, with ADT computed from the previous calendar year's EU trading on and off venue \citep[Art.~7 and Annex~II, Table~1]{eu-rts1-2017-587}; for ETFs it was \texteuro1 million and has been \texteuro3 million since Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/944 entered into force on 5 June 2023 \citep[Art.~1(4)]{eu-rts1-amend-2023-944}.

An order management facility may hold a reserve order of at least \texteuro10{,}000, or another order at least the venue's preset minimum tradable quantity \citep[Art.~8(2)]{eu-rts1-2017-587}; Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1246 (published 3 November 2025) redefined the reserve order and exempted it from the no-interaction condition, and narrowed the benchmark (VWAP or TWAP) case of negotiated transactions to periods long enough to have ``no relation to the current market price'' \citep[Art.~2(4) and~(6)]{eu-rts1-amend-2025-1246}; it left the large-in-scale table for shares unchanged, while replacing the standard-market-size table (Annex~V). Its points (2), (3)(a) and~(c), (5), (10)(a) and~(13) of Article~2 apply from 2 March 2026, the rest from entry into force (Art.~3).

A query of the Publications Office's CELLAR metadata for acts amending the two regulations returned three for 2017/587 (2019/442, 2023/944 and 2025/1246) and two for 2017/588 (2019/443 and 2023/960); an amendment not yet indexed there would be missed.

\emph{From double to single volume cap.} Under the original MiFIR Article 5, trading under those two waivers in an instrument was limited to 4 percent of total EU trading on any one venue and 8 percent across the EU over the previous 12 months; breaching either led the competent authorities to suspend the waivers within two working days for six months, on monthly ESMA data, with warning reports at 3.75 and 7.75 percent \citep[Art.~5]{eu-reg-600-2014}.

Regulation (EU) 2024/791 of 28 February 2024, published on 8 March 2024 and in force from 28 March 2024, replaced it with a single cap: trading venues suspend their own use of the reference price waiver for three months when trading under it exceeds 7 percent of total EU trading in the instrument, based on quarterly ESMA data covering the previous 12 months; the negotiated trade waiver is no longer capped; monitoring under the new cap ``shall start on 29 September 2025''; ESMA reports annually from 29 September 2027 and the Commission may adjust the threshold \citep[Art.~1(4), amending MiFIR Art.~5]{ex-eu-mifir-review-2024}.

\emph{Transition.} ESMA stated on 27 March 2024 that the double cap would continue, for both waivers, until the single cap applied \citep[p.~2]{esma-transition-statement-2024}; its double-volume-cap page records that the double cap was discontinued on 28 September 2025, ending any ongoing suspension, and that the first single-cap file was planned for 9 October 2025 \citep[retrieved 2026-10-16]{esma-dvc-page}; on 10 October 2025 ESMA confirmed that the single cap ``has become operational with the first calculation results published on 9 October 2025'' \citep[p.~3]{esma-mifir-review-statement-2025}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Lehalle and Laruelle's account (2018).} MiFID's ``few and simple mechanisms'' were best execution, three venue statuses and transparency (chap.~1, sec.~1.1.2, p.~42): regulated markets with public books, responsible for circuit breakers; MTFs, often pegged to regulated markets; and systematic internalisers, which few firms chose because of the transparency attached (pp.~43--44); the pre-trade waivers were large in scale, order management facility, negotiated trade and reference price, under which dark pools operated (p.~45). Writing before MiFID~II applied, they expected a dark cap of 4 and 8 percent enforced yearly with six-month suspensions and a tick-size regime set by price and trading activity (Introduction, pp.~8--9); the first matched the original Article~5, the second is MiFID~II Article~49, which requires tick-size regimes calibrated to liquidity and average spread \citep[Art.~49]{eu-dir-2014-65}.

\emph{The tick-size regime now} (read 2026-10-16). For shares and depositary receipts, trading venues must apply a tick at least equal to the one in the Annex table for the instrument's liquidity band, set by the average daily number of transactions (ADNT) on its most relevant market (six bands: below 10, 10--80, 80--600, 600--2{,}000, 2{,}000--9{,}000 and 9{,}000 or more), and for the order's price range (19 ranges from below 0.1 to 50{,}000 and above); ETFs whose underlyings are solely such equities take the most liquid band; a share whose most relevant market runs only a periodic auction takes the least liquid band \citep[Arts.~2--3 and Annex]{eu-rts11-2017-588}.

Competent authorities publish each ADNT by 1~March from the previous calendar year's trades, excluding reference-price, negotiated and large-in-scale transactions; venues apply the new band from the first Monday of April (originally 1~April) \citep[Art.~3(1), (2) and~(4)]{eu-rts11-2017-588} \citep[Art.~1]{eu-rts11-amend-2023-960}; since 2019 the authority may adjust the ADNT of a share whose highest-turnover venue is in a third country to include that venue's transactions \citep[Art.~1, new Art.~3(8)--(10)]{eu-rts11-amend-2019-443}. The regulation applied from 3 January 2018; the 2019 amendment was published on 20 March 2019 and the 2023 one on 16 May 2023, each in force twenty days later.

Derived from the table (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/execution-market-making/market-estimators-rules-checks.py}, section~6): for prices of 0.5 and above the relative tick runs from 1 to 2.5~bp in the most liquid band and from 40 to 100~bp in the least liquid one; a share at \texteuro25 has a tick of 0.005 with 12{,}000 trades a day, 0.05 with 300 and 0.2 with five.

\emph{Systematic internalisers} must quote, improve and execute on the same ticks since MiFIR Article~17a, inserted by Regulation (EU) 2019/2033 and applicable from 26 March 2020, which allowed midpoint matching of orders large in scale \citep[Art.~63(3) and Art.~67(3)(a)]{eu-reg-2019-2033}; Regulation (EU) 2024/791 replaced Article~17a so that neither the tick regime nor Article~15(2) prevents systematic internalisers ``from matching orders at midpoint'', with no size condition and no later application date stated in the amending act \citep[Art.~1(16)]{ex-eu-mifir-review-2024}. Directive (EU) 2024/790 lets regulated markets apply a third-country venue's tick to shares with a non-EEA ISIN, or traded in a non-EEA currency, whose most relevant market is outside the EU \citep[Art.~1(8), amending Art.~49(2)]{eu-dir-2024-790}, through national transposition.

\emph{Payment for order flow}: the 2024 amendments prohibit investment firms from receiving payment for forwarding or executing retail and opted-in professional client orders, with a Member-State exemption possible until 30 June 2026 for firms already doing so \citep[Art.~1, new MiFIR Art.~39a]{ex-eu-mifir-review-2024}.

\begin{qfmechanism}[Circuit breakers and trading halts: halts, price limits, taxes, margins and collars; when a halt helps; the gravitational effect, propagation and related instruments; incidence by trader type]\label{mech:circuit-breakers-trading-halts}
\qffield{Statement} Harris: ``Circuit breakers are trading rules that limit trading activity'': trading halts, price limits, transaction taxes, margin requirements and position limits, and collars that restrict access to trading systems \citep[chap.~28, sec.~28.3, p.~572]{alpha-harris}. Lehalle and Laruelle classify thresholds as \emph{static} (``a fixed price computed before the opening'') or \emph{dynamic} (``a corridor following the recent values of the price''), and breakers as \emph{local} (one instrument) or \emph{global} (a collection, generally all) \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.5.3, pp.~187--188]{alpha-laruelle}. MiFID~II requires regulated markets to be able ``to temporarily halt or constrain trading if there is a significant price movement in a financial instrument on that market or a related market during a short period'', with calibrated parameters reported to ESMA, and a material venue that halts must let authorities coordinate a market-wide response \citep[Art.~48(5)]{eu-dir-2014-65}. U.S.\ rules are in \hyperref[rule:us-equity-market-structure]{U.S.\ equity market structure rules}.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{When a halt helps} (Harris, sec.~28.3.1, pp.~572--575). If new fundamental information drives the move, a halt ``will merely postpone the inevitable'' and makes prices less informative meanwhile; if an imbalance among uninformed traders drives it, stopping them may help. Halts can let liquidity suppliers arrive, publicise imbalances, give time to meet margin calls and remove stop orders, and switch the pricing rule from discriminatory (continuous trading) to uniform (the reopening call), protecting standing limit orders. Against them: the \emph{gravitational effect}, traders who fear a halt ``may submit their orders earlier'', so a price-triggered rule can raise volatility near its trigger, which discretionary, less predictable halts avoid; and value traders may watch less if they expect halts.

A halt in one market diverts order flow to others trading the same risk, so uncoordinated breakers ``may increase volatility''. Harris's conclusion: the theoretical net effect on transitory volatility is indeterminate, and empirical studies ``generally have been inconclusive'' because extreme events are rare (sec.~28.3.4, pp.~576--577). \emph{One later study} (read 2026-10-16). Hautsch and Horvath study the five-minute single-stock trading pauses introduced in the United States after May 2010 (single-stock pauses from June 2010, a 10 percent move over five minutes; limit up--limit down pauses from April 2013, outside a 5 percent band around the five-minute average price for many stocks) with NASDAQ order-book data: of 1{,}513 NASDAQ pauses from 1 June 2010 to 30 June 2014 they keep 195 in 166 stocks with enough book activity, and compare them, by difference in differences, with 1{,}755 moves of more than 5 percent in five minutes in the same stocks from January 2009 to May 2010, before pauses existed (about nine control events per pause) \citep[CFS Working Paper 571, April 2017 draft, secs.~2.2, 3.1 and~4, pp.~7--17]{hautsch-horvath-2017}.

Around pauses median volatility rose about tenfold, spreads widened before the pause and normalised over the following hour, and top-two-level depth fell by about 25 percent after the pause for at least an hour (sec.~3.2, pp.~14--15). Their conclusion is a trade-off: the pause's existence lets liquidity suppliers lean against price trends, so it works as a safeguard, but volatility and spreads rise before pauses, ``in line with the `magnet-effect''', and stay higher for an hour, so pauses act ``as volatility amplifiers'' rather than cooling markets (sec.~6, pp.~40--41).

Limits: one exchange's book, a selected subsample of pauses, and a pre-regulation control period of 2009--2010 that they test for similar spreads, volume and volatility; the published version (\emph{Journal of Financial Economics} 131(2), 2019, pp.~378--403, by its bibliographic record) was not compared, and studies of price limits and market-wide halts were not read.

\emph{A contrasting estimate}. Kasahara and Yamada estimate the effect of single-stock trading pauses on the Tokyo Stock Exchange, whose triggers are fixed yen thresholds, so that the percentage distance to a pause varies with the price level and serves as an instrument; on order-book data from 14 January 2014 to 30 March 2021 for the 500 most valuable firms (chosen on the previous year-end's capitalisation), pauses ``significantly reduce post-event volatility, narrow quoted bid--ask spreads, and facilitate price discovery'', with benefits attenuated for highly volatile stocks after negative returns and in broad downturns \citep[JPX Working Paper Vol.~52, 10 April 2026, abstract and sec.~3, PDF pp.~3--8]{jpx-wp-52-2026}. They note that their sample is mostly opening-auction pauses triggered by small moves, that they find no magnet effect, and that Hautsch and Horvath's design draws its counterfactual from the pre-regulation period while theirs identifies effects within one regime (PDF p.~4 and conclusion).

Interpretation: the two studies differ in market, pause type and identification, so their opposite volatility findings are not a contradiction to resolve here. \emph{Market-wide halts, March 2020} (read 2026-10-17). In an exchange's filing published by the SEC, the U.S. market-wide circuit breaker produced four Level~1 halts, on 9, 12, 16 and 18 March 2020, and ``in each instance, the markets halted as intended upon a 7\% drop in the S\&P 500 Index, and resumed as intended 15 minutes later''. The SRO working group's study of 31 March 2021 concluded that the halts ``appear to have had the intended effect of calming volatility in the market, without causing harm'', and that the 7, 13 and 20 percent triggers and 15-minute halts were appropriate \citep[Release 34-93365, 15 October 2021, public-inspection PDF pp.~4--6]{fr-cboebzx-mwcb-2021}.

This is the regulated venues' own evaluation reported by one of them; it is not an independent causal estimate, and the study itself was not obtained, its nyse.com link returning HTTP 404. Studies of daily price limits remain unread. An earlier version is the University of Osaka Discussion Paper 26-03 of 31 March 2026 (title and abstract compared only). Studies of daily price limits and of market-wide halts remain unread. The proposition that a halt improves price quality iff its benefit $B$ exceeds its cost $C$ is a definition of improvement, not a result; its content is the list of benefits and costs above.

\emph{Daily price limits}. Chen, Gao, He, Jiang and Xiong use account-level data from the Shenzhen Stock Exchange, where regular stocks have 10 percent daily limits and special-treatment stocks 5 percent. They find that limits ``may lead to unintended, destructive market behavior''. Large investors tend to buy on the day a stock hits its upper limit and sell the next day, and their net buying on the limit-hitting day predicts a stronger long-run reversal. Comparing stocks before and after assignment to the tighter limit gives a causal check \citep[NBER WP 24014, November 2017, abstract and sec.~1]{chen-gao-he-jiang-xiong-2017}. This is evidence on one market's limit rule. The MWCB working group's own study (nyse.com link 404) and the published Hautsch--Horvath article were again not obtained.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Price limits and margin.} Harris's sidebar: a trader with \$100{,}000 margin and \$100{,}000 other assets faces a 25-point fall worth \$250{,}000; without limits the broker immediately loses the margin and must chase \$150{,}000; with a five-point daily limit the trader, unsure whether he is bankrupt, may post margin for two days before defaulting, and owes \$50{,}000 at the end (box ``Am I Bankrupt?'', p.~574); ``Price limits can protect the clearing system by increasing the margin payments that bankrupt traders pay before they default'' (p.~582), and markets with halts ``can have lower margin requirements'' (p.~575).

\emph{Incidence of taxes and margins} (sec.~28.3.2, pp.~575--576): restricting informed traders makes prices less informative and restricting value traders, dealers and arbitrageurs raises transitory volatility, while restricting order anticipators, bluffers and gamblers lowers it; restricting utilitarian traders would be ``highly undesirable''; and it is hard to design rules that discriminate among these types, a tax falling hardest on frequent traders, dealers included.

\emph{Design}: Lehalle and Laruelle ask that breakers respond to liquidity imbalance, although ``all of them only look at prices'', perhaps by combining large moves with poor liquidity; that thresholds reflect the volatility level and its intraday seasonality; and that instruments indexed on halted assets, highly correlated instruments and other venues trading the same instrument be halted in step, which they leave as ``a research question''; they cite China's removal of badly calibrated thresholds in January 2016 (pp.~188--189). \emph{Propagation}: in the Flash Crash of 6 May 2010 the absence of a stock-specific breaker was named among aggravating factors (p.~181).

\emph{The exchanges' own review of March 2020}. The SROs' Market-Wide Circuit Breaker Working Group, with industry advisers, concluded that ``the MWCB mechanism worked as intended'' in the four halts of 9, 12, 16 and 18 March 2020, that the halts ``appear to have had the intended effect of calming volatility in the market, without causing harm'', that the S\&P~500 reference, the 7/13/20 percent triggers and the 15-minute halt are appropriate, and that nothing should stop a halt shortly after the 9:30 open; it recommended making the 2012 pilot rules permanent unchanged, an independent S\&P~500 calculation, minimal trade reporting to the SIP after a halt, and annual MWCB tests for Regulation SCI firms (Executive Summary, pp.~1--2).

Its evidence on ``no harm'' is descriptive: the share of symbols reopening on a trade rather than a quote did not differ from other days, and halt-reopening plus opening auction volume matched January 2020 opening auctions (sec.~D, PDF p.~33) \citep[Report of 31 March 2021, Executive Summary and sec.~D, pp.~1--2 and PDF p.~33]{mwcb-wg-2021}. It is the exchanges' self-assessment, with no counterfactual of the same days without halts.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Harris's examples (the DJIA 10/20/30 percent halts, NYSE Rule 80A collars) are rules of 1998--2002 that have since been replaced (\hyperref[rule:us-equity-market-structure]{U.S.\ rules}). ``Collars'' in Harris restrict access to trading systems, as Rule 80A did for index-arbitrage program trades; the wording ``restrict specific order types or program trades'' fits that case. The design principle that breakers ``should stop disorderly liquidity demand, not ordinary price discovery'' restates Harris's fundamental-versus-transitory distinction; neither source shows how to tell the two apart in real time. The incidence table assigned ``gamblers: discouraging stale-information trading can reduce noise''; Harris's point is that gamblers usually trade on stale information, so discouraging them would reduce transitory volatility (p.~576).

\qffield{Further reading} Harris, chap.~28; Lehalle and Laruelle, chap.~2, sec.~2.5.

\end{qfmechanism}

\begin{qfrule}[Spoofing and layering in securities markets: EU market-abuse law names them; U.S. securities law reaches them through its general manipulation provisions]\label{rule:spoofing-securities-markets}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{EU.} The Market Abuse Regulation, (EU) No 596/2014, defines market manipulation to include placing an order or any other behaviour that ``gives, or is likely to give, false or misleading signals as to the supply of, demand for, or price of'' a financial instrument (Art.~12(1)(a)(i)), and lists among manipulative behaviour ``the placing of orders to a trading venue, including any cancellation or modification thereof, by any available means of trading, including by electronic means, such as algorithmic and high-frequency trading strategies'', that has one of those effects by disrupting the trading system, by ``making it more difficult for other persons to identify genuine orders \dots, including by entering orders which result in the overloading or destabilisation of the order book'', or by creating a false or misleading signal (Art.~12(2)(c)) \citep[Art.~12]{eu-mar-596-2014}.

Annex~I's indicators include orders cancelled with position reversals in a short period and orders that change the representation of the best bid or offer (Annex~I, A(d) and A(f)). The Commission's delegated regulation describes the practice by name: ``Submitting multiple or large orders to trade often away from the touch on one side of the order book in order to execute a trade on the other side of the order book. Once the trade has taken place, the orders with no intention to be executed shall be removed — usually known as layering and spoofing'' \citep[Annex~II, sec.~1, point~5(e)]{eu-2016-522}.

\emph{U.S. securities.} Section~9(a)(2) of the Exchange Act makes it unlawful ``to effect, alone or with 1 or more other persons, a series of transactions'' in a security ``creating actual or apparent active trading in such security, or raising or depressing the price of such security, for the purpose of inducing the purchase or sale of such security by others'' \citep[15 U.S.C. 78i(a)(2), 2023 edition]{usc-15-78i}; Section~9(a)(1) addresses wash and matched orders. Unlike the Commodity Exchange Act's spoofing clause for futures (7 U.S.C. 6c(a)(5)(C), \hyperref[def:order-exposure-decisions]{order exposure}), these provisions do not use the word.

\qffield{Conditions} The MAR text read is the original Official Journal version through CELLAR; CELLAR lists five amending acts (2016/1011, 2016/1033, 2019/2115, 2023/2869, 2024/2809), and a text search of them found no amendment to Article~12, a limited check rather than a comparison with a consolidated text. MAR applies to instruments admitted to EU trading venues and related instruments. \emph{U.S. rules read on 2026-10-17.} Rule 10b-5 makes it unlawful ``to employ any device, scheme, or artifice to defraud'' or ``to engage in any act, practice, or course of business which operates or would operate as a fraud or deceit upon any person, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security'' \citep[17 CFR 240.10b-5(a), (c)]{ecfr-17cfr240-10b-5}. FINRA Rule 2020 forbids members to effect transactions ``by means of any manipulative, deceptive or other fraudulent device or contrivance'' \citep[Rule 2020]{finra-rules-2026}.

FINRA names the patterns, in Rule 5210, Supplementary Material~.03, ``Disruptive Quoting and Trading Activity Prohibited'', version in force since 3 April 2017 \citep[Rule 5210.03]{finra-rules-2026}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Type~1}: a party enters ``multiple limit orders on one side of the market at various price levels'', the level of supply and demand then changes, the party's orders on the opposite side are executed, and it then cancels the displayed orders;
\item \emph{Type~2}: a party narrows the spread by placing an order inside the NBBO, then executes on the opposite side against a participant ``that joined the new inside market''.
\end{itemize}
A ``frequent pattern'' of these facts constitutes the activity, whether on one venue or across several. FINRA's adopting notice states that the Supplementary Material ``does not include an express intent element'', that the order of the events does not change its applicability, and that it took effect on 15 December 2016; violations can found an expedited permanent cease-and-desist order under the Rule~9800 Series, against the trader or against a firm providing market access to such a client \citep[Executive Summary; Background \& Discussion]{finra-rn-17-22}. The effective date differs from the 3~April 2017 start of the version selected on FINRA's rule page; the notice does not explain the difference, which may reflect a later amendment. SEC and FINRA enforcement actions against layering were not read (sec.gov refuses automated retrieval), so how the provisions have been applied is not stated here.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: for a firm's surveillance (RTS~6, Art.~13, \hyperref[rule:mifid-rts6-algorithmic-controls]{RTS~6}), the EU texts give testable patterns: orders away from the touch on one side, a trade on the other, then cancellation; high order-to-trade or cancellation ratios around a trade; changes to the displayed best quotes that do not lead to execution. These are indicators, which ``shall not necessarily be deemed, in themselves, to constitute market manipulation'' (MAR, Annex~I, A).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Cancellation as such is not manipulation: market makers cancel most orders. Whether \emph{intent} must be shown differs by regime and by limb; an earlier version of this entry said that legality under MAR and Rule 10b-5 turns on intent and called FINRA's Rule 5210.03 the exception, which was wrong for MAR.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{EU administrative law (MAR).} Article~12(1)(a), the limb that covers orders, is framed by effect: an order that ``gives, or is likely to give, false or misleading signals'' or ``secures, or is likely to secure'' an abnormal or artificial price, ``unless the person \dots\ establishes that such transaction, order or behaviour have been carried out for legitimate reasons, and conform with an accepted market practice''. Article~12(1)(b) turns on use of ``a fictitious device or any other form of deception or contrivance''; the information limb (c) and the benchmark limb (d) require that the person ``knew, or ought to have known'' that the information was false or misleading \citep[Art.~12(1)]{eu-mar-596-2014}. None of these limbs states an intent requirement.

ESMA said so in its technical advice: ``even situations that were not committed with intent are under the scope of market manipulation of MAR'', and an indicator or example that seems to describe manipulative intent ``does not imply that, in the absence of the intent that conduct might not fall within the scope of the definition'' \citep[Annex~III, feedback on Q1, para.~12, printed p.~78; sec.~2.2.2, para.~8, printed p.~9]{esma-2015-224}.
\item \emph{EU criminal law.} Directive 2014/57/EU requires Member States to make manipulation a criminal offence ``at least in serious cases and when committed intentionally'' (Art.~5(1)); its recital adds that sanctions for breaches of MAR ``do not require that intent is proven or that they are qualified as serious'' \citep[Art.~5(1); recital~23]{eu-2014-57-csmad}. Intent is therefore an element of the criminal offence, not of the administrative one.
\item \emph{U.S. securities law.} Section~9(a)(2) has its own purpose element (``for the purpose of inducing the purchase or sale of such security by others'', quoted above). Rule 10b-5 requires scienter: the Supreme Court held that ``scienter is an element of a violation of \S~10(b) and Rule 10b-5, regardless of the identity of the plaintiff or the nature of the relief sought'', including SEC injunctive actions, after defining scienter as ``intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud'' \citep[Aaron v.\ SEC, 446 U.S. 680 (1980), syllabus and opinion, pp.~689--695; Ernst \& Ernst v.\ Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185 (1976), syllabus, pp.~194--214]{us-scienter-cases}. Whether recklessness suffices, a question \emph{Hochfelder} left open, was not followed further.
\item \emph{FINRA.} Rule 5210.03 has no express intent element and turns on a frequent pattern of the listed facts (above). It is a self-regulatory rule for members, alongside, not an exception to, the regimes above.
\end{itemize}
The futures-market spoofing clause (7 U.S.C. 6c(a)(5)(C)) is not covered here. Harris's 2002 legality table (\hyperref[def:bluffing-manipulation]{bluffing and manipulation}) predates both MAR and the Dodd--Frank spoofing clause.
