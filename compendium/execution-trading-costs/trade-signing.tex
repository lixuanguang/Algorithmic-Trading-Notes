\section{Signing trades}

\begin{qfmethod}[Trade-sign classification: the quote rule, the tick rule and Lee--Ready, block prices, signed volume, and what an inferred sign does and does not say]\label{meth:trade-sign-classification}
\qffield{Statement} Trade data rarely say which side initiated a trade, so the sign $q_t=+1$ (buyer-initiated) or $-1$ is inferred. If all executions occur at the posted quotes, $q_t=+1$ at the ask and $-1$ at the bid; more generally $q_t=\operatorname{Sign}(p_t-m_t)$ with $m_t$ the prevailing midpoint (the \emph{quote rule}), which leaves midpoint trades unclassified; for those, the trade takes the sign of the most recent price change (the \emph{tick rule}) \citep[chap.~9, sec.~9.7.1, p.~89]{s6-hasbrouck}. Harris describes the combination as the Lee and Ready algorithm: above the midpoint a buyer was aggressive, below it a seller; at the midpoint, an uptick or zero uptick means a buyer, otherwise a seller \citep[chap.~21, p.~423]{alpha-harris}. For block trades, a print closer to the bid than to the ask was probably seller-initiated, otherwise buyer-initiated, presuming the initiator paid a concession \citep[chap.~15, sec.~15.5, p.~332]{alpha-harris}.

Signed volume $q_tV_t$, or a concave transform such as $q_t\sqrt{V_t}$, enters impact models; Hasbrouck advises against convex transforms such as $q_tV_t^2$, which amplify extreme volumes (sec.~9.7.2, p.~89).

\qffield{Conditions} Methods timeless; the block statistic as reported in 2002; quote-lag evidence from the NYSE and AMEX in 1987--1988; accuracy evidence from NASDAQ in 2011. Signing needs the quotes and trades in the right order: in limit-order markets the interleaving is usually accurate, but with hybrid markets, different dissemination paths, coarse time stamps or manual steps it is ``likely to be suspect'' (Hasbrouck, p.~89). The rule at the posted quotes works only where the book can be rebuilt and executions are against visible orders, not hidden or reserve ones (p.~89).

\emph{Lee and Ready's evidence} (read 2026-10-16 from a scan; cited pages checked visually). On 150 NYSE firms in 1988, for nonopening trades where the last quote revision was more than 5 seconds old (90.9 percent of trades), the tick test called 92.1 percent of trades at the ask buys and 90.2 percent at the bid sells, and outperformed the reverse tick test \citep[sec.~I, Table~I, pp.~736--737]{lee-ready-1991}: agreement with the quote-based classification, not accuracy against known directions.

For isolated trades (the first after 11 a.m.\ each day with no other trade in a 2-minute window), 59.3 percent of NYSE and 72.4 percent of AMEX quote revisions around the trade were recorded \emph{ahead} of it, with a sharp drop between 5 and 6 seconds before, hence the rule to compare a trade with the quote at least 5 seconds old; this cut the share of trades apparently inside the spread from 35.7 to 30.1 percent (sec.~II, pp.~737--739, Fig.~2). The 5-second delay fits their 1988 data; ``A different delay may be appropriate for other time periods'', and in September and October 1987 most pretrade quotes came within 2 seconds of the trade (n.~10, p.~738).

The lag is a property of the reporting process in a given market and year, not a constant. \emph{Accuracy against known directions} (read 2026-10-16). Jurkatis evaluates the rules on NASDAQ's own book, May to July 2011, over 134 million transactions with nanosecond time stamps, where the direction of the standing order, and so the initiator, is known; he coarsens the time stamps to mimic slower data \citep[secs.~1 and~4, pp.~3 and~12]{jurkatis-2020}. With nanosecond stamps all algorithms classify about 98 percent of volume correctly; at one-second stamps the Lee--Ready, EMO and CLNV rules fall to about 90 percent (EMO the best) and his full-information algorithm, which matches each trade to the quote change it leaves in the book, keeps 95; in seconds with many trades the traditional rules drop to 85 percent or lower; and interpolating time stamps within the second, as Holden and Jacobsen propose, \emph{lowers} the traditional rules' accuracy against using the last quote before the recorded time (pp.~3 and 12--14).

His review reports Lee--Ready accuracy of about 85 percent on NASDAQ ITCH and DTAQ data in 2011 (Chakrabarty et al.\ 2015), 79 percent on Euronext second-stamped data in 2007--2008 and 46 percent on LSE microsecond data in 2017 (Panayides et al.\ 2019), against 75--93 percent in older studies, and lag recommendations from zero to two seconds, most based on indirect evidence (pp.~1--2 and n.~4); those studies were not read. The EMO rule classifies at the ask or bid and uses the tick test for everything else; CLNV calls trades within 30 percent of the spread of the ask (bid) buys (sells) and uses the tick test in the middle 40 percent (sec.~2.1, pp.~4--5): the three disagree on trades inside the spread (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/execution-market-making/market-estimators-rules-checks.py}, section~5). The evidence is for one exchange's book; consolidated-tape data, with more venues and reporting paths, may differ.

\qffield{Practice} Checked (\nolinkurl{order-flow-checks.py}, ten assertions on prices held as exact decimals): midpoint trades after an uptick, or after a zero tick whose last non-zero change was up, are buys, and after a downtick or a zero downtick sells, for whole-tick midpoints (10.01 in 10.00--10.02) and half-tick ones (10.005 in 10.00--10.01); a midpoint trade with no earlier price change is left unclassified; trades just above or below a half-tick midpoint (10.006, 10.004) follow the quote rule whatever the tick; a trade at the bid is a sell, and at the ask a buy, whatever the previous tick. An implementation must compare prices exactly: in binary floating point $(10.00+10.01)/2$ is 10.004999\dots, below 10.005, so the half-tick midpoint trade after a downtick is wrongly called a buy.

Sign persistence in event time is a stylised fact (\hyperref[ev:high-frequency-stylised-facts]{stylised facts}); Hasbrouck notes that signed size often adds little explanatory power beyond the sign itself (sec.~9.7.2, p.~90). Summed over an interval, signed volume is the trade imbalance of \hyperref[def:order-flow-imbalance]{order-flow imbalance}, not the event-based OFI.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The sign identifies which side demanded immediacy at that moment, not who was better informed or more eager: a buyer who pegs the bid can accumulate aggressively while the algorithm calls every trade passive (Harris's sidebar, p.~423). Measured as transaction costs, the algorithm overstates the costs of traders who do not only take liquidity, and cannot see that several trades filled one order, so it understates that order's impact cost (p.~423). Harris reports that ``analysts have determined that sellers initiate approximately 80 percent of all large block trades in the U.S.\ stock markets'', and reads the asymmetry as consistent with three of his four block-trading problems: latent demand (buyers can buy only from holders when short selling is hard), price discrimination (sellers can credibly reveal their full size) and asymmetric information (large buyers are presumed informed) (pp.~332--333); he gives no sample or period.

His classification rule is the block price against the prevailing quotes: closer to the bid means seller-initiated (sec.~15.5, p.~332). \emph{Source search}: no citation stands at the statement. The chapter's bibliography lists the candidate studies: Kraus and Stoll (1972), Holthausen, Leftwich and Mayers (1987, 1990), Chan and Lakonishok (1993, 1995), Madhavan and Cheng (1997) and Saar (2001) (Bibliography, ``Chapter~15: Block Traders'', pp.~607--608). All are in subscription journals. Saar's open working-paper version (October 1999) is a theory of the price-impact asymmetry and reports no buyer--seller split \citep[abstract and secs.~1--2]{saar-1999-wp}. Outside the United States the split need not be lopsided: Alzahrani et al.\ count 2.3 million block buys and 1.9 million block sales on the Saudi market in 2005--2008 \citep[abstract]{alzahrani-2010-wp}. That is another market and definition, not a test of Harris's figure, which remains unsourced. A proposed taxonomy lists the tick rule without its use at the midpoint, and its ``false persistence'' from misclassification and order splitting was not found in these passages.

All rules have ``only a very limited success in classifying trades executed inside the quotes'', which biases accuracy for large trades, high-volume periods and ECN trades.
