\section{Transaction costs and transaction cost analysis}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Transaction costs: explicit, implicit and missed-trade costs, and an accounting split of shortfall]\label{def:transaction-costs}
\qffield{Statement} Transaction costs include all costs associated with trading \citep[sec.~21.1, pp.~421--422]{alpha-harris}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{explicit costs}, which a cost accountant would identify: commissions, exchange fees, taxes, and the resources devoted to trading, such as running a buy-side desk;
\item \emph{implicit costs}, which arise because traders affect prices: the bid--ask spread paid by buying at the ask and selling at the bid, and the price impact of large orders;
\item \emph{missed trade opportunity costs}, when orders fail to fill, or fill late. Harris's example: a cotton buyer who bid 64.95 cents when the price was 65 and saw it rise to 68 lost 2.75 cents a pound against an aggressive fill at 65.25, \$137{,}500 on 100 contracts.
\end{itemize}
Explicit costs are easy to measure. Implicit and missed-trade costs need a benchmark: what prices would have been without the trade, or what the unfilled order would have cost to complete (p.~422). Kissell lists ten components, commissions, fees, taxes, rebates, spreads, delay cost, price appreciation, market impact, timing risk and opportunity cost, and classifies them as investment-related, trading-related and opportunity costs, fixed or variable, visible or hidden \citep[chap.~3, pp.~89--95, Tables~3.1--3.2]{s7-kissellscience}.

Narang names three major components for a quant's cost model \citep[chap.~5, ``Transaction Cost Models'', sec.~5.1, pp.~86--91]{s7-narang}: \emph{commissions and fees}, paid to brokerages, exchanges and regulators, small per trade for quants who use only a bank's infrastructure, with brokers' clearing and settlement fees usually inside the commission (pp.~86--87); \emph{slippage}, ``the change in the price between the time a trader (or quant system) decides to transact and the time when the order is actually at the exchange for execution'' (p.~87); and \emph{market impact}, how much an order moves the market by its demand for liquidity, ``normally defined as the difference between the price at the time a market order enters the exchange and the price at which the trade is actually executed'' (pp.~88--89).

His terms map onto the ones above as set out under Pitfalls.

\qffield{Notation} Interpretation: with the signed quantities of \hyperref[def:implementation-shortfall]{implementation shortfall} ($S$ intended, $s_j$ filled at $p_j$, decision price $P_d$, arrival midpoint $P_0$, end price $P_n$) and $m_j$ the quotation midpoint just before fill $j$, shortfall splits additively as
\[ \mathrm{IS}=\underbrace{S(P_0-P_d)}_{\text{delay}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_js_j(p_j-m_j)}_{\text{half-spread paid}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_js_j(m_j-P_0)}_{\text{midpoint drift}}+\underbrace{\bigl(S-\textstyle\sum_js_j\bigr)(P_n-P_0)}_{\text{opportunity}}+\text{fees}. \]
The second term is the filled size times Harris's liquidity premium, half the effective spread at each fill (\hyperref[def:spread-measures]{spread measures}); the middle two terms are Wagner's trading-related part. A buy of 10{,}000 shares (decision 50.00, arrival 50.10, fills of 3{,}000 at 50.20 and 50.30 against pre-fill midpoints 50.15 and 50.25, rest cancelled, $P_n=50.60$, fees 60) gives $1{,}000+300+600+2{,}000+60=3{,}960$, the paper-minus-real total; the mirror-image sell gives the same.

\qffield{Conditions} Timeless (accounting); Narang's account as of 2024. The split is an accounting identity, not a causal attribution. The midpoint drift mixes the order's own impact with market movement and other traders' impact. Kissell defines market impact as the difference between the price path with the order and without it, which cannot be observed together, so impact cannot be measured exactly \citep[chap.~3, pp.~91--92]{s7-kissellscience}; Harris likewise needs a no-trade benchmark (p.~422). The half-spread term measures each fill against its own contemporaneous midpoint, so it misses the cumulative impact of earlier fills, which falls into the drift (Harris's split-order example, pp.~428--429). Kissell's timing risk is the uncertainty of cost, not a realised component (p.~92), and his price appreciation is the price trend that would have occurred anyway (p.~91).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: an index or market adjustment (\hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}) removes the part of the drift predicted by the market's move; what remains is still a residual, which includes impact, stock-specific news and model error, and is impact only under the model used to remove the rest. Of a split into spread, impact, timing and fees, spread, delay (timing before arrival), opportunity and fees are measurable as above, but ``impact'' is available only as a model estimate or a market-adjusted residual, not as an observed component.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Count each cost once: the spread is inside fill prices, so adding a quoted or effective spread to an arrival-price shortfall counts it twice; fees enter once. Adverse selection and delay are not implicit costs relative to a benchmark: in Harris adverse selection is a cause of the spread, and in Kissell delay is an investment-related cost before the order reaches the market. Borrow charges are not among the source's explicit costs. Kissell describes the spread as the round-trip cost for small orders, not blocks (p.~90).

\emph{Narang's terms.} Interpretation of the mapping, with his claims kept as his:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Slippage} runs from the decision to the order's arrival at the exchange, so it is the delay term $S(P_0-P_d)$ above, signed by side: his sale decided at \$100.00 that reaches the market at \$99.90 costs \$0.10 a share, and a move the other way is a gain (pp.~87--88). The word is not used uniformly (interpretation); name the benchmark and the interval.
\item \emph{Market impact} in his definition runs from arrival to fill. It is therefore the trading-related part of shortfall (half-spread paid plus midpoint drift), not impact in the causal sense of \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}. His own example shows the difference: a sale decided at \$100.00 reaches the market at \$100.05 and fills at \$100.20 in a rising market, giving slippage of $-\$0.05$ and ``impact'' of $-\$0.15$. That is a negative arrival-to-fill cost against a benchmark; it does not identify what the order itself did to the price, and he concedes that impact may be impossible to separate from slippage or from the stock's own move (pp.~89--90).
\item \emph{Known after execution?} Narang writes that one does not know how much a trade impacts the market ``until the trade has already been completed'' (p.~89). After completion the fills and the realised cost against a benchmark are known; the impact is not, because the price that would have prevailed without the order is never observed (Kissell, above; \hyperref[def:price-impact]{price impact}). It is estimated by a model or across many orders.
\item \emph{Conditional claims.} That trend-following strategies suffer most from slippage and mean-reverting ones least, sometimes gaining (p.~88), is a statement about the expected sign of the price move over the delay relative to the trade's side, and holds only to the extent that prices continue in the direction a strategy trades or revert against it over that interval; he gives no data.

Lower latency reduces slippage by shortening the delay (p.~88), and higher volatility raises it (pp.~88 and~91); interpretation: with no expected drift over the delay, a longer delay or a higher volatility widens the spread of outcomes rather than raising their mean, and raises the expected cost only together with an adverse drift, such as an accurate short-term forecast that the market starts to price, which is his own condition (p.~88). His GOOG figure, an average daily range 16 times the average day-to-day move, is undated and unsourced.
\item \emph{Fees.} The pairing of brokerages with access, exchanges with security and regulators with infrastructure reads his list ``respectively''; the text does not pair them, and on p.~87 credits exchanges with access to liquidity and with guaranteeing that both counterparties perform. Interpretation: in current market structure that guarantee is given by a central counterparty, which ``interposes itself between counterparties'' and ensures the performance of open contracts \citep[para.~1.13, p.~9]{exp-pfmi}, whether or not the exchange operates it. His fixed charges as a floor ``below which the expense of trading cannot go'' (p.~91) ignore the liquidity rebates he describes on p.~90 (about 0.2 cents a share to a provider against 0.3 cents charged to a taker, the venue keeping about 0.1).
\item \emph{Clearing and settlement.} Narang's clearing, ``regulatory reporting and monitoring, tax handling, and handling failure'' before settlement, and his settlement, ``the delivery of securities in exchange for payment in full'' (pp.~86--87), are his descriptions of what brokers charge for, not standard definitions. The CPSS--IOSCO principles describe securities settlement systems that transfer securities ``either free of payment or against payment'', with delivery versus payment when ``delivery of the security occurs if and only if payment occurs'', and that may add clearing functions ``such as the confirmation of trade and settlement instructions'' \citep[para.~1.12, p.~8]{exp-pfmi}, and central counterparties that net trades multilaterally and take margin \citep[para.~1.13, p.~9]{exp-pfmi}. Standard definitions are those of the principles' glossary \citep[Annex~H, pp.~174--178]{exp-pfmi}.

\emph{Clearing} is ``the process of transmitting, reconciling, and, in some cases, confirming transactions prior to settlement, potentially including the netting of transactions and the establishment of final positions for settlement''; the term is ``sometimes \dots\ also used (imprecisely) to cover settlement'', and for futures and options also means ``the daily balancing of profits and losses and the daily calculation of collateral requirements'' (p.~175).

\emph{Final settlement} is ``the irrevocable and unconditional transfer of an asset or financial instrument, or the discharge of an obligation by the FMI or its participants in accordance with the terms of the underlying contract'', ``a legally defined moment'' (p.~176). \emph{Delivery versus payment} is ``a securities settlement mechanism that links a securities transfer and a funds transfer in such a way as to ensure that delivery occurs if and only if the corresponding payment occurs'' (p.~176); without it the seller bears \emph{principal risk}, delivering irrevocably ``but not receive payment'' (p.~178).

A \emph{central counterparty} ``interposes itself between counterparties \dots, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer and thereby ensuring the performance of open contracts'' (p.~174), by \emph{novation}, which replaces the original contract with two new ones, or by \emph{open offer} at the time of the trade (p.~177). So Narang's ``clearing'' bundles post-trade services (reporting, tax handling, fails) that the standard term does not include, and his settlement is final settlement under DvP; who guarantees performance is the CCP, not the exchange as such or the broker (Interpretation). Harris's description of the same post-trade chain is in \hyperref[def:market-structures-venues]{market structures and venues}.
\end{itemize}

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Transaction cost analysis: measuring costs after the trade, predicting them before, and comparing brokers and algorithms]\label{meth:transaction-cost-analysis}
\qffield{Statement} Traders measure transaction costs to evaluate how well they and their brokers implemented their strategies, whether they traded too aggressively or not enough, and which brokers should get future orders; they predict costs to judge whether strategies are worth implementing. Exchanges, brokers (notably to show best execution when paid for order flow), sponsors (ERISA commission duties) and regulators measure them too \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', pp.~420--421]{alpha-harris}.

Lehalle and Laruelle separate post-trade analysis, usually of one day, which explains what the market context did, from TCA over months of trades, which uses averaging to cancel most market effects and find what part of performance comes from the algorithm's design; its outcome is to match an algorithm or provider to a market context or trading style, so that flow is routed accordingly, using breakdowns by volatility, jumps, liquidity and news, and comparing only stocks of similar liquidity (bid--ask spread, tick size in basis points, quantity on the book, turnover, trade size, free float, trading rate) \citep[sec.~3.1.3, pp.~202--203]{alpha-laruelle}.

\qffield{Notation} Kissell's measures \citep[chap.~3, pp.~110--113]{s7-kissellscience}, over the trading horizon, are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{index cost} &= \frac{\text{index VWAP}-\text{index arrival}}{\text{index arrival}}\times10^4,\\
\text{index-adjusted cost} &= \text{arrival cost}-\hat\beta\,\text{index cost},\\
Z &= \frac{\text{pre-trade estimate}-\text{arrival cost}}{\text{pre-trade timing risk}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
These are eqs.~(3.21)--(3.23); $Z>0$ means the trade beat its estimate. A cost \emph{measure} is ex post and single-valued; a cost \emph{forecast} is ex ante, a mean with a standard deviation, the timing risk (pp.~95--96).

\qffield{Conditions} Practitioner methods as of 2002, 2014 and 2018. Prediction: explicit information (contracted fees and displayed liquidity, an upper bound on impact for orders smaller than displayed size) and implicit information from past costs, modelled by regression on order variables (size, price placement), contemporaneous market variables (spread, displayed size, recent volume, recent returns, money flow) and general ones (average volume, volatility, market capitalisation); past costs predict future ones only if liquidity conditions are unchanged \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', sec.~21.7, pp.~438--439]{alpha-harris}. Comparison: estimates from single trades are noisy, averaging removes noise but not bias or gaming, and comparing brokers is meaningful only for similar trading problems (p.~432).

Kissell's $Z$ should be near mean zero and variance one if pre-trade estimates are accurate; the test should be run by order size and strategy, on shares actually traded rather than the full order, on enough orders, and with the investor's own pre-trade model rather than the broker's (pp.~112--113).

\qffield{Practice} Kissell separates cost from performance: comparing the execution price with the day's VWAP is a performance proxy, with the close a tracking-error proxy, while comparison with the open or the arrival price is a cost (p.~96). Harris advises that strategists and traders share why they trade, and that incentives reward joint performance rather than blame (pp.~439--440).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Interpretation: the population, aggregation weights and benchmark must be stated: averaging by order and by value answer different questions, and both depend on which orders reached each broker. Lehalle and Laruelle's averaging cancels market effects only if they are unrelated to which algorithm was chosen; if traders send urgent or difficult orders to one provider, its average cost is worse whatever its quality, so a routing table built on unadjusted averages confounds selection with skill, and outperformance of a benchmark does not show causal superiority. The failure modes (wrong exposure, wrong clock, own-impact contamination, gaming, selection) are consistent with Harris's biases (sec.~21.4) and Kissell's conditions but are a synthesis, not a list in either.

Kissell's index cost is ``not adjusted for the side of the trade'' (p.~112); with a side-signed arrival cost this is wrong for sells: a sale at 50~basis points below arrival while the index fell 50~basis points and $\hat\beta=1$ shows 100~basis points of adjusted cost instead of 0, so the index term needs the side factor (Interpretation). His approximation ``Index VWAP $=\tfrac12R_m$'' is an approximation to the index cost, not the index VWAP. Venue variables (hidden fill rates, fee tiers, queue loss, routing latency), limit price and urgency are not among the source's prediction variables.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:transaction-costs]{transaction costs}; \hyperref[def:execution-benchmark]{execution benchmarks}; \hyperref[meth:execution-decision-support]{pre-trade, monitoring and post-trade tools}; \hyperref[meth:aggressiveness-tradeoff]{aggressiveness trade-off}; \hyperref[def:best-execution]{best execution}; the market adjustment parallels \hyperref[meth:relative-performance-evaluation]{relative performance evaluation} of managers.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Trading aggressiveness: balancing the transaction cost of trading harder against the missed-trade cost of trading softer]\label{meth:aggressiveness-tradeoff}
\qffield{Statement} If the additional transaction costs of trading more aggressively are less than the missed trade opportunity costs it would save, traders earn more by trading more aggressively; if the transaction costs saved by trading less aggressively exceed the additional opportunity costs, they earn more by trading less aggressively. Most traders do not estimate these marginal costs but assume they equal average costs, trading harder or softer as their per-unit opportunity costs exceed or fall short of their per-unit transaction costs; Harris advises estimating marginal transaction costs from the costs of the last trades that fill orders \citep[chap.~``Liquidity and Transaction Cost Measurement'', sec.~21.6, pp.~437--438]{alpha-harris}.

\qffield{Conditions} Interpretation: the objective is expected total cost, transaction cost plus missed-trade cost, of an order whose size is given; the choice variable is aggressiveness (limit placement, urgency, participation); the rule is the first-order condition of that minimisation and assumes both costs change smoothly with aggressiveness and that the missed-trade cost is measured realistically. Harris states it as advice, with no model or proof. Opportunity costs count only for orders that could have been filled, and depend on the horizon at which they are measured (pp.~435--436).

\qffield{Practice} Well-informed traders, especially on short-term information, should accept substantial transaction costs rather than fail to trade, and strategists should give traders price limits that reflect the value of their information (p.~439). Interpretation: a mean--variance schedule (\hyperref[mod:almgren-chriss-execution]{Almgren--Chriss}) is one formal version of the same trade-off, with price risk in place of missed-trade cost.

\qffield{Pitfalls} For the last slice the control variable is marginal cost: Harris's point is that marginal, not average, costs should drive the decision, and that the last fills estimate them. Interpretation: comparing average costs gives the right direction only if marginal and average costs move together.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:transaction-costs]{transaction costs}; \hyperref[def:implementation-shortfall]{implementation shortfall} (the opportunity term); \hyperref[mod:almgren-chriss-execution]{mean--variance execution}; \hyperref[def:order-exposure-decisions]{order exposure decisions}.

\end{qfmethod}
