\subsection{Balance-sheet analysis}

These entries explain how intangible assets and goodwill, financial instruments and non-current liabilities are recognised, measured and disclosed, and how a common-size balance sheet and its ratios are calculated and read.

\begin{qfmethod}[Intangible assets and goodwill on the balance sheet: recognition, measurement after recognition, impairment testing and the disclosures an analyst uses]\label{meth:intangibles-goodwill-reporting}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Intangible assets.} An intangible asset is recognised only if future economic benefits are probable and its cost is reliably measurable \citep[IAS~38 para.~21]{eu-2023-1803-ias38}. Internally generated goodwill, brands, mastheads, publishing titles and customer lists are never recognised, because their cost cannot be separated from developing the business (paras.~48 and~63--64). After recognition an entity chooses cost or, only where an active market exists, revaluation (paras.~72 and~75). A finite-lived asset is amortised from when it is available for use, with its life and method reviewed each year as estimates (paras.~97 and~104). An indefinite-lived asset, with no foreseeable limit to its cash flows, is not amortised but tested for impairment annually and whenever there is an indication (paras.~88 and~107--108). Disclosures distinguish internally generated from other intangibles by class: lives or rates, methods, gross amounts, accumulated amortisation, a reconciliation of movements, and the research and development expensed (paras.~118 and~126).

\emph{Goodwill} is recognised only in a business combination. It is the excess of the consideration (plus any non-controlling interest and previously held interest) over the acquisition-date fair value of identifiable net assets \citep[IFRS~3 para.~32]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs3}. Identifiable intangibles, those that are separable or arise from contractual or legal rights, are recognised apart from goodwill (para.~B31). Goodwill is allocated to cash-generating units no larger than an operating segment and tested at least annually \citep[IAS~36 paras.~10 and~80]{eu-2023-1803-ias36}. An impairment loss reduces goodwill first, then other assets pro rata (para.~104), and is never reversed (para.~124). For units with significant goodwill, entities disclose the basis of recoverable amount and the key assumptions (para.~134).

\emph{US GAAP} does not amortise goodwill or indefinite-lived intangibles \citep[paras.~16--18]{us-goodwill-intangibles-2001}. Since 2017 the goodwill impairment charge has been the excess of a reporting unit's carrying amount over its fair value, capped at the goodwill allocated to it, rather than an implied-fair-value calculation \citep[pp.~2--3]{us-goodwill-2017}. SEC filers apply this to tests in fiscal years beginning after 15~December 2019.

\qffield{Notation} Goodwill $=$ consideration $+$ NCI $+$ prior interest $-$ fair value of identifiable net assets. The net assets include deferred tax on fair-value uplifts, which itself increases goodwill.

\qffield{Conditions} IFRS as adopted in the EU in 2023. US Statements are cited as issued; later Codification amendments, such as private-company alternatives, were not reviewed.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.} An acquirer pays 500 for a business with book net assets of 250. Fair-value uplifts are:
\begin{itemize}
\item PP\&E 30, depreciated over six years;
\item customer relationships 90, amortised over ten years;
\item a brand 60 with indefinite life;
\item in-process research and development 40.
\end{itemize}
Deferred tax at 25~percent on the uplifts is 55, so identifiable net assets are 415 and goodwill is 85. Post-acquisition depreciation and amortisation are 14 a year; the brand, the in-process project (until completion) and goodwill are tested for impairment instead.

\emph{Analysis.} Harbour's goodwill of 120 is 9.4~percent of 2025 total assets (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{case}). The analyst reads the impairment-test assumptions, such as the discount rate, growth and headroom, against the forecast. Lack of amortisation means goodwill reflects past purchase prices, not current value. Many analysts therefore also examine returns on tangible capital.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating unamortised goodwill as evidence of value. Comparing an acquisitive company with one that grew organically, whose equivalent intangibles were expensed. Ignoring the sensitivity disclosures that show how close a unit is to impairment.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:intangible-acquisition-routes]{purchased, internal and acquired intangibles}; \hyperref[meth:impairment-derecognition]{impairment}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Financial instruments and non-current liabilities: classification and measurement of financial assets and liabilities, expected credit losses, bonds at amortised cost, current versus non-current classification with covenants, and disclosures]\label{meth:financial-instruments-liabilities}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Financial assets} under IFRS are classified by business model and contractual cash-flow characteristics \citep[IFRS~9 para.~4.1.1]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs9}:
\begin{itemize}
\item amortised cost for assets held to collect cash flows that are solely principal and interest (para.~4.1.2);
\item fair value through OCI for such assets held to collect and sell (para.~4.1.2A);
\item fair value through profit or loss otherwise (para.~4.1.4).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Equity investments at fair value through OCI.} Equity investments fail the cash-flow test and are measured at fair value through profit or loss, but an entity may elect to present later fair-value changes in OCI (para.~4.1.4). The election is irrevocable, made at initial recognition and instrument by instrument. It is unavailable for an equity investment held for trading or for contingent consideration recognised by an acquirer in a business combination (paras.~5.7.5 and~B5.7.1). Held for trading means acquired principally to sell in the near term, part of a portfolio with a recent pattern of short-term profit-taking, or a derivative (Appendix~A). Amounts in OCI are never transferred to profit or loss, even on sale, although the cumulative gain may be moved within equity; dividends go to profit or loss unless they clearly recover part of the cost (paras.~5.7.6 and~B5.7.1).

\emph{Credit losses and US GAAP.} Expected credit losses are provided on a 12-month basis, or over the lifetime once credit risk has increased significantly (paras.~5.5.3 and~5.5.5). US GAAP measures most equity investments at fair value through net income \citep[p.~1]{us-equity-investments-2016}. Assets at amortised cost are presented net of lifetime expected credit losses based on reasonable and supportable forecasts \citep[p.~2]{us-credit-losses-2016}.

\emph{Financial liabilities} are mostly measured at amortised cost (IFRS~9 para.~4.2.1). Initial measurement is fair value less directly attributable transaction costs (para.~5.1.1), and interest follows the effective-interest method. For liabilities designated at fair value, changes due to own credit risk go to OCI (para.~5.7.7). US GAAP presents debt issuance costs as a deduction from the debt, consistent with IFRS \citep[p.~1]{us-debt-issuance-costs-2015}.

\emph{Current or non-current.} A liability is current if it is settled in the operating cycle, held for trading, due within 12 months, or if the entity lacks at the reporting date the right to defer settlement for at least 12 months \citep[Annex, IAS~1 para.~69]{eu-2023-2822-ias1}. Covenants tested on or before the reporting date affect that right even if compliance is assessed later. Covenants tested only afterwards do not, but must be disclosed with the risk of breach (paras.~72B and~76ZA). A breach before year-end makes a loan current even if the lender grants a waiver after year-end (para.~74). A right to roll over a facility makes a drawing non-current (para.~73). These amendments apply in the EU from financial years beginning on or after 1~January 2024 (Art.~2).

\emph{Risk disclosures.} Entities disclose the nature and extent of credit, liquidity and market risks. These include contractual maturity analyses of liabilities and sensitivity analyses of profit and equity to reasonably possible changes in market variables \citep[IFRS~7 paras.~31--34, 39--40]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs7}.

\qffield{Notation} Effective interest rate $r$ solves $\text{carrying amount}_0=\sum_t \text{CF}_t/(1+r)^t$. Interest expense is $r\times$ opening carrying amount. The discount or issuance cost accretes into the carrying amount.

\qffield{Conditions} IFRS~9 and IFRS~7 as adopted in 2023; later amendments, such as those to classification of certain instruments, were not reviewed. The US Updates are cited for their main provisions; CECL's effective dates were later deferred for some entities, a change not reviewed here.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original} (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{Harbour}, 2025).
\begin{itemize}
\item Debt of 355 is the term loan 250, the RCF 40 and lease liabilities 65.
\item The current portion of debt is 25 of the term loan and 24.75 of lease principal; the RCF is non-current because Harbour has the right to roll it over.
\item Had Harbour breached a net-debt covenant tested at 31~December, the whole term loan would be current despite a January waiver. The current ratio would then fall from 1.84 to 0.94, since current liabilities rise by 225 to 458.75.
\item The maturity analysis and the covenant disclosures, not the current/non-current split alone, show refinancing risk.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Equity election} (original). On 1~February 2026 a company buys two holdings of 10 each: a 4~percent stake in a supplier, held for the relationship, and shares bought to sell within weeks on an expected price move. By year-end each is worth 12 and pays a dividend of 0.3.
\begin{itemize}
\item The supplier stake is eligible. If the company elects on 1~February, the gain of 2 goes to OCI and stays there even when the stake is later sold; only the dividend reaches profit.
\item The trading holding is held for trading, so the election is unavailable: the gain of 2 and the dividend both go to profit or loss.
\item An election made only at year-end, after the price rose, is not available for either holding, and an earnout payable to the sellers of an acquired business could never be elected.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Reading the current ratio without checking covenant-driven reclassifications. Assuming any equity investment can be moved to OCI, or that the election can be made after prices move. Netting issuance costs differently across companies when comparing leverage. Treating fair-value gains from a company's own deteriorating credit as income.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:balance-sheet-ratios]{balance-sheet ratios}; \hyperref[def:long-term-liability-compensation-disclosures]{long-term liability disclosures}; \hyperref[meth:cash-flow-balance-sheet-quality]{balance-sheet quality}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Common-size balance sheets and balance-sheet ratios: structure, liquidity and solvency measures, and their interpretation]\label{meth:balance-sheet-ratios}
\qffield{Statement} A common-size balance sheet expresses each item as a percentage of total assets. It shows asset intensity, working-capital structure and financing mix, and allows comparison across size and time \citep[pp.~28--33]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}. Balance-sheet ratios fall into two groups:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{liquidity}: current ratio (current assets over current liabilities), quick ratio (cash and receivables over current liabilities) and cash ratio;
\item \emph{solvency}: debt to equity, debt to capital and debt to assets.
\end{itemize}
Ratios are starting points, not conclusions. Accounting methods affect them: LIFO understates inventories and so the current ratio, and unrecognised obligations understate leverage (pp.~34--35).

\qffield{Notation} Current ratio CA$/$CL; quick ratio (cash $+$ receivables)$/$CL; debt$/$equity; net debt $=$ debt $-$ cash.

\qffield{Conditions} Point-in-time measures, sensitive to year-end timing and window dressing. Under IFRS~16, lease liabilities are debt; under US operating-lease accounting the liability is on the balance sheet, but the expense is not split into interest and depreciation (\hyperref[meth:lease-reporting]{leases}).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original} (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{Harbour}).

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{\qquad}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Percent of total assets & 2025 & 2024 & Ratio & 2025 & 2024\\
\midrule
Cash & 2.94 & 2.97 & Current ratio & 1.84 & 1.87\\
Receivables & 14.11 & 13.80 & Quick ratio & 0.93 & 0.94\\
Inventories & 16.62 & 16.73 & Cash ratio & 0.16 & 0.17\\
Net PP\&E & 47.04 & 47.26 & Debt to equity & 0.55 & 0.55\\
Right-of-use assets & 5.17 & 5.02 & Debt to capital & 0.35 & 0.36\\
Development costs & 4.70 & 4.18 & Equity to assets & 50.75 & 50.61\\
Goodwill & 9.41 & 10.04 & & &\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{Interpretation.} Harbour is asset-intensive, with nearly half its assets in plant. Liquidity is adequate but depends on inventory: the quick ratio is below one. Leverage was stable in ratio terms, but debt rose by 20 in 2025 through the year-end RCF drawing. Equity also rose, so the flat debt-to-equity ratio hides higher absolute borrowing. Adding the after-tax pension deficit of 36.12 to debt raises debt to equity to 0.60 (\hyperref[meth:post-employment-benefit-modelling]{pensions}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating a high current ratio as strength when it reflects slow-moving inventory. Ignoring debt-like items, such as pension deficits, provisions and supplier finance. Comparing year-end balance sheets of companies with different seasonal year-ends.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:financial-ratio-families]{ratio families}; \hyperref[meth:financial-instruments-liabilities]{liability classification}.

\end{qfmethod}
