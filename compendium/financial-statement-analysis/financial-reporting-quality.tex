\section{Financial reporting quality}

These entries separate the quality of reporting from the quality of the results reported, set out why low-quality reports arise and what disciplines them, describe the methods used to manage earnings, cash flows and balances, and apply a sequence of tests to Harbour's statements and to an aggressive variant of them.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Reporting quality against results quality, and a spectrum from decision-useful reports to fiction]\label{def:reporting-results-quality}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Reporting quality} is whether the reports faithfully represent the company's transactions and position. Financial statements must present fairly the effects of transactions under the standards \citep[IAS~1 para.~15]{eu-2023-1803-ias1}. Statements containing material errors, or immaterial errors made intentionally to achieve a particular presentation, do not comply \citep[IAS~8 para.~41]{eu-2023-1803-ias8}. \emph{Results quality} is about the economics reported: whether earnings are adequate and sustainable, cash flows are backed by operations, and assets and liabilities are realistically measured.

The textbook gives two views of earnings quality. One is the degree of conservatism; the other, which this chapter uses, is the absence of distortion between reported numbers and the business \citep[p.~112]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}. High-quality reporting can reveal low-quality results, and polished results can hide poor reporting.

\emph{A spectrum} (Interpretation, ordering the categories in the sources above, from best to worst):
\begin{enumerate}
\item compliant, decision-useful reporting of sustainable results that earn an adequate return;
\item compliant, decision-useful reporting of results that are low or unsustainable;
\item compliant reporting with biased but permitted choices and estimates (aggressive or conservative);
\item compliant but managed reporting: cosmetic earnings management through accruals, or real management through business decisions (pp.~108--111);
\item departures from the standards, misstatement;
\item fictitious transactions, fraud, such as recording fictitious sales \citep[p.~389]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}.
\end{enumerate}

\qffield{Notation} Not applicable.

\qffield{Conditions} The categories overlap; the spectrum orders the analyst's suspicion, not legal categories. Quality applies to earnings, cash flows and balance-sheet items separately (\hyperref[meth:cash-flow-balance-sheet-quality]{cash-flow and balance-sheet quality}).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Harbour's 2025 statements, as constructed, sit at level~2: compliant and informative, but with operating margin below 2024 and partly dependent on a restructuring that must deliver savings.
\item The \hyperref[meth:report-quality-evaluation]{aggressive variant} moves to level~4 or worse. It records a bill-and-hold sale before control passes, capitalises administrative costs as development, and uses supplier finance to lift operating cash flow. The revenue item may also breach IFRS~15, which would be level~5.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Equating conservatism with quality; excess conservatism today raises later profits. Treating a clean audit opinion as proof of level~1.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:reporting-incentives-discipline]{incentives and discipline}; \hyperref[meth:earnings-management-presentation]{methods}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Motivations and conditions for low-quality reporting, and the mechanisms that discipline reporting quality with their limitations]\label{def:reporting-incentives-discipline}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Motivations} \citep[pp.~109--110]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}:
\begin{itemize}
\item contracting: bonus plans with lower and upper bounds, which encourage raising earnings within the band and building reserves outside it; debt covenants, which encourage avoiding breaches;
\item stock-price effects: around equity offerings, mergers, insider sales or option exercises; smoothing to lower perceived risk; beating expectations after guiding them down;
\item other: lowering profits for political or regulatory reasons or labour negotiations; big baths after management changes.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Conditions.} Earnings are managed only where incentives exist. Opportunity comes from accounting flexibility: intangibles, volatile operations, costs incurred before production, and complex revenue recognition. Management's reputation and history are relevant (pp.~111--112). Managers bear primary responsibility for reporting, and their discretion can inform or mislead (p.~72).

\emph{Discipline} (pp.~72--73):
\begin{itemize}
\item standards and regulators: the SEC reviews filings and brings enforcement actions;
\item external audit: in the EU the auditor of a public-interest entity reports significant assessed risks, including fraud, and how far the audit could detect irregularities \citep[Art.~10(2)]{eu-audit-regulation-2014};
\item internal controls: US management must report material weaknesses \citep[para.~(a)]{sec-sk-item-308};
\item governance: board approval, an audit committee and internal audit;
\item litigation risk.
\end{itemize}
Rules also constrain non-GAAP presentation (\hyperref[meth:earnings-management-presentation]{non-GAAP rules}).

\emph{Limitations.} Audits give reasonable, not absolute, assurance, and audit failures occur \citep[pp.~118--119]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}. The textbook cites a survey in which auditors challenged results in under 40~percent of audits and most challenged CFOs did not change their figures (p.~69). Enforcement follows events. Standards leave estimates and choices that discipline cannot remove.

\qffield{Notation} Not applicable.

\qffield{Conditions} US mechanisms are described from the textbook (2009) and current SEC rules; EU audit content from Regulation 537/2014. Survey evidence is quoted as reported in the textbook.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original} (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{Harbour}).
\begin{itemize}
\item Incentives are present: the 2026 lender review (\hyperref[ex:integrated-credit-decision]{decision}) makes leverage and cover salient, and a dividend lock-up tied to net debt to EBITDA would add pressure on EBITDA.
\item Opportunity is moderate: capitalised development and restructuring provisions give discretion.
\item Discipline: an unqualified opinion, any significant risks the auditor names, and the covenant definitions all bear on the assessment.
\end{itemize}
The analyst raises scrutiny of EBITDA-sensitive items near covenant tests.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Assuming incentives without evidence of opportunity, or the reverse. Treating regulation as preventing rather than deterring.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:reporting-results-quality]{quality}; \hyperref[meth:reporting-warning-signs]{warning signs}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Managing results through presentation, choices and estimates: conservative against aggressive accounting, non-GAAP and management-defined measures, and methods affecting earnings, cash flows and balance sheets]\label{meth:earnings-management-presentation}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Conservative against aggressive.} Conservative choices understate current results and assets; aggressive ones overstate them. Conservatively measured earnings are seen as higher quality because they are less likely to overstate. However, excessive conservatism lowers relevance and shifts profit to later periods \citep[p.~119]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}. If assets are overstated, cumulative earnings are overstated; if provisions are understated, earnings are overstated (pp.~120--121).

\emph{Strategies} (pp.~108--109): raising current income, big baths in poor years or at management changes, and income smoothing through reserves.

\emph{Methods} (pp.~110--111):
\begin{itemize}
\item income shifting: channel loading and early recognition; capitalising costs; choosing FIFO or straight-line; intermittent one-time charges;
\item classification: moving expenses below the line, loading overhead into discontinued operations, recurring ``special'' charges;
\item estimates: useful lives, allowances, warranty and restructuring provisions, valuation allowances and impairment assumptions;
\item cash flows: securitising receivables, stretching payables, supplier finance, and classification choices (\hyperref[meth:cash-flow-interpretation]{cash flows});
\item balance sheet: keeping obligations off balance sheet, covenant-sensitive classification and netting.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Presentation and non-GAAP measures.} A US registrant that publishes a non-GAAP measure must present the most directly comparable GAAP measure and a quantitative reconciliation, and must not mislead \citep[paras.~(a)--(b)]{sec-regulation-g}. In filings the GAAP measure needs equal or greater prominence, with a statement of why the measure is useful. Filings must not:
\begin{itemize}
\item remove cash-settled charges from liquidity measures other than EBIT and EBITDA;
\item adjust a performance measure to eliminate or smooth items \emph{identified as} non-recurring, infrequent or unusual when such an item is reasonably likely to recur within two years or a similar one occurred within the prior two;
\item present non-GAAP measures on the face of the statements;
\item use confusingly similar titles \citep[para.~(e)(1)]{sec-sk-item-10e}.
\end{itemize}
The two-year condition governs the \emph{description}, not the adjustment. The SEC staff reads it as a ban on calling a charge non-recurring, infrequent or unusual unless it meets the criteria. A registrant may still adjust for a recurring charge, subject to Regulation~G and Item~10(e) \citep[Question~102.03]{sec-non-gaap-cdi-2022}. That permission is limited by the general ban on misleading measures. Excluding normal, recurring, cash operating expenses necessary to operate the business may mislead. ``Recurring'' includes charges that occur repeatedly or occasionally, even at irregular intervals, and the staff weighs the adjustment's nature against the company's operations, strategy and industry (Question~100.01).
From 2027 IFRS reporters disclose every \emph{management-defined performance measure}, a subtotal of income and expenses used in public communications to convey management's view of performance, in one audited note. The note gives a description, the calculation, a reconciliation to the closest IFRS subtotal, and the tax and non-controlling-interest effects of each item \citep[Annex, IFRS~18 paras.~117--118 and~121--123]{eu-2026-338-ifrs18}.

\qffield{Notation} Not applicable.

\qffield{Conditions} The textbook's examples are US cases to 2008; the non-GAAP rules are current SEC text and IFRS~18 as adopted in 2026, effective 2027. The SEC staff's interpretations (last updated December 2022) state the staff's views; they are not Commission rules. Real earnings management (cutting R\&D or maintenance to meet targets) changes cash flows and cannot be undone by accounting adjustments (p.~108).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original} (\hyperref[meth:report-quality-evaluation]{Harbour's aggressive variant}, 2025). Three actions raise profit by 19.125 after tax:
\begin{itemize}
\item a bill-and-hold sale of 30 at a 35~percent margin (aggressive revenue recognition);
\item capitalising 15 of administrative cost as development (an expense choice);
\item delaying payment of 25 of invoices through supplier finance, which leaves profit unchanged but raises operating cash flow by 25.
\end{itemize}
Operating cash flow rises by 40: 25 from supplier finance and 15 because the capitalised cost moves to investing. Harbour's ``adjusted operating profit'' excluding restructuring (111) is a management-defined measure under IFRS~18 and a non-GAAP measure in a US filing, and must be reconciled. Suppose Harbour also restructured in 2023 or expects to in 2027.
\begin{itemize}
\item It could not describe the 18 as non-recurring, infrequent or unusual.
\item It could still present the adjustment, labelled as a restructuring charge, if the measure is not misleading.
\item If restructuring in fact recurs every year or two as part of running the business, excluding its cash cost may be a normal, recurring, cash operating expense whose removal misleads, however it is labelled.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Accepting ``adjusted'' measures without the reconciliation. Reading the two-year rule as either banning every recurring adjustment or permitting any adjustment that avoids the word ``non-recurring''. Treating conservatism as costless. Searching only earnings while cash flows and classification are managed.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:reporting-warning-signs]{detection}; \hyperref[meth:non-recurring-items]{non-recurring items}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Warning signs and methods for detecting manipulation in financial reports]\label{meth:reporting-warning-signs}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Red flags} \citep[p.~113]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}:
\begin{itemize}
\item poor performance (desperate companies use desperate means);
\item earnings persistently above operating cash flow;
\item pretax book profit persistently above taxable income;
\item a qualified audit report;
\item auditor resignation or unusual changes;
\item frequent unexplained policy changes;
\item inventories rising faster than sales;
\item structures that circumvent recognition;
\item frequent one-time charges and big baths.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Detection methods.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Trend and peer comparison of ratios that link related accounts: receivables against revenue (days sales outstanding), inventories against cost of sales and by classification, payables against purchases, capitalised costs against spending, and provisions against activity.
\item Cash against accruals. Compute accruals from the cash-flow statement as profit less operating and investing cash flow, scaled by net operating assets. Sloan's measure, from the balance sheet, is the change in non-cash current assets less the change in current liabilities (excluding short-term debt and taxes payable) less depreciation, over average total assets \citep[pp.~293--294]{wide-sloan-accruals}.
\item Tax against book: deferred tax growth and the book-tax gap.
\item Reading notes and governance: policy and estimate changes, related parties, auditor communications, control weaknesses and segment reclassifications.
\item Restating with consistent policies (\hyperref[meth:accounting-analysis-adjustments]{adjustments}).
\end{enumerate}
Classification-based schemes, such as supplier finance, can move a single indicator the ``wrong'' way. Indicators are therefore read together, not one at a time.

\qffield{Notation} Days sales outstanding $=365\times$average receivables$/$revenue. Cash-flow accruals $=(\text{profit}-\text{CFO}-\text{CFI})/$average net operating assets.

\qffield{Conditions} Indicators flag questions, not conclusions; growth, acquisitions and business changes move them legitimately. A statistical manipulation-detection model (Beneish, 1999) is in the catalogue but its paper was not available to read, so its variables and weights are not given here.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original} (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{Harbour}, 2025, against the \hyperref[meth:report-quality-evaluation]{aggressive variant}).

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & Reported & Variant & Variant, supplier finance as debt\\
\midrule
Profit & 55.38 & 74.51 & 74.51\\
Operating cash flow & 162.04 & 202.04 & 177.04\\
CFO$/$profit & 2.93 & 2.71 & 2.38\\
Days sales outstanding & 58.30 & 61.66 & 61.66\\
Days payables outstanding & 91.06 & 100.47 & 91.06\\
Growth in capitalised development & 20\% & 50\% & 50\%\\
Cash-flow accruals (amount) & 17.34 & 11.47 & 36.47\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

CFO over profit stays above two because Harbour is asset-intensive, so it does not flag the scheme. Measured accruals actually fall by 5.875, because supplier finance inflates operating cash flow. The receivables, payables and capitalisation indicators all move. Reclassifying the supplier finance as borrowing makes accruals rise by exactly the 19.125 profit boost. Harbour's reported 2025 Sloan accrual measure is $-4.69$~percent, low and driven by depreciation.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Relying on one indicator. Treating a high CFO-to-profit ratio as proof of quality in capital-intensive firms. Ignoring that classification choices change both cash flow and accrual measures.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:earnings-quality-persistence]{earnings quality}; \hyperref[meth:cash-flow-balance-sheet-quality]{cash-flow quality}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Evaluating the quality of financial reports: a step-by-step framework demonstrated on a company and its aggressive variant]\label{meth:report-quality-evaluation}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Steps} (adapted from \citep[pp.~111--113]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item understand the business, industry and the key accounting policies that matter for it;
\item evaluate how much flexibility the policies and estimates give;
\item identify incentives and conditions for bias, and management's reporting history;
\item examine the reporting strategy, including disclosure quality, audit opinion and auditor changes;
\item apply the warning signs and accrual tests (\hyperref[meth:reporting-warning-signs]{detection});
\item adjust the statements to a defensible basis and compare the conclusions before and after.
\end{enumerate}
Problems that affect quality fall into three kinds: recognition and measurement (amount and timing), classification (which line or category), and omission or presentation (what is disclosed and how prominently).

\qffield{Notation} Not applicable.

\qffield{Conditions} The demonstration uses a hypothetical company and an invented variant; the conclusions illustrate the method, not evidence about real companies.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Demonstration} (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{Harbour} 2025, reported against variant).
\begin{enumerate}
\item The business is a manufacturer with long-lived assets, inventories, development spending and a pension plan. Key policies are revenue timing, development capitalisation, provisions and cash-flow classification.
\item Flexibility is moderate to high in development and provisions.
\item Incentives: the 2026 facility negotiation, with covenants on net debt to EBITDA.
\item Reporting strategy: in the variant, disclosures would show a bill-and-hold arrangement, a jump in capitalised development and new supplier finance terms. IFRS requires the supplier finance disclosures from 2024 \citep[Annex, IAS~7 paras.~44F--44H]{eu-2024-1317-supplier-finance}.
\item Warning signs: days sales outstanding up 3.36 days, days payables up 9.41 days, capitalised development up 50~percent, accruals masked by supplier finance (\hyperref[meth:reporting-warning-signs]{table}).
\item Adjustments: reverse the bill-and-hold sale (revenue $-30$, cost $-19.5$); expense the 15; treat the supplier finance of 25 as debt and its cash flow as financing. Adjusted profit returns to 55.38 and operating cash flow to 162.04, while debt and cash are both 25 higher than reported.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Evaluation.} The variant's apparent improvement (profit up 35~percent, cash flow up 25~percent) is an artefact of reporting choices. Its net debt to EBITDA is overstated as healthy, so the lender would assess it on the adjusted figures.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Stopping at step~5 without restating. Treating disclosed arrangements as acceptable because they are disclosed. Applying the framework mechanically without industry knowledge.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:reporting-results-quality]{reporting quality}; \hyperref[meth:accounting-analysis-adjustments]{adjustments}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Evaluating earnings quality: indicators, sustainable (persistent) earnings, mean reversion, and why the accrual component reverts faster]\label{meth:earnings-quality-persistence}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Sustainable earnings} are the level expected to persist. The analyst recasts the income statement to separate recurring from transitory components, adjusts items to the periods they belong to, and measures earning power over several years. Five, or up to ten, years is typical, possibly weighted towards recent years \citep[pp.~602--603, 617]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}. Indicators of quality include:
\begin{itemize}
\item the share of earnings from recurring operations;
\item the cash backing of earnings;
\item conservative rather than aggressive choices;
\item discretionary expenses held steady rather than cut;
\item low exposure to business risk and to one-time items (pp.~118--121).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Mean reversion and accruals.} Accounting returns revert towards the mean. Sloan, using 40{,}679 NYSE and AMEX firm-years from 1962 to 1991, estimates a persistence coefficient of 0.841 for earnings (operating income over average total assets) \citep[pp.~293 and~298]{wide-sloan-accruals}. Splitting earnings into accrual and cash components, the accrual component is less persistent: 0.765 against 0.855 in pooled regressions, and lower in 86~percent of industries (p.~298, Table~3 p.~300). Firms with extreme accruals revert mostly within a year (p.~299). Prices behaved as if investors did not fully distinguish the components: high-accrual firms had negative subsequent abnormal returns concentrated around later earnings announcements (p.~290).

\qffield{Notation} $\text{Earnings}_{t+1}=\gamma_0+\gamma_1\,\text{Accruals}_t+\gamma_2\,\text{Cash}_t+v_{t+1}$, with $\gamma_1<\gamma_2$ in Sloan's sample; all scaled by average total assets.

\qffield{Conditions} Sloan's evidence is US, 1962--1991, excludes banks and insurers, and uses balance-sheet accruals before cash-flow statements were standard. Later work, in the catalogue's review by Richardson, Tuna and Wysocki, was not read here, so the persistence of the pricing result after publication is not assessed. The simulation below is synthetic: it illustrates the estimation, not the evidence.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A simulated panel of 40{,}000 observations is built with true persistence of 0.60 for accruals and 0.85 for cash. Least squares recovers 0.601 and 0.847, and a pooled regression on total earnings gives 0.763, between the two. Earnings with a higher accrual share are therefore forecast to revert more.
\item Harbour's 2025 earnings are heavily cash-backed. Its Sloan accrual measure is $-4.69$~percent, and its cash-flow accruals of 17.34 are 1.8~percent of net operating assets.
\item Excluding the restructuring (\hyperref[meth:non-recurring-items]{non-recurring items}), sustainable profit is about 68.9, and the forecast tests whether the 2024 margin returns.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Using one year as earning power. Treating all accruals as manipulation; most reflect growth and normal operations. Extrapolating earnings with high accruals at the persistence of cash-backed earnings.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:ratio-based-earnings-forecast]{forecasting earnings}; \hyperref[meth:reporting-warning-signs]{accrual measures}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Evaluating cash-flow and balance-sheet quality, and the sources of information about risk]\label{meth:cash-flow-balance-sheet-quality}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Cash-flow quality} is high when operating cash flow comes from sustainable operations: customer receipts and supplier and employee payments at normal terms. It is low when it comes from one-off working-capital releases, securitisation, stretched payables, supplier finance or classification choices \citep[p.~415]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}. Indicators:
\begin{itemize}
\item operating cash flow relative to profit and to investment needs;
\item the composition of working-capital movements;
\item supplier finance disclosures \citep[Annex, IAS~7 paras.~44F--44H]{eu-2024-1317-supplier-finance};
\item consistency of interest and dividend classification (\hyperref[meth:statement-linked-cash-flows]{IAS~7 and IFRS~18});
\item capitalised outlays moved into investing.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Balance-sheet quality} concerns completeness (obligations recognised, such as leases, pension deficits and guarantees), measurement (conservative or optimistic carrying amounts, impairment assumptions, provisions), and classification (current or non-current and covenants) (pp.~120--121; \hyperref[meth:financial-instruments-liabilities]{liabilities}).

\emph{Sources of risk information:}
\begin{itemize}
\item financial-instrument risk disclosures: credit, liquidity and market exposures, maturity analyses and sensitivities \citep[IFRS~7 paras.~31--34 and~39--40]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs7};
\item sources of estimation uncertainty with a significant risk of material adjustment \citep[IAS~1 para.~125]{eu-2023-1803-ias1};
\item covenant disclosures (\hyperref[meth:financial-instruments-liabilities]{IAS~1});
\item pension sensitivities and impairment key assumptions;
\item risk factors \citep[paras.~(a)--(b)]{sec-sk-item-105} and management's discussion of known trends and uncertainties \citep[para.~(a)]{sec-sk-item-303};
\item the auditor's report on significant risks \citep[Art.~10(2)(c)]{eu-audit-regulation-2014} and control-weakness reports \citep[para.~(a)]{sec-sk-item-308};
\item market and industry information (\hyperref[def:filings-disclosures-information-sources]{sources}).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} Not applicable.

\qffield{Conditions} The SEC items apply to US registrants; the IFRS and EU provisions as adopted. Disclosure content depends on materiality judgements, and absence of a disclosure is not evidence of absence of risk.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original} (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{Harbour}, 2025).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Cash flow.} Of the operating cash flow of 162.04, 12 is a provision not yet paid, while receivables and inventories absorbed 27 with growth. No supplier finance is used, and the interest classification is disclosed. Quality is adequate, with a known 2026 outflow.
\item \emph{Balance sheet.} Debt-like items are all recognised: leases, pension deficit and provision. Goodwill of 120 depends on impairment assumptions. The RCF's non-current classification rests on a roll-over right. Quality is good, with goodwill and pension sensitivity as the main measurement risks.
\item \emph{Risk sources to read:} the borrowings maturity and covenant notes, the pension sensitivity table, the goodwill test assumptions, and the auditor's significant risks.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Ignoring debt-like items outside borrowings. Reading risk factors as boilerplate when they change. Overlooking that strong operating cash flow can come from financing-like arrangements.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:report-quality-evaluation]{evaluation framework}; \hyperref[def:long-term-liability-compensation-disclosures]{disclosures}.

\end{qfmethod}
