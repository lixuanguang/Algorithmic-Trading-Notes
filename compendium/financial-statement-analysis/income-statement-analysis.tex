\section{Income statement analysis}

\begin{qfmethod}[Revenue recognition, contract timing and analytical consequences]\label{meth:revenue-recognition-analysis}
\qffield{Statement} IFRS 15 and ASC 606 organize revenue around the contract, distinct performance obligations, transaction price, its allocation and satisfaction of obligations. Recognition follows transfer of control, not invoicing or collection. Over-time recognition requires the customer to receive benefits as work occurs, control the asset being created, or a no-alternative-use asset plus an enforceable right to payment; otherwise recognition occurs at a point in time. Variable consideration is constrained against significant reversals. IFRS uses ``highly probable'' in that constraint and US GAAP ``probable'' within their respective terminology, so identical words elsewhere should not be assumed to imply identical thresholds \citep[IFRS~15 paras.~9, 22, 27, 31, 35, 38, 47, 56 and~73]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}; \citep[Summary, pp.~2--5]{us-revenue-2014}.
\qffield{Practice} A distinct device and two years of support have standalone prices 100 and 50; the customer pays 120 upfront. Allocate 80 to the delivered device and 40 to future support. At delivery, cash rises 120, revenue 80 and the contract liability 40; each full support year releases 20. Recognizing all 120 at delivery overstates revenue and profit at delivery by 40 before tax and understates liabilities equally; after one full support year the overstatement would be 20, while cash is unchanged. A principal controlling the goods reports gross consideration; an agent reports its fee, so revenue margins across the two are not directly comparable. Consignment to a dealer is not a sale before control passes. For bill-and-hold, invoicing alone is insufficient: assess the customer's substantive request, separate identification, readiness and the seller's inability to redirect the goods.
\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating rising contract assets as cash collections; ignoring returns, rebates, warranties and financing components; assuming every construction contract qualifies for over-time recognition. Trace revenue-policy changes into receivables, deferred revenue and cash, then assess whether growth reflects demand or earlier recognition \citep[IFRS~15 paras.~B34--B35 and~B77--B82]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Expense recognition and capitalization across the statements]\label{meth:expense-capitalization-analysis}
\qffield{Statement} Costs become assets only when the applicable recognition criteria are met; cash payment alone establishes neither an expense nor an asset. Inventory costs are expensed when goods are sold, prepaid services as consumed, and depreciable assets over their useful lives. Other period costs are recognized as incurred. Abnormal waste and selling costs do not become inventory merely to preserve margin. Depreciation allocates depreciable cost, rather than forecasting market value \citep[IAS~2 paras.~10--16 and~34; IAS~16 paras.~7, 15, 43 and~50--51]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}.
\qffield{Practice} An isolated development project costs 120: research 30 and qualifying post-criteria development 90, completed at year-end and available for use next year over three years. IFRS expenses 30 and capitalizes 90; a US non-software R\&D case generally expenses 120. Assume tax deducts all 120 immediately under the same hypothetical tax law. Compared with expensing, capitalization adds an asset 90, a deferred-tax liability 22.5 and equity/net income 67.5. It moves 90 of cash outflow from operating to investing activities in this stipulated example; total cash is identical. Next year's amortization is 30 and the deferred tax reverses 7.5, reducing net income by 22.5 relative to expensing. EBITDA, earnings growth, asset turnover and CFO can therefore differ without a better project.
\qffield{Conditions} Qualifying development is not a discretionary earnings-management election; IAS 38's criteria must all be demonstrated. US software rules are exceptions to general R\&D expensing, discussed in the intangible entry. Financing costs and their cash classification must be considered separately. A change in useful life is a prospective estimate, not permission to undo prior depreciation \citep[IAS~38 paras.~54 and~57; IAS~8 para.~36]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}; \citep[chap.~8, solutions~1--9 and~39--48, PDF pp.~201--205]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}.
\qffield{Source scope} ASC 730-10-25-1, reprinted in the issued 2023 amendment, retains expense recognition for R\&D within its scope; acquisition and joint-venture exceptions are distinct from internally incurred non-software development \citep[ASC~730-10-25-1, pp.~37--38]{us-research-2023}.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Non-recurring items, policy changes, estimates and errors]\label{meth:nonrecurring-policy-estimate-adjustments}
\qffield{Statement} Separate continuing operating economics from disposals, unusual gains and charges, but do not assume that a manager's ``adjusted'' measure is sustainable. Discontinued operations have a separate after-tax presentation when the governing disposal criteria are met; an isolated fire or restructuring charge is usually still in continuing operations. IFRS forbids an extraordinary-item category. A policy change normally applies retrospectively, subject to the new standard's transition rules and practicability; an estimate revision applies prospectively; a material prior-period error is restated retrospectively \citep[IAS~1 paras.~87, 97; IAS~8 paras.~14, 19, 22, 34, 36 and~42; IFRS~5 paras.~32--33]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}.
\qffield{Practice} In an isolated Meridian variant, reported pretax profit is 154, including an asset-sale gain of 12 and restructuring expense of 8 recurring across several years. At a 25 percent tax rate, reported net income is 115.5. Remove the gain but retain the recurring restructuring cost: normalized pretax profit is 142 and net income 106.5. Adding back every charge would overstate sustainable profit. Keep the original cash outlay and disposal proceeds in the cash forecast even when the income is normalized.

An asset costing 100 with residual value 10 is depreciated 18 a year over five years. After two years its carrying amount is 64. New evidence supporting six remaining years gives future depreciation $(64-10)/6=9$ per year, without restating the first two years. If the original estimate was a known misstatement, use error-correction rules instead. Explain the justification, tax effect, period comparability and impact on the denominator of ROA; an analyst's normalized series is distinct from the filed accounts.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Basic and diluted EPS, sequencing and antidilution]\label{meth:basic-diluted-eps-analysis}
\qffield{Statement} Basic EPS divides profit attributable to common shareholders (after relevant preferred dividends) by weighted-average common shares, not closing shares. A simple capital structure has no dilutive potential common shares. For a complex structure, apply the treasury-stock method to options and the if-converted method to convertibles; adjust earnings for saved after-tax interest or preferred dividends. Include instruments from most to least dilutive, testing each against continuing-operations EPS. Exclude instruments that increase EPS or reduce loss per share. Splits and bonus issues adjust comparative share counts retrospectively \citep[IAS~33 paras.~10, 19, 26, 31, 33, 41, 43, 45, 47, 64 and~70]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}; \citep[ASC~260-10-45-40, pp.~16--17]{us-convertibles-2020}.
\qffield{Practice} All share counts are millions. Net income 120 less preferred dividends 10 gives 110. Beginning common shares 40 and 20 issued on 1 July give weighted shares 50 and basic EPS 2.20. Ten full-year options at strike 6 with average market price 10 supply exercise cash 60, notionally buying back six shares; four incremental shares reduce EPS to $110/54=2.0370$. A bond convertible into five shares saves six of after-tax interest: incremental EPS 1.20 is dilutive, so include it, obtaining $116/59=1.9661$. Preferred stock convertible into four shares would save ten of dividends, incremental EPS 2.50; exclude it. Options with strike 12 and average price 10 are also excluded.

With a continuing-operations common loss of 30 and weighted shares 50, basic and diluted EPS are both $-0.60$ if these instruments all reduce loss per share. A two-for-one split halves both EPS measures without changing earnings. The assumed treasury repurchase is a denominator convention, not an actual cash buyback. A rising EPS caused by real buybacks should be separated from profit growth, financing cost and future dilution.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Evaluating common-size income statements]\label{meth:common-size-income-performance}
\qffield{Statement} Divide income-statement items by revenue, retaining meaningful subtotals: gross margin after cost of sales, operating margin after operating costs, pretax margin after financing and non-operating items, and net margin after tax. Compare time periods and peers on a common perimeter and accounting basis; size normalization does not remove product mix, principal/agent presentation, lease or capitalization differences \citep[chap.~3, solution~23, PDF p.~179; chap.~6, solutions~12--19, PDF p.~191]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}.
\qffield{Practice} Meridian's gross margin stays 40 percent in 2025--2026. Cash-type operating expense falls from 19 to 18 percent of sales and depreciation stays 5 percent, so operating margin improves from 16 to 17 percent. Interest falls from 2.5 to 2 percent; pretax margin rises from 13.5 to 15 percent and net margin from 10.125 to 11.25 percent at the unchanged 25 percent tax rate. Sales grow 11.11 percent while net income grows 23.46 percent: scale efficiency and financing, rather than a larger gross markup, explain the difference. Receivables grow 25 percent, however, raising a collection question that margin analysis alone misses.
\qffield{Interpretation} A peer with a higher gross margin but heavier distribution expense may have the same operating margin; first inspect whether freight is in cost of sales or selling expense. A one-time gain below operating profit can raise net margin without improving customer economics. Use segment trends and cash conversion to decide whether an apparent margin improvement can persist.
\end{qfmethod}
