\section{Inventory accounting}

\begin{qfmethod}[Inventory cost, net realizable value and write-downs]\label{meth:inventory-cost-nrv}
\qffield{Statement} IAS 2 measures ordinary inventory at the lower of cost and net realizable value (NRV), estimated selling price less completion and selling costs. Cost includes purchase, normal conversion and costs bringing goods to their present location/condition; abnormal waste, unrelated storage and selling expense are excluded. IFRS reverses a write-down when NRV recovers, up to original cost. US FIFO/average-cost inventory also uses lower of cost and NRV after ASU 2015-11; US LIFO and retail-method inventory retain lower-of-cost-or-market rules, with replacement cost bounded by NRV and NRV less a normal profit margin. A US write-down creates a new cost basis and generally cannot be reversed \citep[IAS~2 paras.~9--16, 28, 33--34]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}; \citep[ASC~330-10-35-1A--35-7, pp.~4--6]{us-inventory-2015}.
\qffield{Practice} Stock costs 100; expected selling price 95 and selling cost 5 give NRV 90 and a write-down of 10. Assuming tax deductibility only on sale and probable tax recovery, recognize a deferred-tax asset 2.5 at the hypothetical 25 percent rate. Net assets and equity fall 7.5; cash is unchanged. With initial debt and equity both 50, debt/equity rises from 1 to $50/42.5=1.176$. Inventory turnover can mechanically rise because both expense increases and the carrying inventory falls, so better turnover need not mean better operations. If NRV later rises to 105 while the stock is still held, IFRS restores the carrying amount to 100, reversing the associated DTA; US retains 90 before any further write-down.
\qffield{Conditions} Commodity broker-traders and certain producers have scope exceptions. Tax treatment is an assumption, not a universal immediate tax saving. For public US issuers the 2015 amendment applies to fiscal years beginning after 15 December 2016; its prospective transition is already historical in the 2026 examples.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[FIFO, LIFO and weighted average under inflation and deflation]\label{meth:inventory-cost-flow-comparison}
\qffield{Statement} Cost formulas allocate historical purchase costs between expense and unsold stock; they need not describe physical movement. IFRS permits FIFO and weighted average for interchangeable items and specific identification for appropriate distinct items, but not LIFO. US GAAP permits LIFO. Periodic weighted average differs from moving average, and periodic LIFO can differ from perpetual LIFO when purchases and sales alternate \citep[IAS~2 paras.~23--25]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}; \citep[chap.~7, solutions~4--14, PDF pp.~193--194]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}.
\qffield{Practice} Two lots of 100 units cost 10 then 14; all purchases precede a sale of 150 units at 20. Revenue is 3,000 and total purchase cost 2,400. FIFO allocates 1,700 to COGS and 700 to inventory; LIFO 1,900 and 500; average cost 1,800 and 600. Gross profits are 1,300, 1,100 and 1,200. Reversing purchase prices to 14 then 10 reverses the FIFO/LIFO results: FIFO COGS 1,900 and inventory 500; LIFO 1,700 and 700. Thus rising prices generally give FIFO higher profit/assets and lower turnover, with the reverse under falling prices, subject to quantities and liquidation effects.
\qffield{Interpretation} If tax follows the selected cost method at 25 percent, rising-price LIFO saves 50 of current cash tax relative to FIFO in this example; inventory choices alone do not create cash before tax. Liquidating old low-cost LIFO layers can raise profit unsustainably. If a disclosed LIFO reserve grows from 120 to 200, convert COGS to FIFO by subtracting 80, and add 200 to inventory. A hypothetical 25 percent deferred-tax adjustment adds 50 to liabilities and 150 to equity. Keep the analytical conversion separate from an actual tax-method change.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Evaluating inventory disclosures and operating explanations]\label{meth:inventory-disclosure-diagnostics}
\qffield{Statement} Read cost formulas, carrying amounts by class, expense recognized, write-downs and reversals, their reasons and pledged stock. Reconcile raw materials, work in progress and finished goods with production and sales. Turnover alone cannot distinguish efficient purchasing from a write-down, stockout or LIFO liquidation. Currency translation, acquisitions and changing prices can alter inventory balances without corresponding unit changes \citep[IAS~2 para.~36]{adopted-accounting-standards-2023}; \citep[chap.~7, solutions~52--55, PDF pp.~199--200]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}.
\qffield{Practice} Meridian's stock grows from 180 to 200 while sales rise from 900 to 1,000: both grow 11.11 percent. Yet the average-inventory conversion time is $190/600\times365=115.58$ days, and that duration could be risky for a rapidly obsolete product. Suppose a note shows finished goods rising 40 while raw materials fall 20. A demand slowdown or production overshoot is more credible than a general input-price explanation; inspect subsequent sales, customer cancellations, unit ages and markdowns. Compare a peer on the same cost basis before inferring operating superiority. A fall in inventory with a rising LIFO reserve need not be impossible: higher replacement prices can outweigh a quantity decline.
\qffield{Decision} For a lender, apply lower collateral advance rates to slow-moving or pledged stock and request an aged inventory report before expanding the borrowing base. For an equity forecast, reduce margin or add an explicit obsolescence charge if post-year-end realizations support it; retain a range if they do not. Do not book a numerical adjustment merely because a screening ratio is unusual.
\end{qfmethod}
