\subsection{Leases and employee compensation}

These entries cover lease accounting for lessees and lessors, the types of employee compensation, and the reporting of post-employment benefits and share-based payment. They also cover the disclosures on long-term liabilities and compensation, and how share-based pay and pensions enter forecasts and valuations.

\begin{qfmethod}[Lease reporting for lessees and lessors: IFRS~16's single lessee model against Topic~842's finance and operating leases, lessor classification, and the effects on statements and ratios]\label{meth:lease-reporting}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Lessee, IFRS.} At commencement a lessee recognises a right-of-use asset and a lease liability \citep[IFRS~16 para.~22]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs16}. The liability is the present value of unpaid lease payments, discounted at the rate implicit in the lease or else at the incremental borrowing rate (para.~26). The asset is measured at cost and depreciated as property, plant and equipment (paras.~23 and~31). The liability accrues interest and is reduced by payments (para.~36). Interest is presented within finance costs, separately from depreciation (para.~49). Principal payments are financing cash flows, interest follows the entity's IAS~7 classification, and short-term, low-value and variable payments outside the liability are operating (para.~50). Lessees may expense short-term and low-value leases straight-line (paras.~5--6). The standard applies from 1~January 2019 (para.~C1).

\emph{Lessee, US GAAP.} Topic~842 also puts a right-of-use asset and liability on the balance sheet for leases longer than twelve months, but keeps two classes \citep[pp.~1--4]{us-leases-2016}:
\begin{itemize}
\item finance leases are accounted for like IFRS~16; principal payments are financing, and interest and variable payments are operating;
\item operating leases have a single, generally straight-line lease cost, and all their cash payments are operating.
\end{itemize}
The FASB notes that IFRS~16 treats all leases like Topic~842 finance leases and also exempts low-value assets (pp.~7--8). Public business entities apply it from fiscal years beginning after 15~December 2018 (p.~7).

\emph{Lessor.} A lease transferring substantially all risks and rewards of ownership is a finance lease; otherwise it is an operating lease \citep[IFRS~16 paras.~61--63]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs16}. A finance lessor recognises a receivable at the net investment (para.~67). An operating lessor keeps the asset and recognises lease income straight-line (para.~81). US lessor accounting was largely unchanged by Topic~842 \citep[p.~4]{us-leases-2016}.

\qffield{Notation} For level payments $P$ in arrears over $n$ years at rate $r$: liability $=P\,[1-(1+r)^{-n}]/r$; interest in year $t$ $=r\times$ opening liability.

\qffield{Conditions} IFRS~16 as adopted in 2023; later amendments, such as those on sale and leaseback, were not reviewed. Topic~842 summary as issued in 2016; the 2020 deferral for some entities was not reviewed.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.} A five-year lease pays 100 a year in arrears at 5~percent.
\begin{itemize}
\item The liability starts at 432.95.
\item Under IFRS~16, expense is depreciation of 86.59 plus interest. Year~1 is 108.24 (interest 21.65), falling to 91.35 in year~5.
\item Under Topic~842 as an operating lease, the cost is 100 every year. Totals are equal at 500.
\item IFRS EBITDA excludes the whole 100. Operating cash flow excludes the principal, and under IFRS~18 the interest too.
\item After year~1 the IFRS right-of-use asset is 346.36 against the US operating asset of 354.60, which equals the liability here because payments are level.
\end{itemize}
For a lessor, interest income on a finance lease and rental income less depreciation on an operating lease both total 67.05 over the term. Only the timing and presentation differ.

\emph{Ratio effects} (lessee, IFRS against US operating): higher EBITDA, operating profit and operating cash flow; front-loaded total expense; the same debt and assets on adoption but different interest-cover and EBITDA-based leverage.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Comparing EBITDA or operating cash flow of IFRS and US lessees without restating operating-lease cost. Excluding lease liabilities from debt under IFRS~16 while including them in EBITDA. Treating IFRS~16 adoption as an economic change.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:accounting-analysis-adjustments]{lease restatement}; \hyperref[def:long-term-liability-compensation-disclosures]{lease disclosures}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Types of employee compensation and post-employment benefits: defined contribution against defined benefit plans, IAS~19 against US GAAP measurement, and economic against reported pension cost]\label{meth:post-employment-benefit-reporting}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Types of compensation} \citep[IAS~19 para.~8]{eu-2023-1803-ias19}:
\begin{itemize}
\item short-term benefits, settled within twelve months;
\item post-employment benefits;
\item other long-term benefits;
\item termination benefits;
\item share-based payment, settled in shares or in cash based on share prices, under IFRS~2 (\hyperref[meth:share-based-compensation-reporting]{share-based}).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Defined contribution} plans fix the employer's contribution: the expense is the contribution payable for the period's service, and only unpaid or prepaid amounts reach the balance sheet (para.~51). \emph{Defined benefit} plans promise a benefit, leaving investment and actuarial risk with the employer. The obligation is measured by the projected unit credit method (para.~67), discounted at high-quality corporate bond yields of matching currency and term, or government yields where no deep market exists (para.~83). The net liability (obligation less plan assets) is recognised (para.~63). Service cost and net interest on the net liability (net liability times the discount rate) go to profit or loss. Remeasurements go to other comprehensive income and are never recycled; they comprise actuarial gains and losses and the return on assets beyond the interest-rate income (paras.~120, 122--123 and~127).

\emph{US GAAP.} The funded status is recognised on the balance sheet, with changes through comprehensive income \citep[p.~1]{us-pension-funded-status-2006}. Net periodic cost uses an \emph{expected} return on plan assets, applied to a market-related value that may smooth fair value over up to five years. Unrecognised gains and losses are amortised only beyond a corridor of 10~percent of the larger of obligation and assets \citep[paras.~20, 30, 32 and~34]{us-pensions-1985}. Since 2018 for public business entities, service cost is presented with other compensation and the other components outside operating income \citep[pp.~2--3]{us-pension-cost-presentation-2017}.

\emph{Economic cost} is service cost plus interest cost less the actual return on assets plus actuarial losses. Reported cost defers part of it, so the analyst reconciles reported to economic cost and liability \citep[pp.~163--165]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}.

\qffield{Notation} DBO defined benefit obligation; FVPA fair value of plan assets; deficit $=$ DBO $-$ FVPA. Roll-forward: $\text{DBO}_1=\text{DBO}_0+\text{service}+\text{interest}+\text{actuarial loss}-\text{benefits}$; $\text{FVPA}_1=\text{FVPA}_0+\text{actual return}+\text{contributions}-\text{benefits}$.

\qffield{Conditions} IAS~19 as adopted in 2023. FAS~87 (1985) and FAS~158 (2006) are cited as issued; the current Codification was not consulted. Asset ceilings, settlements and multi-employer plans were not reviewed.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.} Opening obligation is 1{,}000 and plan assets 800, a deficit of 200. Inputs: service cost 30, discount rate 4~percent, actual return 2~percent, expected return (US) 6~percent, benefits paid 50, contributions 40, an actuarial loss of 20, and an opening unrecognised US loss of 120 with ten years of remaining service. The deficit ends at 234.
\begin{itemize}
\item IAS~19 profit or loss: service 30 plus net interest 8 equals 38. OCI: actuarial loss 20 plus the return shortfall $32-16=16$, total 36.
\item US GAAP: service 30 plus interest 40, less expected return 48, plus corridor amortisation $(120-100)/10=2$, gives 24 in profit or loss and 50 in OCI.
\end{itemize}
Both total 74, the economic cost, and the change in deficit is $74-40=34$. US expected-return accounting reports 14 less cost in profit; the total does not differ.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating US pension income from high expected returns as operating profit. Ignoring OCI remeasurements in pension cost. Comparing IFRS and US pension costs without separating service cost.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:post-employment-benefit-modelling]{modelling pensions}; \hyperref[def:long-term-liability-compensation-disclosures]{disclosures}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Share-based compensation in the statements: grant-date fair value, recognition over the vesting period, vesting and market conditions, cash-settled awards, and the IFRS--US differences]\label{meth:share-based-compensation-reporting}
\qffield{Statement} Goods or services received in a share-based payment are recognised as they are received. The credit goes to equity for equity-settled awards and to a liability for cash-settled ones \citep[IFRS~2 para.~7]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs2}. Employee services are measured indirectly at the grant-date fair value of the equity instruments (paras.~10--11). When awards vest after a service period, the expense is recognised over that period (para.~15).

Non-market vesting conditions (service, profit targets) are excluded from the fair value. Instead, the number of instruments expected to vest is estimated and trued up to the number that actually vest (paras.~19--20). Market conditions, such as a share-price target, are built into the fair value and not trued up (para.~21). Cash-settled awards are remeasured at fair value each period through profit or loss (para.~30).

\emph{Tax.} Where the deduction depends on intrinsic value at exercise, IFRS recognises deferred tax on the expected deduction. Any excess of the deduction over cumulative expense is recognised directly in equity \citep[IAS~12 paras.~68A--68C]{eu-2023-1803-ias12}.

\emph{US GAAP.} Since 2017 for public business entities, all excess tax benefits and deficiencies go through income tax expense as discrete items \citep[pp.~1--3]{us-share-payment-2016}. Excess tax benefits are operating cash flows. Shares withheld to pay employee taxes are financing outflows. Entities may account for forfeitures as they occur instead of estimating them.

\qffield{Notation} Cumulative expense at date $t$ $=$ grant-date fair value $\times$ instruments expected to vest $\times$ fraction of the vesting period elapsed; the year's expense is the change.

\qffield{Conditions} IFRS~2 as adopted in 2023; ASU~2016-09 for US differences. Graded vesting, modifications and the private-company practical expedients were not reviewed. Option fair values depend on volatility, expected term and dividend assumptions, disclosed under IFRS~2 para.~47.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.} Three million options are granted at the money with share price 20, expected term five years, risk-free rate 3~percent, volatility 30~percent and dividend yield 1~percent.
\begin{itemize}
\item The Black--Scholes--Merton value is 5.7157 per option; a one-million-path Monte Carlo gives 5.7215.
\item The options vest after three years of service. Expected forfeitures of 10~percent give expense of 5.1441 in each of years~1 and~2.
\item Actual forfeitures are 15~percent, so year~3 is trued up to 4.2868, and cumulative expense equals the 14.575 fair value of the options that vest.
\item A US entity electing to account for forfeitures as they occur would recognise $3\times5.7157/3=5.7157$ in years~1 and~2 and absorb the reversal later.
\end{itemize}
Harbour's 2025 share-based expense of 9 is non-deductible, which explains its 2.25 tax-rate effect (\hyperref[meth:tax-rate-measures]{tax rates}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Adding back share-based expense as ``non-cash'' without counting the dilution it represents. Comparing IFRS and US effective tax rates in years of large exercises. Treating a forfeiture true-up as a recurring saving.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:share-based-compensation-modelling]{forecasting share-based pay}; \hyperref[meth:earnings-per-share]{diluted EPS}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Presentation and disclosure of long-term liabilities and share-based compensation: debt and covenants, leases, post-employment benefits and share-based payment]\label{def:long-term-liability-compensation-disclosures}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Debt.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Classification into current and non-current depends on the right to defer settlement at the reporting date. When that right depends on future covenants, entities disclose the liabilities, the covenants and facts indicating difficulty in complying \citep[Annex, IAS~1 paras.~69--76ZA]{eu-2023-2822-ias1}.
\item Financial-instrument disclosures add contractual maturity analyses and liquidity-risk management \citep[IFRS~7 para.~39]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs7}.
\item US registrants present debt net of issuance costs \citep[p.~1]{us-debt-issuance-costs-2015}.
\item Supplier-finance liabilities and their payment terms are disclosed (\hyperref[meth:cash-flow-interpretation]{supplier finance}).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Leases.} Right-of-use assets and lease liabilities are presented or disclosed separately, with the line items that include them \citep[IFRS~16 para.~47]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs16}. Lessees disclose depreciation by class, interest, short-term, low-value and variable lease expense, and total cash outflow (para.~53).

\emph{Post-employment benefits.} IAS~19 requires disclosures that explain the plan's characteristics and risks, the amounts in the statements, and the effect on future cash flows \citep[IAS~19 para.~135]{eu-2023-1803-ias19}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the nature of benefits, the regulatory framework and governance (para.~139);
\item reconciliations of obligation, assets and asset ceiling (para.~140);
\item the significant actuarial assumptions in absolute terms (para.~144);
\item sensitivity of the obligation to each significant assumption (para.~145);
\item funding policy, expected contributions for the next year and the obligation's weighted average duration (para.~147).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Share-based payment.} IFRS~2 requires \citep[IFRS~2 paras.~44--47 and~50--51]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs2}:
\begin{itemize}
\item a description of each arrangement;
\item a roll-forward of options (outstanding, granted, forfeited, exercised, expired and exercisable) with weighted average exercise prices;
\item the share price at exercise, and the range of exercise prices and remaining life;
\item how fair value was measured (model, inputs, volatility basis);
\item total expense, the equity-settled part, and liabilities for cash-settled awards.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} Not applicable.

\qffield{Conditions} IFRS as adopted in 2023 with the 2023 IAS~1 covenant amendments; US disclosure requirements beyond the Updates cited were not reviewed.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Interpretation.} The disclosures supply the inputs for analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item the debt maturity table gives refinancing needs by year;
\item covenant disclosures show headroom (\hyperref[ex:integrated-credit-decision]{credit decision});
\item the lease cash-outflow total and liability give the debt adjustment;
\item the pension duration and sensitivity give the obligation's rate risk: for an obligation of duration 10.6, as in the \hyperref[meth:post-employment-benefit-modelling]{level-benefit example}, a 50~bp rise in the discount rate lowers it by about 5~percent;
\item the option roll-forward and exercise prices give the forecast of dilution (\hyperref[meth:share-based-compensation-modelling]{dilution}).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Reading the balance-sheet classification without the covenant note. Using a pension deficit without its sensitivity. Forecasting share counts without the option roll-forward.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:financial-instruments-liabilities]{liabilities}; \hyperref[meth:lease-reporting]{leases}; \hyperref[meth:post-employment-benefit-reporting]{pensions}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Modelling share-based compensation: forecasting expense and shares outstanding, treasury-stock dilution at forecast prices, and consistent treatment in valuation]\label{meth:share-based-compensation-modelling}
\qffield{Statement} A forecast treats share-based compensation as an operating expense linked to the business. Typical drivers are a share of revenue or of other personnel cost, or the grant programme disclosed. The share-based payment disclosures give the outstanding options, exercise prices, remaining lives and grant fair values \citep[IFRS~2 paras.~45 and~47]{eu-2023-1803-ifrs2}.

Shares outstanding are forecast from existing shares, exercises (cash in, shares out), buybacks and new grants. Dilution uses the treasury-stock method at the forecast average share price \citep[IAS~33 paras.~45--47A]{eu-2023-1803-ias33}.

\emph{Valuation.} Statutory operating cash flow adds the expense back because no cash is paid; a valuation must not. The consistent convention separates three groups of awards \citep[pp.~445--449]{investment-valuation-2012}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{future grants}: an operating expense, usually forecast as a share of revenue and deducted from free cash flow; keeping today's margin in the terminal value assumes grants continue at today's rate;
\item \emph{awards already outstanding}: claims on equity, valued with an option-pricing model and deducted from equity value before dividing by the primary shares, at the estimated value per share, which makes the calculation iterative;
\item \emph{outstanding but unvested awards whose cost is still to be recognised}: either in the forecast expense or deducted as claims, never both.
\end{itemize}
Adding the expense back while ignoring the shares given away treats compensation as free \citep[slide~14]{free-cash-flow-primer-2022}. The treasury-stock method ignores the options' time value and so overstates value per share. Growth in earnings per share from buybacks financed by debt is a capital-structure effect, not operating growth.

\qffield{Notation} Incremental diluted shares $=\sum_j n_j\max(0,1-K_j/\bar P)$ for tranches $j$ with $n_j$ options at exercise price $K_j$, with $\bar P$ the forecast average price; unvested tranches add unrecognised service cost to $K_j$.

\qffield{Conditions} The treasury-stock method approximates dilution for reporting; for valuation, an option-pricing approach, which values options as claims, is more precise. Forecast share prices are circular with the valuation; iterate or use scenarios.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item In the \hyperref[ex:harbour-pro-forma-model]{Harbour forecast} share-based expense grows 5~percent a year: 9.45, 9.92 and 10.42 in 2026--2028. It is deducted in operating profit and, being non-cash, added back in the forecast operating cash flow, as the statement requires. Valuation FCFF reverses the add-back: at 8~percent the three years' expense has a present value of 25.53, which an add-back would have counted as value. Because it is not deductible, there is no tax effect. Terminal NOPAT keeps the expense as an operating cost, so grants continue at the 2028 rate (\hyperref[meth:forecast-horizon-steady-state]{valuation}).
\item Harbour's 5 million vested options (exercise price 8, three years remaining, volatility 30~percent) are the outstanding claim. Valued with Black--Scholes--Merton at the iterated value per share of 8.49, they are worth 9.28, deducted from equity before dividing by 100.75 million shares. Treasury-stock dilution at the same value would give 8.56, higher because it ignores time value. Unvested awards are not deducted again because their remaining cost is in the forecast expense.
\item For the 2.55 million options vesting from the grant above (exercise price 20), forecast dilution is 0.51 million shares at an average price of 25 and nil at 18. The dilution therefore enters only in scenarios where the share price rises.
\item Harbour's 5 million vested options at 8 add 1.364 million diluted shares at an average price of 11 (\hyperref[meth:earnings-per-share]{EPS}).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Holding the share count fixed while adding back share-based expense. Deducting the expense for future grants and also dividing by a share count that includes those future grants. Using the current share price for dilution in a scenario where the price differs. Ignoring the cash received on exercise.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:share-based-compensation-reporting]{reporting}; \hyperref[meth:free-cash-flow-coverage]{free cash flow}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Modelling post-employment benefits: forecasting service cost, interest and contributions, splitting operating from financing, treating the deficit as debt-like, and sensitivity]\label{meth:post-employment-benefit-modelling}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{The five-step analysis} \citep[pp.~163--166]{financial-statement-analysis-2009}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item reconcile reported and economic cost and liability;
\item adjust the statements;
\item evaluate the actuarial assumptions;
\item assess pension risk;
\item assess the cash-flow implications.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{In a forecast:}
\begin{itemize}
\item service cost is an operating, compensation-like expense that grows with the workforce and wages;
\item net interest (deficit times discount rate) is financing in nature; IFRS~18 classifies it in the financing category from 2027, with service cost in operating \citep[Annex, IFRS~18 paras.~B53--B55]{eu-2026-338-ifrs18};
\item contributions are the cash outflow and can differ from the expense under funding rules disclosed under IAS~19 para.~147;
\item in valuation and credit analysis the deficit, after tax where contributions are deductible, is a debt-like claim deducted from enterprise value or added to debt.
\end{itemize}
Remeasurements are not forecast in a base case but are stress-tested with the disclosed sensitivities \citep[IAS~19 para.~145]{eu-2023-1803-ias19}.

\qffield{Notation} Deficit$_{t+1}=$ deficit$_t(1+r)+$ service cost $-$ contributions $\pm$ remeasurements. The approximate change in the obligation for a rate change $\Delta r$ is $-D\times\Delta r\times$DBO, with $D$ the disclosed (modified) duration.

\qffield{Conditions} The after-tax treatment assumes contributions are deductible when paid and the company remains taxable; Harbour's case simplifies deductibility (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{case}). Duration approximations ignore convexity.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Original} (\hyperref[ex:harbour-tools-statements]{Harbour}).
\begin{itemize}
\item The 2025 deficit is 48.16. Service cost of 11 is in selling and administration, net interest of 2.16 in finance costs, and contributions of 15 in operating cash flow.
\item The forecast grows service cost 3~percent a year, charges 4~percent net interest on the opening deficit and assumes contributions of 15, so the deficit falls as contributions exceed cost.
\item As a debt-like claim after tax, the deficit adds 36.12. That raises 2025 debt to equity from 0.55 to 0.60, and the lender's downside leverage for the recommended option from 1.99 to 2.18 times (\hyperref[ex:integrated-credit-decision]{decision}).
\item In valuation the after-tax deficit is deducted from enterprise value, so FCFF excludes contributions above service cost (\hyperref[meth:free-cash-flow-coverage]{free cash flow}). Under Harbour's simplified tax rule the cost is deducted when recognised, so settling the existing deficit brings no further deduction and the pre-tax 48.16 would be the stricter claim, lowering equity by 12.04.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Sensitivity.} For a 25-year level benefit stream discounted at 4~percent (modified duration 10.57), a 50~bp rate rise lowers the obligation by 5.08~percent exactly, against 5.28~percent by the duration approximation.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating pension contributions above expense as an operating cash drain without recognising they reduce a debt-like deficit. Mixing pre-tax deficits with after-tax cash flows. Forgetting that falling discount rates raise the deficit even when assets perform.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:post-employment-benefit-reporting]{reporting}; \hyperref[meth:accounting-change-effects]{IFRS~18 classification}.

\end{qfmethod}
