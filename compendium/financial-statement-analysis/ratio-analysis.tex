\section{Ratio analysis}

\begin{qfmethod}[Calculating activity, liquidity, solvency and profitability ratios]\label{meth:activity-liquidity-solvency-profitability}
\qffield{Statement} Trend, common-size, peer and ratio analysis reduce complex accounts to comparisons. Match numerator and denominator: annual flows usually use average balance-sheet stocks; snapshot liquidity ratios use closing balances. Specify credit sales, purchases, day count and whether debt includes leases or all liabilities. Ratios are affected by estimates, seasonality, inflation and consolidation; a benchmark is useful only when the business and accounting bases are comparable \citep[chap.~6, solutions~1--19, PDF pp.~189--191]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}.
\qffield{Practice} Using 365 days and arithmetic opening/closing averages, Meridian has receivable turnover $1{,}000/135=7.407$ and DSO 49.275 days, inventory turnover $600/190=3.158$ and DIO 115.583 days. Payable days use credit purchases, not sales: $110/620\times365=64.758$, giving cash conversion cycle 100.100 days. If COGS is used as a proxy for purchases, label the approximation. Asset turnover is $1{,}000/833.75=1.1994$. Current/quick/cash ratios are 2.244/1.218/0.449. Interest-bearing debt/equity is 0.456, EBIT interest cover 8.5, gross/operating/net margins 40/17/11.25 percent, ROA $112.5/833.75=13.49\%$ and ROE $112.5/446.25=25.21\%$.
\qffield{Interpretation} Turnover measures resource use, liquidity measures near-term capacity, leverage and coverage measure financing risk, and profitability measures returns on sales or capital. A write-down can improve a later asset turnover or ROA denominator without operational improvement. Negative equity, near-zero earnings and mixed fiscal periods can make familiar ratios misleading or undefined. Supplement ratios with dated cash forecasts and economic drivers.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Combining ratios and industry measures into a company assessment]\label{meth:company-ratio-evaluation}
\qffield{Statement} Interpret ratios jointly: a lower margin may be compensated by faster turnover; leverage can raise ROE while increasing default risk; a high current ratio can coexist with poor collections and obsolete inventory. Peer comparisons require consistent fiscal periods, accounting policies, geography, product mix and risk. Industry ratios supplement rather than replace statements: retailer same-store sales and sales per area, bank net interest margin and credit-loss coverage, insurer combined ratio, or property occupancy and same-property NOI address different economic drivers \citep[chap.~6, solutions~5--19, PDF pp.~189--191; chap.~12, solutions~1--7, PDF p.~221]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}.
\qffield{Practice} Meridian's operating margin rises from 16 to 17 percent and debt/equity falls from 0.610 to 0.456, supporting improved profitability and solvency. Yet cash falls 12.5 percent and receivables rise 25 percent versus sales growth of 11.11 percent. The current ratio falls from $400/170=2.353$ to 2.244; quick ratio moves from $220/170=1.294$ to 1.218. Do not call the entire business ``stronger'' from ROE alone. For this distributor, the relevant operating cross-checks are stock age, fill rates, customer concentration, gross profit per delivery and collection days, not a bank's net interest margin. For an original distributor comparison, 400 of gross profit on 80 thousand deliveries gives USD 5,000 per delivery; a peer with 450 on 100 thousand gives USD 4,500. If Meridian's on-time fill rate is 92 percent versus the peer's 98 percent, higher gross profit per delivery alone does not establish superior service or sustainable profitability. Investigate delivery size, product mix and lost orders before ranking the firms.
\qffield{Conclusion} Maintain a favorable assessment of operating margins conditional on customer and supplier evidence, but reduce confidence in immediate liquidity until collections are verified. Compare a high-margin peer only after checking whether it outsources distribution or reports commission revenue net. Investigate rather than automatically penalize an industry-specific ratio change: for example, same-store growth can improve while new stores lose money and consolidated cash falls.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Three- and five-component DuPont analysis]\label{meth:dupont-roe-analysis}
\qffield{Statement} With consistent periods and average balance-sheet stocks,
\[
ROE=\frac{NI}{Sales}\frac{Sales}{\overline A}\frac{\overline A}{\overline E}
=\frac{NI}{PBT}\frac{PBT}{EBIT}\frac{EBIT}{Sales}\frac{Sales}{\overline A}\frac{\overline A}{\overline E}.
\]
The components separate net margin into tax burden, interest/non-operating burden and operating margin, then combine efficiency with leverage. Intermediate ratios can be unstable when PBT or EBIT is near zero; the algebra does not make them economically meaningful \citep[chap.~6, solutions~16--19, PDF p.~191; chap.~18, solutions~1--2, PDF p.~255]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}.
\qffield{Practice} Meridian's three-factor decomposition is $0.1125\times1.1994\times1.8683=25.21\%$. Its five-factor route is $0.75\times(150/170)\times0.17\times1.1994\times1.8683=25.21\%$, agreeing with $112.5/446.25$. An independent comparison illustrates leverage: a firm with 10 percent margin, turnover 1.2 and leverage 1.5 earns ROE 18 percent. If margin drops to 8 percent but leverage rises to 2, ROE rises to 19.2 percent despite weaker operations. With unchanged leverage it would be 14.4 percent.
\qffield{Interpretation} Attribute a higher ROE to its components before calling it value creation. Buybacks, impairments and losses can shrink equity and raise or destabilize ROE. If the debt used to raise leverage also changes interest expense, update the margin and interest burden; the illustrative component comparison is not a claim that financing has no cost. Relate the return to capital cost and risk, not only to last year's percentage.
\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Using ratios as forecast drivers rather than mechanical extrapolations]\label{meth:ratio-driven-earnings-forecast}
\qffield{Statement} Begin with sales by volume, price and mix; forecast cost ratios, working-capital turns, depreciation from asset schedules, interest from debt and taxes from jurisdictional earnings. Historical ratios are evidence for assumptions, not fixed laws. A forecast of earnings alone is incomplete if it ignores the receivables, inventory and financing required to support those earnings \citep[chap.~6, solution~22, PDF p.~192; chap.~12, solutions~1--2, PDF p.~221]{statement-analysis-workbook-2020}; \citep[chap.~11, pp.~285 and~301--302]{investment-valuation-2012}.
\qffield{Practice} For Meridian, 2027 sales of 1,100 with COGS 60 percent and cash-type operating expense 18 percent give 660 and 198. Scheduled depreciation 55 leaves EBIT 187. Interest is 8 percent of opening debt 220, or 17.6, because the scheduled repayment of 20 occurs at year-end. Tax at 25 percent gives net income 127.05. Closing AR at 15 percent of sales is 165, inventory at one-third of COGS is 220, and AP at 20 percent of COGS is 132. These closing-balance driver ratios differ from the average-balance ratios used to assess historical turnover. They must be defined rather than quietly interchanged.
\qffield{Sensitivity} A one-point increase in COGS/sales costs 11 before tax or 8.25 after tax before financing responses. A five-point increase in closing AR/sales absorbs 55 of cash but does not directly reduce earned revenue. The full forecast below links those sensitivities to cash and debt capacity. Segment-specific margins and collection patterns are preferable where a consolidated percentage masks a changing mix.
\end{qfmethod}
