\begin{qfdefinition}[Sovereign credit risk: issuers and CDS reference entities, inverted sovereign curves, the 40 percent recovery convention against Moody's recoveries, and the 1998--2006 default record with later cases left to check]\label{def:sovereign-credit-defaults}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner gives ISDA's 2003 definition of a sovereign, ``any state, political subdivision, or government, or any agency, instrumentality, ministry, department, or other authority (including the central bank) thereof'', and notes that a country has many issuers (for Greece, the Hellenic Republic's several bond programmes and state companies) in several currencies \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``Sovereign Credit'', PDF pp.~269--270]{s7-gliner}. The \emph{reference entity} is the legal name used in CDS, ``Greece is the Hellenic Republic'' (Table~9.3, pp.~274--275). An inverted curve ``further out'' typically means ``the market is pricing in default risk'', as in Portugal's five-year yield above its long bonds (sec.\ ``Curve Inversion'', p.~271). Loss severity is one minus recovery, ``usually assumed to be 40 percent''; Moody's recoveries on defaulted sovereign bonds for 1983--2006 average 55 percent with a standard deviation of 24 percent (Figure~9.10, p.~278).

His Table~9.4 (from Moody's, \emph{Sovereign Default and Recovery Rates, 1983--2006}, June 2007) lists defaults from 1998 to 2006, among them Russia (August 1998, \$72,709 million: local-currency Treasury obligations first, then a moratorium on foreign-currency obligations, restructured in 1999--2000), Argentina (November 2001, \$82,268 million, a distressed exchange with haircuts of about 70 percent), Uruguay (April 2003, \$5,744 million, a distressed exchange extending maturities by five years), and Peru, Moldova, the Dominican Republic and Belize (pp.~275--278).

\qffield{Notation} Recovery $R$ as a fraction of par; loss given default $1-R$; amounts in the table in millions of U.S.\ dollars of debt affected.

\qffield{Conditions} Default record to 2006; Greece 2012 and Argentina 2020 from IMF reports of 2012--2022; Argentine default counts as published in 2010 (Reinhart), 2020 (CRS) and 2021 (Fitch). \emph{The recovery comparison.} Gliner concludes that a mean of 55 with a standard deviation of 24 ``is not enough evidence to dispute the standard 40 percent''. Whether it is depends on the number of independent defaults, which he does not give: the mean differs from 40 at the 5 percent level once there are more than about ten independent issuers. Recoveries also differ in measurement (trading prices after default against the value of exchanged bonds) and by restructuring.

\qffield{Practice} Peripheral euro-area spreads to Bunds, which one account calls key risk indicators, are the subject of \nolinkurl{dm-sovereign-bonds}; Gliner's account of Greece's two bailouts and of five-year CDS moving from 345 to 890~bp in April--May 2010 is in his chapter~11 (PDF p.~389).

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Unverified examples}: ``Greece 2012: PSI restructuring, largest sovereign default in history, \$200B+'' and ``Argentina 2020: Ninth default, \$65B restructured''. \emph{Against IMF reports}. Greece: the exchange subjected \texteuro197 billion of debt to the exchange, with a nominal haircut of 53$\tfrac12$ percent and NPV losses of 70 to 75 percent (at exit yields of 9--12 percent), ``the largest write down recorded for a pre-default sovereign debt restructuring''; 85.8 percent of domestic-law holders participated before collective action clauses were used \citep[PDF p.~46]{imf-cr-12-57}; the IMF's later evaluation gives \texteuro197 billion exchanged for \texteuro62 billion of new debt and \texteuro30 billion of EFSF notes, a write-down of \texteuro106 billion or 52 percent of 2012 GDP, with 97 percent of eligible bonds tendered \citep[PDF pp.~45--46]{imf-cr-17-44}.

So ``\$200B+'' can only be the face amount exchanged converted to dollars, not the loss, and ``largest'' holds for the write-down. Argentina: ``The September 2020 restructuring of US\$82 billion in FX-denominated debt held by the private sector (US\$65.5 billion of which corresponded to the foreign-law debt exchange)'' gave cash-flow relief of about US\$36 billion over 2020--30, a recovery value of about 52 percent at a 10 percent exit yield and 99 percent participation, using two-limb CACs and redesignation \citep[Box~4, PDF p.~42]{imf-cr-22-92}; \$65B is the foreign-law part only.

``Ninth default'' is the Congressional Research Service's count: Argentina ``has defaulted on its external debt (debt held by foreigners) nine times since independence in 1816'', and missing a \$503 million interest payment on New York-law dollar bonds on 22 May 2020 put it ``into its ninth default'' \citep[pp.~1--2]{crs-if10991-v6}; the note gives no list of the nine. Other counts use other definitions and are not to be merged with it. Reinhart's chartbook lists seven external default episodes for 1816--2009 (1827--1857, 1890--1893, 1951, 1956--1965, 1982--1993, 1989 and 2001--2005, the 1989 episode given no separate duration and lying within 1982--1993) and, separately, five domestic default episodes (1890--1893, 1982, 1989--1990, 2001--2005 and 2007--2009), on Reinhart and Rogoff's episode definitions \citep[Figure~3a and table, printed p.~16, PDF p.~17]{reinhart-chartbook-2010}; that dataset ends in 2009, before the 2014, 2019 and 2020 events.

Fitch, which counts rating defaults, records Argentina in default in 2001 (more than US\$80 billion of foreign-currency bonds), 2014 (a missed payment on foreign-law discount bonds after the grace period), 2019 (distressed debt exchanges on 30 August and 20 December through unilateral extensions of local-law bills) and 2020 (a distressed exchange from the decree of 5 April and missed interest within the 22 May grace period, rated RD) \citep[pp.~2--3]{fitch-sovereign-2020-study}.

So whether May 2020 was the ninth default depends on whose list and definition is used; the CRS figure is an attributed count, not a fact independent of method. The CDS side of Greece 2012 is now dated from ISDA: a unanimous determination on 9 March 2012 that a Restructuring Credit Event had occurred, after collective action clauses reduced payments on Greek-law bonds, and an auction on 19 March 2012 \citep[releases of 9 and 19 March 2012]{isda-greece-2012} (\hyperref[mech:cds-contract-credit-events]{CDS contracts}).

These sources support neither a 20--80 percent range of recovery rates nor higher recovery in orderly restructurings. An inversion prices default risk only when it is credit-driven; a front-end inversion in a default-free curve prices rate cuts (\hyperref[meth:curve-trades-carry-rolldown]{curve trades}).

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mech:cds-contract-credit-events]{CDS contracts and credit events}; \hyperref[meth:curve-trades-carry-rolldown]{curve inversion}; \hyperref[meth:fx-valuation-signals]{FX valuation signals} (sovereign risk and currencies).

\qffield{Further reading} Restructuring practice \citep{gap-sovereign-restructuring-playbook}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmethod}[Analysing sovereign and sub-sovereign credit: economic, fiscal, external and institutional indicators; debt dynamics and the debt-stabilising primary balance; debt structure and gross financing needs; local- against foreign-currency debt; general obligation against revenue bonds]\label{meth:sovereign-credit-analysis}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Indicators.} Emerging-market debt investors monitor real growth, inflation, the current account, the fiscal balance, public debt, the net external position, reserves against short-term external debt, foreign direct investment, private credit, bank loans to deposits and non-performing loans, and external debt to exports. They also weigh political and institutional stability \citep[pp.~370--371]{s7-fabozzifixed}.

\emph{Debt sustainability in three steps} (pp.~371--374):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Debt dynamics.} With $D$ debt to GDP, $pb$ the primary balance, $g$ real growth, $\pi$ inflation, $i$ the effective nominal rate, $i^f$ the rate on foreign-currency debt, $\alpha$ its share and $e$ the depreciation,
\[ D_t-D_{t-1}=-pb+\frac{i-\pi(1+g)-g+\alpha e(1+i^f)}{1+g+\pi+g\pi}\,D_{t-1}\qquad\text{(eq.~17-1)}. \]
The debt-stabilising primary balance $pb^*$ sets the change to zero (eq.~17-2). It is typically a surplus when growth is below the real interest rate.
\item \emph{Debt structure.} Foreign-currency debt raises the ratio when the currency falls. Floating-rate and short-maturity debt raise exposure to rate rises and refinancing. Captive domestic holders lower rollover risk. Domestic-law debt is easier to restructure than New York- or English-law debt.
\item \emph{Gross financing needs.} The primary deficit plus debt service, interest and amortisation (eq.~17-3), exposed to interest-rate risk and to rollover risk, the loss of market access.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Local against foreign currency.} Local-currency debt carries currency, convertibility and capital-control risk for foreign holders, and its yields have been more volatile (p.~362). A government can in principle print its own currency but not a foreign one, so foreign-currency debt is usually the weaker credit; losing market access forces drawing on reserves or printing money (p.~374; Interpretation for the ranking).

\emph{Non-sovereign government issuers.} US municipal bonds come in two security structures \citep[pp.~206--207]{s7-fabozzifixed}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{General obligation} bonds are secured by the issuer's taxing power, unlimited or limited, sometimes ``double barreled'' with fees or grants.
\item \emph{Revenue} bonds are secured by a project's or enterprise's revenues: airports, hospitals, universities, utilities.
\end{itemize}
Credit analysis covers the legal documents, politics and management, the underwriter, economic and credit indicators, and red flags (p.~1022). For revenue bonds it covers the limits of the pledged revenues, the flow of funds, the rate covenant, the priority of claims and the additional-bonds test (pp.~1023--1024). New York City's note defaults of 1975 showed that legal protections can give way under budget pressure, and Detroit's and Puerto Rico's defaults of 2013--2016 drew attention to the reliability of municipal ratings (pp.~1021--1022).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Veloria} (original; \texttt{trading\_notes.numerical.credit\_risk\_analysis.credit\_cds\_securitisation\_checks}).
\begin{itemize}
\item Debt is 70 percent of GDP, 40 percent of it in dollars at 5 percent. The effective interest rate is 6.5 percent, real growth 2 percent, inflation 4 percent and the primary deficit 1 percent of GDP. The currency falls 10 percent.
\item By eq.~17-1 the debt ratio rises 4.05 points, to 74.05 percent. Rolling nominal domestic debt, dollar debt and GDP forward gives the same.
\item The debt-stabilising primary balance is a surplus of 3.05 percent of GDP. Without the depreciation it would be 0.28 percent.
\item Gross financing needs are 1 percent deficit + 4.55 percent interest + 8 percent amortisation = 13.55 percent of GDP.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Conclusion.} The currency, not the budget, drives this year's deterioration. A fiscal adjustment of 4 points is implausible in one year, so the sustainability question turns on the exchange rate, the dollar share and the ability to roll 8 percent of GDP. Veloria's foreign-currency debt is the weaker claim; its local-currency debt carries currency risk for a foreign investor even if repayment is likely.

\qffield{Conditions} The formula treats the effective rate as a weighted average and the depreciation as applying to foreign-currency debt at the start of the year. IMF debt-sustainability guidance and the rating agencies' sovereign methodologies were not accessible or not read.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Judging sustainability on the debt level without its currency, maturity and holders. Treating a primary surplus as sufficient when growth is below the real rate. Treating a municipal revenue bond as backed by the issuer's taxes.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:sovereign-credit-defaults]{sovereign defaults and recoveries}; \hyperref[def:government-agency-supranational-funding]{government funding}; \hyperref[def:credit-risk-components]{credit risk}.

\end{qfmethod}
