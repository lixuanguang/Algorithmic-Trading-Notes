\section{Sovereign credit}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Sovereign credit risk: issuers and CDS reference entities, inverted sovereign curves, the 40 percent recovery convention against Moody's recoveries, and the 1998--2006 default record with later cases left to check]\label{def:sovereign-credit-defaults}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner gives ISDA's 2003 definition of a sovereign, ``any state, political subdivision, or government, or any agency, instrumentality, ministry, department, or other authority (including the central bank) thereof'', and notes that a country has many issuers (for Greece, the Hellenic Republic's several bond programmes and state companies) in several currencies \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``Sovereign Credit'', PDF pp.~269--270]{s7-gliner}. The \emph{reference entity} is the legal name used in CDS, ``Greece is the Hellenic Republic'' (Table~9.3, pp.~274--275). An inverted curve ``further out'' typically means ``the market is pricing in default risk'', as in Portugal's five-year yield above its long bonds (sec.\ ``Curve Inversion'', p.~271). Loss severity is one minus recovery, ``usually assumed to be 40 percent''; Moody's recoveries on defaulted sovereign bonds for 1983--2006 average 55 percent with a standard deviation of 24 percent (Figure~9.10, p.~278). His Table~9.4 (from Moody's, \emph{Sovereign Default and Recovery Rates, 1983--2006}, June 2007) lists defaults from 1998 to 2006, among them Russia (August 1998, \$72,709 million: local-currency Treasury obligations first, then a moratorium on foreign-currency obligations, restructured in 1999--2000), Argentina (November 2001, \$82,268 million, a distressed exchange with haircuts of about 70 percent), Uruguay (April 2003, \$5,744 million, a distressed exchange extending maturities by five years), and Peru, Moldova, the Dominican Republic and Belize (pp.~275--278).

\qffield{Notation} Recovery $R$ as a fraction of par; loss given default $1-R$; amounts in the table in millions of U.S.\ dollars of debt affected.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{The recovery comparison.} Gliner concludes that a mean of 55 with a standard deviation of 24 ``is not enough evidence to dispute the standard 40 percent''. Whether it is depends on the number of independent defaults, which he does not give: the mean differs from 40 at the 5 percent level once there are more than about ten independent issuers (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/slice37-checks.py}, section~12). Recoveries also differ in measurement (trading prices after default against the value of exchanged bonds) and by restructuring.

\qffield{Practice} Peripheral euro-area spreads to Bunds, which the legacy calls key risk indicators, are the subject of \texttt{dm-sovereign-bonds}; Gliner's account of Greece's two bailouts and of five-year CDS moving from 345 to 890~bp in April--May 2010 is in his chapter~11 (PDF p.~389).

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Legacy additions not in Gliner and not checked}: ``Greece 2012: PSI restructuring, largest sovereign default in history, \$200B+'' and ``Argentina 2020: Ninth default, \$65B restructured''. \emph{Checked against IMF reports} (second slice-37 follow-up). Greece: the exchange subjected \texteuro197 billion of debt to the exchange, with a nominal haircut of 53$\tfrac12$ percent and NPV losses of 70 to 75 percent (at exit yields of 9--12 percent), ``the largest write down recorded for a pre-default sovereign debt restructuring''; 85.8 percent of domestic-law holders participated before collective action clauses were used \citep[PDF p.~46]{imf-cr-12-57}; the IMF's later evaluation gives \texteuro197 billion exchanged for \texteuro62 billion of new debt and \texteuro30 billion of EFSF notes, a write-down of \texteuro106 billion or 52 percent of 2012 GDP, with 97 percent of eligible bonds tendered \citep[PDF pp.~45--46]{imf-cr-17-44}. So ``\$200B+'' can only be the face amount exchanged converted to dollars, not the loss, and ``largest'' holds for the write-down. Argentina: ``The September 2020 restructuring of US\$82 billion in FX-denominated debt held by the private sector (US\$65.5 billion of which corresponded to the foreign-law debt exchange)'' gave cash-flow relief of about US\$36 billion over 2020--30, a recovery value of about 52 percent at a 10 percent exit yield and 99 percent participation, using two-limb CACs and redesignation \citep[Box~4, PDF p.~42]{imf-cr-22-92}; the legacy's \$65B is the foreign-law part only. ``Ninth default'' (checked 2026-10-16; this supersedes ``not checked''). It is the Congressional Research Service's count: Argentina ``has defaulted on its external debt (debt held by foreigners) nine times since independence in 1816'', and missing a \$503 million interest payment on New York-law dollar bonds on 22 May 2020 put it ``into its ninth default'' \citep[pp.~1--2]{crs-if10991-v6}; the note gives no list of the nine. Other counts use other definitions and are not to be merged with it. Reinhart's chartbook lists seven external default episodes for 1816--2009 (1827--1857, 1890--1893, 1951, 1956--1965, 1982--1993, 1989 and 2001--2005, the 1989 episode given no separate duration and lying within 1982--1993) and, separately, five domestic default episodes (1890--1893, 1982, 1989--1990, 2001--2005 and 2007--2009), on Reinhart and Rogoff's episode definitions \citep[Figure~3a and table, printed p.~16, PDF p.~17]{reinhart-chartbook-2010}; that dataset ends in 2009, before the 2014, 2019 and 2020 events. Fitch, which counts rating defaults, records Argentina in default in 2001 (more than US\$80 billion of foreign-currency bonds), 2014 (a missed payment on foreign-law discount bonds after the grace period), 2019 (distressed debt exchanges on 30 August and 20 December through unilateral extensions of local-law bills) and 2020 (a distressed exchange from the decree of 5 April and missed interest within the 22 May grace period, rated RD) \citep[pp.~2--3]{fitch-sovereign-2020-study}. So whether May 2020 was the ninth default depends on whose list and definition is used; the CRS figure is an attributed count, not a fact independent of method. The CDS side of Greece 2012 is now dated from ISDA: a unanimous determination on 9 March 2012 that a Restructuring Credit Event had occurred, after collective action clauses reduced payments on Greek-law bonds, and an auction on 19 March 2012 \citep[releases of 9 and 19 March 2012]{isda-greece-2012} (\hyperref[mech:cds-contract-credit-events]{CDS contracts}). The legacy's ``recovery rates vary widely (20\%--80\%)'' and ```orderly' restructurings tend to have higher recovery'' were not checked. The legacy's ``market prices default risk'' holds for credit-driven inversions; a front-end inversion in a default-free curve prices rate cuts (\hyperref[meth:curve-trades-carry-rolldown]{curve trades}).
