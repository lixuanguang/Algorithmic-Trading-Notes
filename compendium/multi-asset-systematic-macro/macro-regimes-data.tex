\section{Regimes, indicators, releases and valuation}

\begin{qfdefinition}[A regime map of asset responses: growth, recession, inflation, deflation, liquidity and geopolitical shocks across rates, credit, equities and sectors, currencies and commodities, as hypotheses to test]\label{def:macro-regime-asset-map}
\qffield{Statement} A \emph{regime map} lists the direction in which each asset class is expected to move under a type of macro shock. Gliner's version is qualitative: a risk-on/risk-off grouping of assets, correlations that rise in stress (Figure~4.5, around Lehman), and the need to know which relationships hold in the current environment \citep[chap.~4, sec.\ ``Assessing the Relationships between Assets'', PDF pp.~98--102]{s7-gliner}; sector rotation, defensive sectors such as consumer staples in recession, and the need to know ``whether or not the economy is in contraction, recovery, expansion, or slowing growth'' (chap.~8, sec.\ ``Sector Rotation'', PDF pp.~216--217, Figure~8.3). The legacy map, kept here as the hypotheses it is:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Growth shock}: yields up, curve steeper, credit tighter, equities and cyclicals up, industrial commodities up, growth-linked currencies (AUD, CAD, EM) up.
\item \emph{Recession}: yields down, curves bull-steepen or flatten, credit wider, equities down, USD up, commodities down except gold; defensives (staples, utilities, health care) lead.
\item \emph{Inflation shock}: nominal yields up, curve bear-steepens, TIPS beat nominals, commodities (energy, agriculture) up; equities depend on real rates; low-credibility central banks' currencies punished.
\item \emph{Deflation or disinflation}: nominal yields and breakevens down, duration rallies.
\item \emph{Liquidity shock or financial crisis}: USD up sharply, Treasuries rally, credit spreads gap wider, correlations jump, volatility spikes, the cross-currency basis widens, funding markets seize.
\item \emph{USD weakness}: commodity prices up. \emph{Geopolitical crisis}: energy and gold up.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Sector rotation against Gliner's Figure~8.3} (corrected in the slice-37 follow-up; p.~217). The figure lists four phases: contraction (health care, telecom, consumer staples, utilities), recovery (financials, materials, consumer discretionary, technology), expansion (industrials, energy, materials, technology) and slowing growth (financials, telecom, consumer staples, utilities). The legacy table matches it only in part: its recession list (staples, utilities, health care) omits telecom; its recovery list adds real estate, which the figure does not show, and omits materials and technology; its expansion list omits energy; and its late-cycle ``Energy, Materials (inflation hedges)'' contradicts the figure's slowing-growth phase, which is defensive. Both are schematic: Gliner's figure gives no data, period or source.

\qffield{Notation} A \emph{bull steepener} is a steeper curve with falling yields, a \emph{bear steepener} with rising yields (\hyperref[meth:curve-trades-carry-rolldown]{curve trades}).

\qffield{Conditions} None of these signs is a law. They depend on the monetary regime and on what kind of shock moves inflation: the stock--bond correlation, for example, has changed sign across decades, which is what the pathway \texttt{macro-regimes-stock-bond} sets out to model. Classifying regimes needs information available at the time; a map built on ex post NBER dates or revised data is look-ahead.

\qffield{Practice} Use the map to state a thesis's expressions and its hedges, then test each sign on dated regimes with real-time data before relying on it. The legacy phrase that the curve ``summarises the entire macro narrative'' and that fixed income ``tells what the market thinks will happen before it happens'' is a reading of the expectations component of yields, which also contain term premia (\hyperref[mod:forward-rates-fra]{forward rates and term-structure theories}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy's ``real rates may fall initially'' in an inflation shock and ``curve bull steepens then flattens'' in a recession are ordering claims with no source; the ordering depends on the policy response. An inverse correlation between commodities and the dollar (legacy) holds on average in some samples and fails in others. \emph{Own test of the recession row in real time} (batch of 2026-10-17; protocol fixed before computing). Regimes were classified with a Sahm-type rule (three-month average unemployment at least 0.5 point above its minimum over the previous twelve months) on each quarterly vintage of the Philadelphia Fed's Real-Time Data Set, 1965--2025 (241 vintages; the three 2026 vintages skipped because October 2025 is missing in them), and asset responses measured over the next three months. In the 53 signal quarters none of the five recession signs is supported at $\abs{t}>2$: the 10-year yield change $-0.05$ point relative to other quarters ($t=-0.5$), the Baa spread $-0.07$ ($t=-0.8$), equity excess returns $+1.2$ percent ($t=+0.8$, the wrong sign, since the rule fires late and recoveries follow), defensive sectors (staples, utilities, health care) less the market $-0.7$ ($t=-1.0$, wrong sign) and the dollar $+0.8$ percent ($t=+1.5$). With ex-post NBER windows, which use future information, the signs are as the map says (yields $-0.16$, spread $+0.25$, equities $-3.1$ percent, defensives $+1.1$, dollar $+0.8$) but none reaches $\abs{t}=2$ (spread 1.78). So the recession row describes what happens inside recessions dated afterwards, and is not a tradable rule on this real-time classification. Script, protocol and outputs: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/macro-regimes-stock-bond/regime_recession_test.py}, \nolinkurl{regime_recession_results.txt} and \nolinkurl{regime_quarters.csv}. Limits: one row of the map, one real-time rule, three-month windows, U.S. assets only; commodities untested.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:global-macro-strategy]{global macro}; \hyperref[def:macroeconomic-indicators]{macroeconomic indicators}; \hyperref[def:central-bank-policy-framework]{central-bank policy}; \hyperref[def:tips-breakevens]{TIPS and breakevens}.

\qffield{Further reading} The pathway's readings on regimes and the stock--bond correlation (for example \citep{wide-ang-timmermann-regimes}).

\qffield{Index} regime map; macro regime; growth shock; recession; inflation shock; deflation; liquidity shock; risk on risk off; sector rotation; defensive sectors; cyclical sectors; stock--bond correlation; regime-dependent correlation

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Macroeconomic indicators for macro trading: growth, inflation, employment, external accounts, fiscal, consumption, housing and business surveys, and demographics, with the FOMC's inflation goal and Gliner's demographic table read correctly]\label{def:macroeconomic-indicators}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner's chapter on data releases \citep[chap.~12, PDF pp.~401--428]{s7-gliner} groups the indicators a macro trader watches:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Growth} (sec.\ ``Measuring Growth'', p.~402): GDP, nominal and real, quarterly with revisions.
\item \emph{Inflation} (sec.\ ``Inflation'', pp.~404--405): CPI and core CPI, PCE, the euro area's HICP, and the PPI, which measures ``the average change over time in the selling prices received by domestic producers''.
\item \emph{Employment} (sec.\ ``Employment and Population'', pp.~406--409): the unemployment rate, the labour force and participation rate (labour force as a share of the civilian noninstitutional population), and nonfarm payrolls, ``released on the first Friday of the month'', with average hourly earnings.
\item \emph{External accounts} (sec.\ ``Balance of Payments'', pp.~410--412): the current account (trade balance, net income and transfers), the trade balance, and the terms of trade, export prices relative to import prices.
\item \emph{Government} (sec.\ ``Government Indicators'', pp.~413--415): the budget balance and debt-to-GDP.
\item \emph{Consumption} (sec.\ ``Consumption Indicators'', pp.~416--417): U.S.\ consumption ``more than 70 percent of GDP in 2011'' and, with the United States at about 20 percent of world GDP, the U.S.\ consumer at ``a little more than 15 percent of global GDP'' (p.~416); retail sales, the savings rate and consumer confidence (Conference Board, University of Michigan).
\item \emph{Industry and services} (sec.\ ``Industry and Services Indicators'', pp.~418--420): purchasing managers' indices, with above 50 read as expansion and new orders and inventories among their components; industrial production; building permits, which lead housing starts; and capacity utilisation.
\item \emph{Demographics} (sec.\ ``Demographics'', pp.~421--426): working-age (15--64) and prime-saving (35--69) populations, with Table~12.2 (U.S.\ Census Bureau; heading and source on p.~424, data on p.~425) giving each country's peak year for both, and Trichet's remark that the current account ``is an important summary indicator'' (p.~424).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} \emph{Headline} and \emph{core} inflation (core excludes food and energy); PCE the personal consumption expenditures price index; PMI a diffusion index, $\%\text{better}+\tfrac12\%\text{same}$, scaled so that 50 means no change.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{The Fed's inflation goal.} Gliner writes that ``the Fed targets core PCE'' (p.~405), and the legacy that ``central banks target core''. The FOMC's goal is 2 percent inflation ``as measured by the annual change in the price index for personal consumption expenditures'', the headline index; the statement was first adopted effective 24 January 2012 and was reaffirmed effective 27 January 2026 \citep[Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy, pp.~1--2]{fomc-longer-run-goals}. Core measures are watched as guides to the trend, not targeted. Gliner's further statements that PCE ``targets urban consumers'' and HICP ``rural consumers'' are wrong as descriptions: urban coverage defines the CPI-U, not PCE, and the HICP covers household consumption in general. \emph{Payroll dates.} The Employment Situation is usually published on the first Friday but not always; the CES FAQ puts the release ``typically on the third Friday after the conclusion of the reference week'' \citep[Data Overview, Q8]{bls-ces-faq}, which usually, not always, falls on the first Friday of the next month; the BLS's own schedule is the record (the next release, as of retrieval, 6 November 2026) \citep[program page, retrieved 2026-10-16]{bls-ces-page}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Demographic peaks.} The legacy list of ``peak working-age'' years mixes Gliner's two columns: Netherlands 1989, U.K.\ 2009 and U.S.\ 2007 are working-age (15--64) peaks, while Germany 2006, Japan 2016, China 2032, Korea 2023, Russia 2025, Spain 2022, India, Indonesia and the Philippines 2050 are prime-saving (35--69) peaks; Nigeria shows 2050 in both (Table~12.2, heading p.~424, data p.~425; matched year by year in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/slice37-checks.py}, section~15). These are projections published around 2013, and they have moved. \emph{Against the UN's World Population Prospects 2024} (medium variant, 1 July populations 1950--2100, summed from five-year age groups; second slice-37 follow-up) \citep[file \texttt{WPP2024\_PopulationByAge5GroupSex\_Medium}, retrieved 2026-10-16]{un-wpp-2024}: the 15--64 peaks are Germany 1997, Japan 1995, China 2015, Korea 2017, Russia 2010 and Spain 2009 (Gliner: 1986, 1992, 2011, 2015, 2011 and 2004), but the Netherlands 2026, the United Kingdom 2044, India 2048, Indonesia 2045 and the Philippines 2053, against his 1989, 2009, 2027, 2023 and 2037, and the United States and Nigeria are still rising at 2050 (the U.S.\ to 2100); the 35--69 peaks are Germany, Korea, Russia and Spain all in 2024, Japan 2010, China 2031, the Netherlands 2030, and after 2050 for the United Kingdom, the United States, India, Indonesia, Nigeria and the Philippines (second follow-up check script, section~4; extract in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/wpp2024-working-prime-age.csv}). \emph{Why the years differ is not established here} (correction of 2026-10-16; the second follow-up attributed the differences to revised migration and fertility assumptions without evidence). Gliner's table is labelled ``Source: U.S. Census Bureau'' (p.~424) with no vintage, release or retrieval date; WPP 2024 is another publisher's 2024 revision. Publisher, vintage, base-period estimates and, for future years, projection assumptions all differ at once, and no controlled comparison was made (the same publisher across vintages, or both publishers in one vintage; the Census Bureau's International Database now requires an API key), so no cause is attributed. Two kinds of year should also be kept apart. Peaks before about 2012 were historical estimates in both sources (WPP 2024 gives estimates to 2023 and projections after): there, a gap such as Germany's 15--64 peak in 1986 against 1997, or Japan's in 1992 against 1995, cannot come from projection assumptions and must reflect estimation, revision or territorial coverage (for example, how Germany before 1990 is defined), none of which was checked. Peaks after about 2013 were projections in Gliner's table, and those after 2023 are still projections in WPP 2024; revised fertility, mortality and migration assumptions may contribute there, but that too is unverified. The point that projected peaks are revised with each release matters more than any single year. \emph{Other legacy claims.} Consumption ``more than 70 percent of GDP'' in the United States is Gliner's (chap.~8, p.~238; chap.~12, p.~416); the legacy's U.S.\ consumer at about 15 percent of global GDP is also his (p.~416), the product of two approximate 2011 shares rather than a measured figure; the slice-37 record wrongly said it was not in the passages read. PPI leading CPI by one to three months, that the ISM new-orders-minus-inventories spread is a reliable leading indicator, the quits rate as an indicator of labour-market tightness, wage growth driving stickier services inflation and the legacy's median ages are conventional practitioner statements with no source here.

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Capacity utilisation} ``over 100 percent'' (Gliner, p.~420; legacy) is not a reading one sees in the Federal Reserve's series, which measures output relative to sustainable capacity and has stayed well below 100 percent; read deviations from its own history. \emph{Nonfarm payroll scope} (resolved in the slice-37 follow-up). The BLS Handbook of Methods states that CES data ``exclude sole proprietors, the unincorporated self-employed, unpaid volunteer or family workers, farm workers, and domestic workers'', that ``Salaried officers of corporations are included'', and that government employment ``covers only civilian employees; military personnel are excluded'' \citep[CES concepts, page modified 28 February 2025]{bls-hom-ces}; the CES FAQ lists employees of non-profits and religious organisations as in scope, the largest group not covered by unemployment-insurance records, for which CES estimates separately \citep[Employment Concepts, Q4 and~Q6]{bls-ces-faq}. The legacy's exclusion of nonprofits is therefore wrong; its other exclusions (farm, private household, self-employed, military) are right. Both BLS pages were read through a fetching tool's extracts, not as full pages. Debt-to-GDP above 90 percent as a threshold (Gliner, p.~414) is the contested Reinhart--Rogoff finding, not a rule.

\begin{qfmethod}[Leading indicators for equities in Gliner's macro toolkit: commodity prices, the CDX high-yield index, the ISM PMI, the Baltic Dry Index, consumer confidence and Australian-dollar implied volatility]\label{meth:equity-macro-leading-indicators}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner lists indicators that lead or confirm equity moves \citep[chap.~8, sec.\ ``Valuation Techniques for Equities'', PDF pp.~233--240]{s7-gliner}: the year-on-year change in commodity prices, whose rise is inflationary and invites tightening (pp.~233--234); the five-year CDX high-yield index (CDX.NA.HY), rising with risk aversion and inversely related to equities (pp.~234--235); the ISM manufacturing PMI, where ``the ISM \dots\ goes below 50, the United States typically enters recession'' (p.~235); the Baltic Dry Index, a composite of Capesize, Panamax, Handysize and Supramax dry-bulk freight assessments, watched because raw materials lead (p.~236); the ARMS index (pp.~237--238); consumer confidence, since consumption is over 70 percent of U.S.\ GDP (p.~238); and three-month AUD/USD implied volatility, ``highly correlated'' with the VIX and inversely with the S\&P 500, most useful as a rate of change, though ``given the choice, using the VIX is a more effective factor'', and the two combined ``smooth out any error'' (pp.~239--240).

\qffield{Notation} CDX.NA.HY the Markit North American high-yield CDS index, quoted as a price or spread; ISM the U.S.\ Institute for Supply Management.

\qffield{Conditions} These are Gliner's chart readings (Figures~8.12--8.18), without lead-lag statistics or out-of-sample tests; The reading of ISM below 50 as recession has had false signals, since the index has fallen below 50 outside recessions.

\qffield{Practice} Treat each as a candidate signal for the nowcasting pathway: build it from real-time vintages and test its lead against equity returns.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy said the Baltic Dry Index ``cannot be speculated (no futures market)'' and that ship supply is ``fixed''; Gliner says neither. Dry-bulk freight is traded through forward freight agreements settled on Baltic route assessments (the \texttt{freight-forward-hedging} pathway; not checked here), and fleet supply changes with deliveries and scrapping.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:macroeconomic-indicators]{macroeconomic indicators}; \hyperref[meth:equity-index-valuation]{equity-index valuation}; \hyperref[def:vix-variance-dividend-swaps]{VIX}.

\qffield{Further reading} Mixed-frequency nowcasting \citep{wide-banbura-nowcasting}.

\qffield{Index} leading indicators; commodity prices year on year; CDX high yield; ISM PMI; Baltic Dry Index; dry bulk freight; ARMS index; consumer confidence; AUD implied volatility; risk barometer

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfdata}[U.S.\ macro release calendar: the usual monthly cadence of employment, inflation, activity and survey releases, FOMC and ECB meeting frequency, and why the agencies' own schedules are the record]\label{data:us-macro-release-calendar}
\qffield{Statement} The legacy cadence: the Employment Situation (payrolls, unemployment, wages) on the first Friday; CPI, retail sales and industrial production mid-month; housing starts and permits in the third week; GDP (quarterly), personal income and outlays (PCE) and durable goods late in the month; the ISM manufacturing PMI on the first business day and the services PMI on the third. Of these, the first-Friday payroll convention is Gliner's \citep[chap.~12, PDF p.~406]{s7-gliner}, and the BLS's schedule shows the next Employment Situation on Friday 6 November 2026 \citep[program page, retrieved 2026-10-16]{bls-ces-page}. The FOMC holds eight regularly scheduled meetings a year \citep[retrieved 2026-10-16, page updated 7 October 2026]{fomc-page}. The ECB's Governing Council takes monetary policy decisions every six weeks \citep[retrieved 2026-10-16]{ecb-governing-council}, about eight a year.

\qffield{Notation} Release times are U.S.\ Eastern; each agency (BLS, BEA, Census Bureau, Federal Reserve Board, ISM) publishes its own calendar.

\qffield{Conditions} The cadence is a pattern, not a rule: payrolls move when the first Friday falls too early in the month or on a holiday, and government shutdowns have postponed releases. Use the agencies' published calendars, archived as they stood on each date, to rebuild a release timeline without look-ahead.

\qffield{Practice} A backtest that uses data at its reference date rather than its release time, or uses revised values, has look-ahead; keep first-release values and their timestamps (\hyperref[meth:macro-event-queries]{event queries}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy said the ECB meets ``6 per year''; the ECB's own page gives decisions every six weeks. \emph{Placements checked against agency calendars} (slice-37 follow-up, retrieved 2026-10-16). Industrial production (Federal Reserve G.17) is released mid-month, for example 16 October and 17 November 2026 \citep[release dates]{fed-g17-dates}; BEA's advance GDP for the third quarter of 2026 and personal income and outlays (PCE) for September are both due on 29 October 2026, late in the month \citep[2026 schedule]{bea-schedule}; the Census Bureau's calendar shown (for 2027) puts retail sales near the 15th, housing starts and permits near the 20th and durable goods near the 27th of the month after the reference month \citep[economic indicator calendar]{census-econ-calendar}. These agree with the legacy cadence. \emph{CPI} (second slice-37 follow-up): the BLS schedule lists the October 2026 CPI for 10 November 2026, November's for 10 December 2026 and December's for 13 January 2027, all at 8:30~a.m., and the 2027 reference months between the 10th and the 14th of the following month \citep[CPI release schedule, retrieved 2026-10-16]{bls-cpi-schedule}, so CPI comes before retail sales and well before PCE for the same month. \emph{ISM} (second source follow-up, 2026-10-16, from a browser export of the calendar page, which earlier redirected scripted requests to a login; this supersedes ``not checked''). The ISM states that the Manufacturing PMI ``is released on the first business day of the month at 10:00 a.m. (EST)'' and the Services PMI on the third, and lists 2026 dates (Manufacturing, Services): 5 and 7 January, 2 and 4 February, 2 and 4 March, 1 and 6 April, 1 and 5 May, 1 and 3 June, 1 and 6 July, 3 and 5 August, 1 and 3 September, 1 and 5 October, 2 and 4 November, 1 and 3 December, with notes that January's Manufacturing report moves to the 5th ``due to ISM holiday'' and July's Services report moved for the Independence Day holiday observed on 3 July \citep[2026 calendar, p.~1]{ism-calendar-2026}. Counting business days against U.S. federal holidays reproduces every date except January's two and April's Services date: 6 April follows from skipping Good Friday (3 April), which is not a federal holiday, so the ISM's own holidays decide the count (an inference; \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/execution-market-making/source-followup-checks-2.py}, section~5). The legacy rule therefore holds as a rule, with ISM-specific exceptions to be read from each year's calendar.

\begin{qfmethod}[Event queries around data releases and policy decisions: surprise against consensus, revisions, conditional back-tests of asset returns after past events, hit rates and their small-sample error]\label{meth:macro-event-queries}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner's \emph{query} back-tests how assets behaved after past occurrences of an event like the one expected: choose the event and the conditioning (for an expected ``no change'' from the ECB, back-test meetings with no change), list the dates, and tabulate returns at fixed horizons with the share of up and down outcomes and the standard deviation \citep[chap.~3, secs.\ ``Simple Back-Tests and Queries'' and ``An Example of Building a Query'', PDF pp.~79--85]{s7-gliner}. His example is the Bank of England's quantitative easing in July 2012: the June minutes showed a 5--4 vote against more QE (against 8--1 in May), traders expected an expansion, and the query over the six earlier QE announcements shows the FTSE~100 up on 5 of 6 announcement days (83 percent), averaging 0.3 percent, and up on all six four days later, averaging 1.7 percent, at horizons of 1 to 5 days, two weeks and one month (Tables~3.1--3.2, pp.~80--83). He adds analogs, comparing current price paths with past ones (sec.\ ``Historical Correlations and Analogs'', p.~86).

\qffield{Notation} \emph{Surprise} = actual minus consensus forecast, often scaled by its standard deviation; \emph{hit rate} = share of events in which the asset moved in the expected direction.

\qffield{Conditions} With six events, a hit rate means little: five or more up out of six has probability 0.109 under a fair coin, and six of six 0.016 (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/slice37-checks.py}, section~4); overlapping horizons from the same six dates are not independent tests. The conditioning set is chosen after the fact, which invites data mining. A query on a release also needs the surprise, not the level: markets price the consensus beforehand.

\qffield{Practice} The legacy's principles (surprise direction and size drive the move; initial releases move markets and material revisions move them again; NFP and CPI dominate in the United States and PMIs globally; what matters depends on the regime) are standard practitioner views, consistent with the announcement literature the pathway reads, but not shown in Gliner's chapter. Report the number of events and a confidence interval with any hit rate.

\qffield{Pitfalls} ``If position moves against you immediately after event, cut quickly'' is advice, not a result. Back-testing on revised data, or on event dates known only afterwards, adds look-ahead.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[data:us-macro-release-calendar]{release calendar}; \hyperref[def:macroeconomic-indicators]{macroeconomic indicators}; \hyperref[def:central-bank-policy-framework]{central-bank communication}; \hyperref[meth:skill-luck-significance-tests]{tests of skill against luck}.

\qffield{Further reading} High-frequency policy surprises \citep{rt-kuttner-2001} and announcement-day returns \citep{wide-savor-wilson-announcements}, the pathway's readings.

\qffield{Index} event study; query; back-test; analog; data surprise; consensus forecast; revisions; hit rate; small sample; Bank of England quantitative easing 2012; MPC vote split

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Equity-index valuation for macro: top-down country and sector selection, price-to-book, dividend yield, P/E and forward P/E, free-cash-flow yield and market capitalisation to GDP, with Gliner's thresholds as rules of thumb]\label{meth:equity-index-valuation}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner describes a top-down approach to equities, from a global view to countries, then sectors, then optionally companies; if the investor knows where the next opportunity lies, the reward comes from concentrated rather than index exposure \citep[chap.~8, sec.\ ``Taking a Top-Down Macro Approach'', PDF pp.~215--216, Figure~8.1]{s7-gliner}. His valuation measures (sec.\ ``Valuation Techniques for Equities'', PDF pp.~228--233): price-to-book, where ``a P/B greater than 2.5$\times$ is generally thought to be overbought, and a P/B lower than 1.5$\times$ is oversold'' (the S\&P 500 near 1.5 in 2008, p.~228); dividend yield, paid while waiting (p.~229); trailing and forward P/E, with earnings yield its reciprocal (pp.~230--232); price-to-free-cash-flow and its reciprocal, a Graham-and-Dodd screen (pp.~232--233); and stock market capitalisation to GDP, ``more worrisome'' the further above 100 percent and quickly rising, as when Japan's passed 150 percent in 1989 (p.~233).

\qffield{Notation} P/B price over book value per share; P/E price over earnings per share (forward: analysts' next-year estimate); FCF operating cash flow minus capital expenditure.

\qffield{Conditions} The thresholds are rules of thumb for the S\&P 500's history, not levels that transfer across markets or eras: sector mix, accounting, intangible assets and interest rates move normal valuation ranges. Market capitalisation to GDP mixes a global firm's value with domestic output.

\qffield{Practice} For \texttt{cross-asset-style-premia}, value in equity indices is measured cross-sectionally (cheap against expensive countries), not against a fixed threshold. Gliner's Tobin's $q$, which he says gives ``a score between 0 and 1'' (p.~228), is not bounded above by one: $q$ is market value over replacement cost and has exceeded one for long periods.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy's P/B above 2.5$\times$ as ``generally overbought'', as a timing rule, has no test here; valuation predicts long-horizon returns weakly and timing poorly.
