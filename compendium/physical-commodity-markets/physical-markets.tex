\section{Crude oil, metals and agricultural markets}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Crude oil markets: grades, WTI and Brent, OPEC and spare capacity, chokepoints and pipelines, and the 3:2:1 crack spread, as of 2012--2013]\label{def:crude-oil-markets}
\qffield{Statement} Oil supplied 33 percent of world energy demand in 2012, coal 30 and natural gas 24 \citep[chap.~10, sec.\ ``Energy'', PDF p.~301]{s7-gliner}. Quality is measured by API gravity (higher means lighter; below 10 it sinks in water; 25 or less is ``heavy'') and sulphur content (sweet against sour), light sweet grades being the most valuable (pp.~301--302). OPEC has 12 members producing over 35 percent of world output and holding more than 70 percent of reserves; Saudi Arabia has the largest spare capacity, so ``the Call on OPEC'' usually means Saudi production rises (pp.~302--305, Table~10.4, OPEC-12 quota 30 million barrels a day as of September 2013).

WTI, the U.S.\ benchmark, is delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma, and trades on NYMEX (CL), 1,000 barrels a contract; Brent, from North Sea fields, ``is used to price more than half of the world's internationally traded crude oil'' and trades on ICE (CO), 1,000 barrels; Brent's premium over WTI in Gliner's time reflected North Sea depletion, seaborne supply risk and pipeline constraints at Cushing (pp.~305--307). World consumption was 90 million barrels a day in 2012; the Strait of Hormuz carried 17 million barrels a day in 2011, ``about 20 percent of global traded oil and 35 percent of seaborne oil'', Malacca 14 million, Bab el-Mandeb 3 million and the Suez Canal 2 million; the Druzhba pipeline runs over 2,300 miles and carries up to 1.4 million barrels a day (pp.~308--310).

The \emph{crack spread} is the refining margin; the 3:2:1 spread (three barrels of crude against two of gasoline and one of heating oil) is the most popular because gasoline output is almost double heating oil's, with product prices in cents a gallon converted at 42 gallons a barrel (sec.\ ``Crack Spread'', pp.~311--312).

\qffield{Notation} 3:2:1 crack per barrel $=\big[2\times(\text{RBOB}\times0.42)+1\times(\text{HO}\times0.42)-3\times\text{CL}\big]/3$, products in cents per gallon and crude in dollars per barrel; Gliner's worked example gives \$27.95 a barrel (p.~312), and crude at \$80, gasoline at \$2.40 and heating oil at \$2.70 a gallon give \$25.00.

\qffield{Conditions} All shares, capacities, membership and contract details are Gliner's, as of 2011--2013. Benchmark composition and trade flows may have changed since and were not checked against EIA or exchange sources. \emph{Membership}. OPEC's own home page, retrieved on 16 October 2026, lists twelve Member Countries in its navigation: Algeria, Congo, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Libya, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Venezuela \citep[home page navigation, retrieved 2026-10-16]{opec-home-2026}; its member-country and press-release pages refused automated access.

\emph{The UAE}. Four kinds of evidence, kept apart. (i) The announcement: the UAE's state news agency reported on 28 April 2026 that the UAE ``announced its decision to exit the Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC and OPEC+), effective 1 May 2026'', having joined in 1967 through Abu Dhabi \citep[pp.~1--2]{wam-uae-opec-2026}.

(ii) EIA's classification: its analysis of 23 June 2026 treats the UAE as having left effective 1 May, and puts OPEC's 2025 crude output, UAE included, at 28.0 million barrels a day, 35 percent of the world total, or 31 percent without the UAE, and OPEC+ at about 46 percent, about 42 without it \citep[web page, retrieved 2026-10-16]{eia-uae-opec-2026}; its live glossary lists eleven current members without the UAE \citep[glossary entry, retrieved 2026-10-16]{eia-opec-glossary}.

(iii) OPEC's own pages: the site's navigation, on the home page and on a press release of 3 May 2026, still lists the UAE among twelve Member Countries, and that release, on a June production adjustment of 188 thousand barrels a day by seven OPEC+ countries (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman), says nothing about membership \citep[release and site navigation, retrieved 2026-10-16]{opec-pr-2026-05-03}.

(iv) The statutory rule: under Article~8A of OPEC's Statute a withdrawal notice ``shall take effect at the beginning of the next calendar year after the date of its receipt by the Conference, subject to the Member having at that time fulfilled all financial obligations arising out of its membership'' \citep[2021 edition, Art.~8A, PDF p.~12]{opec-statute-2021}, which on its face would put the legal effect at 1 January 2027, not 1 May 2026, unless the Conference agreed otherwise.

No OPEC statement on the date or legal effect of the UAE's withdrawal was found, so whether the UAE is a member in law on 16 October 2026 is open; for market data, EIA already counts OPEC without it. EIA also notes the conflict in Iran from 28 February 2026 and the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, so Gliner's chokepoint flows above are historical.

\qffield{Practice} The crack spread is a margin before operating costs; refiners' actual yields and product slates differ from 3:2:1. Political risk in producers and chokepoint disruptions move Brent first, in Gliner's account.

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Terminology.} ``Kerosene (diesel, jet fuel)'' follows Gliner's ``kerosene is used for diesel'' (p.~301); kerosene is jet fuel's base and diesel is a separate distillate. Gliner calls Saudi Aramco ``private'' and lists it among the ``biggest IOCs'' (pp.~303--304); it is a state-owned national oil company. ``Brent \dots\ Delivery at Sullom Voe'' repeats Gliner's ``deliverable to Sullom Voe'' (p.~306). ICE now describes its Brent Crude futures as ``a deliverable contract based on EFP delivery with an option to cash settle against the ICE Brent Index price for the last trading day'', 1,000 barrels, trading to the last business day of the second month preceding the contract month \citep[contract specifications, retrieved 2026-10-16]{ice-brent-spec}: delivery is by exchange for physical, not to a terminal, and there is a cash-settlement option.

\emph{NYMEX WTI, in part}. CME's rulebook still refused automated access, but NYMEX's certification to the CFTC of 27 January 2023 (Submission 23-057) reprints Rules 200101 and 200104 of the Light Sweet Crude Oil futures (CL) for contract months from April 2023: deliverable domestic crude is a pipeline's common Domestic Sweet stream of WTI-type light sweet crude with, among other limits, sulphur at most 0.42 percent by weight, API gravity from 37 to 42 degrees and Reid vapour pressure below 9.5 psi (the test method moving to ASTM D-6377), delivered free on board at any pipeline or storage facility in Cushing, Oklahoma with access to Enterprise, Enbridge or Plains storage, each ``including any successor in such capacity'' \citep[Exhibits A--B]{nymex-23-057}.

\emph{Current chapter} (official PDF downloaded through a browser on 2026-10-16, undated). Chapter~200 now names ASTM D-6377 for vapour pressure and keeps the 23-057 grade and Cushing terms; the trading unit is 1,000 U.S. barrels (42,000 gallons), with a delivery tolerance of 2 percent either way except for book-outs and in-tank or in-line transfers; the minimum fluctuation is \$0.01 a barrel (\$10 a contract); and trading ends on the third business day before the 25th calendar day of the month preceding delivery, or, if the 25th is not a business day, the third business day before the last business day preceding the 25th \citep[Rules 200101 and 200102.B--F]{nymex-ch200-2026}. ``Historically WTI traded at premium (higher grade)'' is an interpretation; Gliner says only that WTI is the higher grade.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:natural-gas-market]{natural gas}; \hyperref[def:contango-backwardation-stocks]{contango and backwardation}; \hyperref[def:commodity-indices-risk-return]{commodity indices}.

\qffield{Further reading} The pathway's readings on benchmarks, price reporting and the negative WTI settlement of April 2020.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Metals markets for macro: gold and real rates, silver, platinum, copper and aluminium, with their main producers, uses and contracts as of 2013]\label{def:metals-markets}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Gold} (XAU) \citep[chap.~10, sec.\ ``Precious Metals'', PDF pp.~319--321]{s7-gliner}: jewellery ``more than 40 percent of demand'' and investment the second driver; central banks held 31,920 tonnes in October 2013, the United States 8,134 tonnes, 73 percent of its reserves (Table~10.6); ``China has overtaken'' South Africa ``as the leading producer'', and ``China, the United States, Australia, and South Africa collectively account for more than 40 percent of global gold production'' (p.~320); India, the largest consumer, used over 500 tonnes for jewellery and 300 for investment in 2011, ``of which 50 percent is used for weddings''; gold is preferred to fiat money when real interest rates are negative (Barsky and Summers's Gibson's paradox); GLD trades at a tenth of an ounce and COMEX gold (GC) is 100 troy ounces.

\emph{Copper} (sec.\ ``Industrial Metals'', pp.~325--327): ``Dr.\ Copper'', a leading indicator; production led by Chile and Peru; China the second producer and largest importer, about 40 percent of imports; uses equipment manufacturing 52 percent, building construction 33 and infrastructure 15; LME copper in dollars a tonne, 25 tonnes a contract, and COMEX copper (HG) in cents a pound, 25,000 pounds. \emph{Aluminium} (pp.~327--328): the most abundant metal in the crust; China over 40 percent of both production and consumption and self-sufficient; energy-intensive to produce; LME aluminium 25 tonnes.

\emph{Copper} (sec.\ ``Industrial Metals'', pp.~325--327): ``Dr.\ Copper'', a leading indicator; production led by Chile and Peru; China the second producer and largest importer, about 40 percent of imports; uses equipment manufacturing 52 percent, building construction 33 and infrastructure 15; LME copper in dollars a tonne, 25 tonnes a contract, and COMEX copper (HG) in cents a pound, 25,000 pounds.

\qffield{Notation} Troy ounce 31.1 grams; LME 3-month rolling forward quoted in dollars per tonne.

\qffield{Conditions} Figures are Gliner's for 2011--2013, from the World Gold Council, GFMS, ICSG, USGS and the World Copper Bulletin as he cites them; production shares are compared with USGS in Pitfalls. \emph{Demand shares now}. Gold: the World Gold Council's full-year 2025 figures give total demand including OTC of 5,002 tonnes, investment 2,175 tonnes and central banks 863, and jewellery at a five-year low of 1,542 tonnes (India 430.5, mainland China 360.1) \citep[press release and jewellery section, 29 January 2026]{wgc-gdt-fy2025}: jewellery is about 31 percent of demand, not Gliner's ``more than 40 percent'', and investment, at about 43 percent, is now the largest use. Silver: the \emph{World Silver Survey 2026} gives 2025 total demand of 1.13 billion ounces and industrial demand of 657.4 million, about 58 percent, so most silver demand is still industrial; mine supply was 846.6 million ounces \citep[press release of 15 April 2026]{silver-institute-wss-2026}.

\emph{Platinum now}. Metals Focus's figures for the World Platinum Investment Council put 2025 total demand at 8{,}671 koz: automotive (autocatalyst) 3{,}039, jewellery 2{,}214, industrial 2{,}266 and net investment 1{,}152, and refined mine production at 5{,}557 koz, of which South Africa 3{,}957, Russia 677, Zimbabwe 515 and North America 212 \citep[Table~1, p.~5]{wpic-pq-2026q2}. Those are shares of 35.0, 25.5, 26.1 and 13.3 percent of demand and 71.2 percent of refined mine supply from South Africa (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/execution-market-making/market-estimators-rules-checks.py}, section~7); Gliner's catalytic converters at 38 percent and jewellery at 31 percent are 2013 figures on another basis. The same table forecasts a 265 koz surplus for 2026 after deficits in 2023--2025, a forecast, not data.

\emph{Copper and aluminium now}. The ICSG's \emph{World Copper Factbook 2025}, on preliminary 2024 figures, gives mine output of 23 million tonnes (Chile 24 percent), smelter output of 23.3 million (China 51 percent), refined output of 27.5 million (China 45 percent) and refined usage, by semis plants and other first users, of 27.4 million, of which China 58 percent \citep[p.~5]{icsg-factbook-2025}: Gliner's ``roughly 40 percent of imports'' for China is not comparable with a usage share.

By end use the Factbook's 2024 chart, sourced to the International Wrought Copper Council, has building construction 26 percent, infrastructure (power and telecom) 17, transport 13, industrial equipment 12, consumer and general products, cooling and electronics 23, and others 9 (p.~48, read as an image); the ICA's 2023 dataset, for 2022 and the copper content of semis by end use (world total 30{,}582.6 thousand tonnes), groups its markets as Gliner does, into building construction 26.2 percent, infrastructure 17.3 and equipment manufacture 56.5 (industrial 11.5, transport 12.9 and other equipment 32.0) \citep[World sheet, rows~48--51]{ica-semis-enduse-2023}, against Gliner's 33, 15 and 52; the Factbook's 2024 chart, grouped the same way, gives 26, 17 and 57 (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/execution-market-making/contract-specifications-statistics-checks.py}, section~3).

Gliner's year and basis are not stated, so the gap is a difference between his figures and the 2022 and 2024 data, not a measured trend. For aluminium, the IAI and Mission Possible Partnership's April 2023 transition strategy says ``Approximately 50\% of global aluminium demand today comes from the transport and construction sectors (26\% and 24\%, respectively)'', with electrical and machinery equipment 11 percent each, foil stock and packaging 8 percent each and consumer durables 6 (94 percent named); it credits the passage on p.~15 to a 2020 World Economic Forum and Accenture report and the same figures on p.~26 to the IAI Material Flow Model, accessed January 2022 \citep[PDF pp.~15 and~26, endnotes 3 and~16]{iai-mpp-2023}, so they describe a model baseline of about 2020, not a dated statistical release, and give no country shares.

\emph{Contracts.} COMEX: copper (HG) 25{,}000 pounds of Grade~1 electrolytic cathode (ASTM B115), quoted in multiples of five hundredths of a cent a pound (\$12.50 a contract); silver (SI) 5{,}000 troy ounces of at least 999 fineness, \$0.005 an ounce outright (\$25) and \$0.001 for spreads; gold (GC) 100 troy ounces of at least 995 fineness, as one 100-ounce bar or three kilo bars, \$0.10 an ounce (\$10); aluminium 25 metric tons of P1020A, \$0.25 a ton (\$6.25) \citep[Rules 107102, 111101--111102, 112101--112102 and 113101--113102]{comex-metals-chapters-2026}.

LME: Copper Grade~A is refined electrolytic copper to BS EN~1978:2022 Cu-CATH-1, GB/T~467-2010 or ASTM B115-10 (2021) Grade~1, in full-plate cathodes of LME-listed brands, and High Grade Primary Aluminium is P1020A (revised January 2018) or GB/T~1196-2018 Al99.70, both in lots of 25 tonnes, 2 percent more or less \citep[Part~6, pp.~6-1 and~6-11]{lme-rulebook-2026-10}; minimum price fluctuations are ``at such levels as the Exchange may from time to time prescribe'' (Rule~11.3.7), so they are not in the rulebook and were not read.

\emph{Aluminium production by country}. USGS's \emph{Mineral Commodity Summaries} (February 2026) estimate world smelter production at 74.0 million tonnes in 2025 (72.8 in 2024), of which China 45.0 (44.0), about 61 percent in both years, India 4.2, Russia 3.9, Canada 3.3 and the United Arab Emirates 2.7; world year-end capacity 79.7 million tonnes \citep[Aluminum, p.~2]{usgs-mcs-2026-aluminum}; shares recomputed in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/execution-market-making/commodity-production-latency-checks.py}. Gliner's ``over 40 percent'' of production is therefore well below the current share; USGS gives no consumption by country, so his consumption share was not checked, and the LME's minimum price fluctuations remain unread (lme.com not retried; the LME notices that set them were not obtained). Gliner's contract sizes match these texts.

\qffield{Practice} The negative relation between gold and real rates is an empirical tendency with long episodes of breakdown; ``gold inversely correlated with real interest rates'' should carry that qualification.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Gliner writes that platinum ``is almost twice has [sic] heavy as gold'' with densities of 21 and 19 (p.~323); the ratio is about 1.11 (checks, section~14). He writes that Chile represents ``more than 40 percent of consumption'' of copper (p.~325), which cannot be right for Chile, a producer and exporter; it probably means China, which he calls the largest importer in the same paragraph, but the text does not say so; Chile at 27 percent of production and China at 43 percent of consumption were not matched to his table. ``India largest consumer (50\% used for weddings)'' compresses his statement about India's 2011 demand. ``China overtook South Africa as largest producer'' is stated by Gliner (p.~320).

\emph{Production shares checked against the data}. \emph{Gold}: USGS's \emph{Mineral Commodity Summaries 2013} gives 2011 and 2012 (estimated) mine output of 362 and 370 tonnes for China, 234 and 230 for the United States, 258 and 250 for Australia and 181 and 170 for South Africa, against world totals of 2,660 and 2,700 tonnes: 38.9 and 37.8 percent for the four \citep[gold, p.~2]{usgs-mcs-2013}, a little under Gliner's ``more than 40 percent'', whose source for that sentence is not stated (his Table~10.7 cites the World Gold Council, GFMS and the LBMA).

\emph{Silver}: Gliner's Table~10.9 (World Silver Survey 2013 and GFMS) is internally inconsistent. Its ounces (Mexico 162.2, China 117.0 million) sum, with Other 120.0, to its stated total of 787.0 million, on which Mexico and China are $279.2/787.0=35.5$ percent and the top five 62.6 percent; but its printed shares (27.3 and 19.7 percent, and 120.0 as 20.2 percent) imply a denominator near 594 and add to 132.5 percent; his ``47 percent'' and ``83 percent'' for the top five follow the printed shares. USGS's 2013 summary gives 2012 estimates of 4,250 tonnes for Mexico and 3,800 for China against a world total of 24,000, 33.5 percent \citep[silver, p.~2]{usgs-mcs-2013}, close to the 35.5 percent the table's own ounces imply.

The entry quotes Gliner as written and records the discrepancy rather than repairing it; the World Silver Survey itself was not read. \emph{Current shares} (USGS, \emph{Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025}, 2024 estimates): gold, China 380 of 3,300 tonnes, and China, Russia, Australia, Canada and the United States together 41 percent; silver, Mexico 6,300 and China 3,300 of 25,000 tonnes (38 percent); copper mine output led by Chile (5,300 of 23,000 thousand tonnes) and refined output by China (12,000 of 27,000) \citep[gold, silver and copper, pp.~1--2]{usgs-mcs-2025}; these are production shares, not the consumption shares previously quoted.

\begin{qfdefinition}[Agricultural markets for macro: corn, wheat, soybeans, cotton and coffee, crop calendars and sensitive stages, CBOT and ICE contracts, ethanol demand, and weather with the livestock cascade]\label{def:agricultural-markets}
\qffield{Statement} Gliner: agricultural supply depends on weather and planting, and ``some of the best agriculture traders have full-time meteorologists on staff''; crop calendars show the sensitive periods \citep[chap.~10, sec.\ ``Agriculture'', PDF p.~330]{s7-gliner}. \emph{Corn} (pp.~330--335): the United States produces over 35 percent and consumes over 30 percent of the world's corn and is the largest exporter; Iowa, Illinois and Nebraska produce 47 percent of U.S.\ corn; uses are feed, ethanol (27 percent of U.S.\ corn demand in 2011, after the Energy Policy Act of 2005) and food; Chinese feed imports rose from nothing in 2007 to over 5 percent of world imports; silking from June is the critical period; CBOT corn (C) is quoted in cents a bushel, 5,000 bushels a contract, 56 pounds a bushel, for March, May, July, September and December.

\emph{Cotton} (pp.~341--344): character, grade and staple; China, India and the United States 65 percent of production in 2013, China the largest producer and consumer, the United States the largest exporter with almost 30 percent; ICE cotton (CT) in cents a pound, 50,000 pounds, roughly 104 bales of 480 pounds. \emph{Coffee} (pp.~344--348): arabica about 60 percent of production, robusta the rest; Brazil the largest producer (37.2 percent in 2012--13) and Vietnam second (16.6 percent), in bags of 60 kilograms (Table~10.21); the United States and the European Union almost half of demand; ICE arabica (KC). \emph{Weather and livestock} (p.~333): when grain prices rise sharply, feeding livestock becomes expensive and farmers slaughter early, so near-term livestock prices fall while later prices rise.

\emph{Cotton} (pp.~341--344): character, grade and staple; China, India and the United States 65 percent of production in 2013, China the largest producer and consumer, the United States the largest exporter with almost 30 percent; ICE cotton (CT) in cents a pound, 50,000 pounds, roughly 104 bales of 480 pounds. \emph{Coffee} (pp.~344--348): arabica about 60 percent of production, robusta the rest; Brazil the largest producer (37.2 percent in 2012--13) and Vietnam second (16.6 percent), in bags of 60 kilograms (Table~10.21); the United States and the European Union almost half of demand; ICE arabica (KC).

\qffield{Notation} Futures month codes F (Jan), H (Mar), K (May), N (Jul), Q (Aug), U (Sep), V (Oct), X (Nov), Z (Dec); one bushel of corn 56 pounds, of wheat and soybeans 60.

\qffield{Conditions} Shares, contract terms and delivery months are Gliner's for 2012--2013 (USDA tables as he reproduces them); CBOT grain terms were checked against the December 2024 filing below; USDA data were not checked. A corn contract at 500 cents is \$25,000 notional (checks, section~14).

\qffield{Practice} Translate a view on a crop's balance sheet (\hyperref[def:contango-backwardation-stocks]{ending stocks}) into a specific contract month, grade and delivery location; the basis between local cash and futures is the pathway's subject.

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Unverified extensions}: El Ni\~no and La Ni\~na, NOAA drought monitors, ``demand relatively inelastic'', Russian wheat export bans, coffee's biennial cycle and 20-year tree life; plausible, but unchecked here. Two are now checked against ICE: Coffee ``C'' (KC) is 37,500 pounds, quoted in cents a pound, for March, May, July, September and December, physically delivered; Cotton No.~2 (CT) is 50,000 pounds net weight, for March, May, July, October and December, matching H, K, N, V, Z \citep[contract specifications, retrieved 2026-10-16]{ice-softs-specs}.

\emph{CBOT grain terms}. Each contract is 5{,}000 bushels with a minimum fluctuation of a quarter cent a bushel (\$12.50 a contract). Corn: No.~2 yellow at par, No.~1 at 1\textonehalf\ cents over and No.~3 at 2 to 4 cents under, in September, December, March, May and July. Soybeans: No.~2 yellow at par, No.~1 at 6 cents over and No.~3 at 6 cents under, in September, November, January, March, May, July and August. Wheat: No.~2 Soft Red Winter, Hard Red Winter, Dark Northern Spring and Northern Spring at par and No.~1 at 3 cents over, in July, September, December, March and May.

All three stop trading on the business day before the 15th calendar day of the delivery month, and daily price limits are reset twice a year at 7 percent of a 45-day average settlement, rounded to 5 cents with a 20-cent floor for corn, expanding by 1.5 times after a limit settlement \citep[Exhibit, Rules 10101--10104, 11101--11102 and 14101--14102]{cbot-24-443}. The filing itself raised load-out premiums on shipping certificates from 6 to 9 cents a bushel and the St.\ Louis--Alton corn and soybean differential from 16.25 to 24 cents over par, effective after the 2027 expiries.

The current Chapters 10, 11 and~14 (official PDFs downloaded through a browser on 2026-10-16, undated) agree with the reprint on these terms, and carry the location differentials in two schedules, for corn up to December 2027 and from March 2028, and for soybeans up to November 2027 and from January 2028 \citep[Rules 10101--10102, 10105, 11101--11102, 11105 and 14101--14102]{cbot-grain-chapters-2026}; no change after the January 2025 effective date was found in the terms above. Gliner's months for corn and soybeans match; his 56- and 60-pound bushels were not checked here. cotton contract size ``50,00 0 lbs'' is a typo for 50,000.
