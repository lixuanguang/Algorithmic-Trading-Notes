\section{Performance evaluation}

\begin{qfmethod}[Relative performance evaluation: total returns, market-adjusted and risk-adjusted returns, market timing, and smoothed returns]\label{meth:relative-performance-evaluation}
\qffield{Statement} Portfolio performance depends on management and on every factor that moves the prices of its holdings, most of which nobody can foresee, so good results may reflect skill or luck \citep[chap.~``Performance Evaluation and Prediction'', sec.~22.1, pp.~443--444]{alpha-harris}. Relative evaluations are therefore more valuable than absolute ones: an absolute evaluation computes the total return; a relative one compares it with a benchmark chosen to show how the portfolio would have done without active management (sec.~22.2, p.~445). Harris's measures (secs.~22.2.1--22.2.2.3, pp.~445--450):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{total return} over an interval, including income and paying out commissions, fees and margin flows; with capital added or withdrawn, either the \emph{internal rate of return}, approximately a time- and value-weighted average of the returns between flows, or the \emph{holding-period return} of one unit invested at the start with distributions reinvested, which differ when flows are not pro rata;
\item \emph{market-adjusted return}, the portfolio return minus a market index return;
\item \emph{risk-adjusted excess return} or realised alpha, the portfolio return minus the portfolio's beta times the market return, with the beta measured concurrently because it changes when the manager trades;
\item \emph{market-timing return}, beta times the market return minus the market return; so that
\end{itemize}
\[ r^p=\underbrace{(r^p-\beta r^m)}_{\text{excess return}}+\underbrace{(\beta r^m-r^m)}_{\text{market timing}}+\underbrace{r^m}_{\text{market}} \]
(p.~450). Harris notes that the decomposition generalises to other risk factors, such as size and growth.

\qffield{Conditions} Returns of illiquid assets depend on estimated values; managers who adjust them slowly, especially downwards, report smooth returns with positive serial correlation; the low volatility inflates $t$-ratios, and forced revaluation later produces large losses, an artificial peso problem \citep[same chap., box ``Smooth Sailing, Rapids Ahead'', p.~448, and sec.~22.5.2.1, pp.~469--470]{alpha-harris}. Holding-period returns compound: $+100$ percent then $-50$ percent is zero, not $+50$ percent (box, p.~447).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: transaction cost analysis applies the same idea over an order's horizon, subtracting beta times the index move from the arrival cost (\hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}); in both, the residual is not proof of skill.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Interpretation: Harris's realised alpha $r^p-\beta r^m$ omits the risk-free rate; the legacy regression form $r^p_t-r^f_t=\alpha+\beta(r^m_t-r^f_t)+u_t$ (Jensen's alpha) differs from it by $(1-\beta)r^f$, for example $-0.9$ percentage points at $\beta=1.3$ and $r^f=3$ percent (checked); the regression also estimates a constant beta, whereas Harris uses concurrent betas. The legacy decomposition $r^p=r^m+(r^p-\beta r^m)+(\beta-1)r^m$ is Harris's identity. The legacy list called the IRR ``money-weighted, useful when capital flows are controlled by the investor'' and added ``trade-weighted'' fill-quality measures; Harris calls the IRR approximately time- and value-weighted and does not discuss who controls flows, and fill quality belongs to transaction cost analysis. Return smoothing is in Harris a problem of fraudulent or careless valuation; the formal treatment the pathway asks for (\texttt{wide-getmansky-lo-makarov}) is unread.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis} (the execution analogue, owned by \texttt{execution-trading-costs}).

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Return measures: time-weighted and money-weighted returns, cumulative, geometric-mean and annualised returns, log returns, gross and net, real and leveraged returns]\label{def:return-measures}
\qffield{Statement} GIPS 2020 defines a \emph{time-weighted return} (TWR) as ``a method of calculating period-by-period returns that reflects the change in value and negates the effects of external cash flows'', and a \emph{money-weighted return} (MWR) as ``the return for a period that reflects the change in value and the timing and size of external cash flows'' \citep[Glossary, pp.~83 and~78]{wide-gips-2020}. An \emph{external cash flow} is capital entering or leaving a portfolio; dividend and interest income are not external cash flows (p.~75). For portfolios in composites, firms must value at least monthly and on the date of all large cash flows, calculate sub-period returns at large cash flows if daily returns are not calculated, with exceptions for private-market investment portfolios, and ``geometrically link periodic and sub-period returns'' (provisions 2.A.23--2.A.24, p.~11). When calculating money-weighted returns, firms must ``calculate annualized since-inception money-weighted returns'' using daily external cash flows (2.A.29, p.~12), but ``returns for periods of less than one year must not be annualized'' (2.A.12, p.~9); accordingly a composite money-weighted report presents the annualised since-inception return through the most recent annual period end, and, for a track record shorter than a full year, the \emph{non-annualised} since-inception return through the initial annual period end (5.A.1.a--b, p.~33). A \emph{gross-of-fees} return is ``reduced by any transaction costs'', and a \emph{net-of-fees} return is ``the gross-of-fees return reduced by investment management fees'' (Glossary, pp.~76 and~78).

\qffield{Notation} Interpretation, derived here: with sub-period returns $r_1,\dots,r_n$, each measured between external cash flows, the cumulative TWR is $R=\prod_{i=1}^n(1+r_i)-1$; the geometric mean per sub-period, for equal sub-periods, is $(1+R)^{1/n}-1$; and a return $R$ over $d$ days annualises mathematically to $(1+R)^{365/d}-1$. That formula is arithmetic; whether an annualised figure may be \emph{presented} is a separate rule, and under GIPS it may not be for periods shorter than a year (2.A.12). The MWR is the internal rate of return $x$ solving $\sum_t CF_t(1+x)^{-t}=0$, with contributions negative and withdrawals and the final value positive, from the investor's side. A continuously compounded (log) return is $\ln(1+R)$. A real return is $(1+r_{\mathrm{nom}})/(1+\pi)-1$ for inflation $\pi$. With own capital $V_0$ and borrowing $V_B$ at rate $r_B$ invested at return $r$ for one period, the return on own capital is $r_L=[r(V_0+V_B)-r_BV_B]/V_0=r+(V_B/V_0)(r-r_B)$.

\qffield{Conditions} The TWR removes the effect of the investor's timing of contributions and withdrawals, which suits judging a manager who does not control them; the MWR includes it, and GIPS allows firms to present it only when they control the external cash flows and the composite or pooled fund is closed-end, fixed-life, fixed-commitment or significantly illiquid (1.A.35, pp.~5--6). The IRR need not exist or be unique: the polynomial in $(1+x)^{-1}$ can have several positive roots when cash flows change sign more than once, or none. Checked: flows of $-1$, $+2.5$, $-1.54$ have IRRs of 10 and 40 percent; flows of $-1$, $+1$, $-1$ have none, since their net present value is at most $-0.75$. Annualising a short-period return assumes it would recur; GIPS forbids annualising returns for periods of less than one year (2.A.12, a calculation-methodology provision that also governs what is presented), and gives no reason; interpretation, that assumption is the obvious one. For periods of a year or more, annualising is a change of units. The leveraged formula assumes one period, borrowing at a fixed rate $r_B$ with no margin calls, fees or forced sales, and $r$ and $r_B$ measured over the same period.

\qffield{Practice} Checked: \$100 grows 10 percent to \$110, \$100 is added, and the \$210 falls 10 percent to \$189. The TWR is $1.10\times0.90-1=-1.0$ percent \emph{cumulative over both periods}, unaffected by the addition, a geometric mean of $-0.50$ percent per sub-period. The MWR solves $-100-100(1+x)^{-1}+189(1+x)^{-2}=0$, giving $x=-3.71$ percent \emph{per sub-period}, or $-7.29$ percent compounded over both. On matching horizons the MWR is lower, $-7.29$ against $-1.0$ percent over the two periods and $-3.71$ against $-0.50$ percent per sub-period, because more money was invested in the losing period. Sub-period returns of $+10$, $-10$ and $+5$ percent compound to 3.95 percent, a geometric mean of 1.30 percent per period. Four percent over 200 days annualises mathematically to 7.42 percent, a figure GIPS would not allow a firm to present (2.A.12). With 8 percent nominal and 3 percent inflation the real return is 4.85 percent, not 5. With $V_0=100$, $V_B=50$, $r=10$ and $r_B=4$ percent, $r_L=13$ percent. Harris's IRR and holding-period return, and the effect of non-pro-rata flows, are in \hyperref[meth:relative-performance-evaluation]{relative performance evaluation}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy time-weighted formula, $\sqrt[1/n]{\prod(1+HPR_i)}-1$, is malformed: the $(1/n)$-th root would raise the product to the power $n$. The geometric mean is $(\prod(1+HPR_i))^{1/n}-1$, and the TWR for the whole period is the product minus one, without the root; the sub-periods must be split at external cash flows. The legacy real return, $(1+r_{\mathrm{Real}})=(1+r_{\mathrm{Nom}})(1+r_{\mathrm{Risk}})/(1+\pi)$, has an unexplained ``risk'' factor that does not belong to an inflation adjustment and is not adopted. The legacy treated pre-tax and after-tax returns only as labels; after-tax returns depend on jurisdiction, investor and realisation timing and were not checked. The source of the legacy's return table is not recorded; its formulas were checked here, not against a source.
