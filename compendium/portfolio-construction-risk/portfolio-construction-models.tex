\section{Portfolio construction models: rules and optimisers}

\begin{qfmethod}[Rule-based position sizing: equal position, equal risk, alpha-driven and decision-tree weighting, with Narang's case against unequal weights]\label{meth:rule-based-position-sizing}
\qffield{Statement} Narang divides quantitative portfolio construction models into two families: \emph{rule-based} models, heuristics ``derived from human experience, such as by trial and error'', which can be ``exceedingly simple or rather complex''; and \emph{optimisers}, which search for the best value of an objective function \citep[chap.~6, ``Portfolio Construction Models'', pp.~99--100]{s7-narang} (the second family is in \hyperref[mod:mean-variance-portfolio-optimisation]{mean--variance optimisation}). He names four rule-based types (sec.~6.1, p.~100):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{equal position weighting}: every position the alpha model deems worth holding gets the same size; signal strength is ignored ``except insofar as the signal is strong enough to be worthy of a position at all'', and the instruments are implicitly assumed homogeneous in risk (sec.~6.1.1, pp.~100--101);
\item \emph{equal risk weighting}: sizes ``inversely to their volatilities (or whatever other measure of risk, such as drawdown, is preferred)'', so that each position is equalised ``by the amount of risk that the allocation contributes to the portfolio'' (sec.~6.1.2, p.~102);
\item \emph{alpha-driven weighting}: size grows with the forecast, under limits from the risk model on single positions and groups, for example 3 percent a position and 20 percent a sector (sec.~6.1.3, pp.~103--104);
\item \emph{decision-tree weighting}: ``a defined set of rules in a particular order to determine position sizing'', named on p.~100 only; the third edition has no section on it.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Why some quants weight equally.} Narang gives two problems with \emph{unequal} weighting, which the equal-weighters avoid (pp.~101--102). First, it assumes enough statistical power to forecast not only direction but also the relative size or probability of moves. Second, it leads to ``a few large bets on the `best' forecasts and many smaller bets on the less dramatic forecasts'', and so to excess idiosyncratic risk: the strongest momentum or reversal signals often come where prices have already moved most, sometimes on real information (his ``adverse selection bias''), and large forecasts can come from bad data, such as a price quoted in pence instead of pounds. Winsorising forecasts can partly address this; equal weights need no such adjustment. \emph{Equal risk weighting} relies on a backward-looking risk measure: his example is bank stocks, stable for a decade and then more volatile than many technology stocks in 2008 (p.~103). \emph{Alpha-driven weighting} relies on the alpha model being right about size as well as direction; trend followers using it ``can suffer sharp drawdowns relatively frequently'', holding the largest position as the trend reverses (p.~104). Costs can enter any rule, for example by setting a forecast to zero when it is below its expected transaction cost (sec.~6.1.4, p.~104; \hyperref[mod:portfolio-transaction-cost-function]{cost functions}). Liquidity can cap any weight (p.~102).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation, checked numerically: his Exhibit~6.1 (p.~102, page image) gives volatilities of 2.5 and 2.0 percent and ``volatility-adjusted'' weights of 39 and 61 percent. Those are inverse-\emph{variance} weights, $(1/2.5^2)/(1/2.5^2+1/2.0^2)=39.0\%$; inverse-volatility weights would be 44.4 and 55.6 percent, which give each stock the same $w_i\sigma_i$ (1.11) while the table's weights do not (0.98 and 1.22). Neither scheme in general equalises the contribution to \emph{portfolio} risk, $w_i(\Sigma w)_i/w^\top\Sigma w$, defined when the portfolio variance $w^\top\Sigma w$ is positive, and dependent on correlations. For two assets inverse-volatility weights do equalise it at any correlation, but not for more. With three assets of 20 percent volatility, where the first two correlate at 0.8 and the third is uncorrelated, equal (inverse-volatility) weights give risk contributions of 39, 39 and 22 percent. Equal contributions need weights of about 29.93, 29.93 and 40.15 percent, $a=1/(2+\sqrt{1.8})$ for the first two and $a\sqrt{1.8}$ for the third (checked). Narang's ``contributes to the portfolio'' therefore describes equal-risk-contribution weighting, which uses the full covariance matrix, more than the inverse-volatility rule he gives (stub \texttt{meth:equal-risk-contribution}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy block put Narang's second argument \emph{against} unequal weighting, ``few large bets on `best' forecast, many smaller bets'', under equal weighting, where it reads as a property of the equal-weight scheme. It describes what equal weighting avoids. The legacy decision-tree details (``constraints may include percentage limits'', ``model size grows dramatically'') are not in the third edition's chapter; they may come from an earlier edition (not checked). Narang's choice between rules is his observation: rule-based users are mostly futures traders with ``intrinsic'' forecasts, and optimiser users are mostly equity market-neutral traders with relative forecasts (sec.~6.4, p.~123). He gives no data for this.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:mean-variance-portfolio-optimisation]{mean--variance optimisation} (the other family); \hyperref[meth:signal-portfolio-allocation]{signal-portfolio allocation} (rule-based signal portfolios mixed by an optimiser); \hyperref[mod:portfolio-transaction-cost-function]{cost functions} (costs as inputs); \hyperref[def:common-investor-contagion]{common-investor contagion} (risk targeting under rising volatility).

\begin{qfmodel}[Mean--variance portfolio optimisation: objectives, inputs, constraints and solvers, and what the legacy claimed about them]\label{mod:mean-variance-portfolio-optimisation}
\qffield{Statement} Markowitz's problem, as written by Boyd, Johansson, Kahn, Schiele and Schmelzer, chooses the weights $w\in\mathbb R^n$ given expected returns $\mu$ and a covariance matrix $\Sigma$ in one of two forms \citep[author version of 5 Jan.\ 2024, sec.~1.1, eqs.~(1)--(2), pp.~4--5]{wide-boyd-markowitz-seventy}:
\[ \max_w\ \mu^\top w\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ w^\top\Sigma w\le\sigma_{\mathrm{tar}}^2,\ \mathbf 1^\top w=1;\qquad \max_w\ \mu^\top w-\gamma\,w^\top\Sigma w\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \mathbf 1^\top w=1. \]
Both trace the same trade-off curve of Pareto-optimal portfolios as $\sigma_{\mathrm{tar}}$ or $\gamma>0$ varies, and both have closed forms, though the first ``can be infeasible when $\sigma_{\mathrm{tar}}$ is too small''. The closed form of the second is $w^\star=\tfrac1{2\gamma}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu+\nu^\star\mathbf 1)$ with $\nu^\star=(2\gamma-\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu)/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$, the optimal dual variable of the budget constraint; its $\Sigma^{-1}$ hints at sensitivity to the inputs. Adding $w\ge0$ (long only) makes the second a quadratic programme (pp.~4--5). Interpretation, checked numerically: the closed form assumes $\Sigma$ positive definite, so that $\Sigma^{-1}$ exists and the objective is strictly concave with one maximiser, and $\gamma>0$. The term $\nu^\star\mathbf 1$ shifts every expected return by the same amount, chosen so that $\mathbf 1^\top w^\star=1$; with a three-asset example, $\gamma=1$ and $\gamma=5$, the weights sum to one and the first-order condition $\mu-2\gamma\Sigma w^\star+\nu^\star\mathbf 1=0$ holds to rounding. The first form is feasible only when $\sigma_{\mathrm{tar}}^2$ is at least the minimum attainable variance under the budget constraint, $1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$. A singular $\Sigma$, for example two perfectly correlated assets, has no inverse and leaves directions of zero variance along which the objective may be unbounded or the maximiser not unique. It needs separate treatment, such as a better-conditioned estimate or explicit constraints, chosen and justified for the problem; substituting a pseudoinverse into the formula is not a justified default (Interpretation). Maximising the Sharpe ratio is a third objective, also solvable by convex optimisation ``at least when the constraints are simple'' (sec.~3.2, p.~20). Narang describes the same model in words: inputs of expected returns, volatilities and a correlation (covariance) matrix; output the efficient frontier; a common objective of ``the highest possible return for a unit of risk''; and in practice also the portfolio's size in currency, a desired risk level ``usually measured in terms of volatility or expected drawdown'', and other constraints such as a hard-to-borrow list, which ``are not required by the optimizer'' \citep[chap.~6, sec.~6.2, pp.~104--106]{s7-narang}.

\qffield{Notation} $\mu$, $\Sigma$, $w$, $\gamma$ (risk aversion), $\sigma_{\mathrm{tar}}$ (target volatility) as in Boyd et al.; the Sharpe ratio $\mu^\top w/\sqrt{w^\top\Sigma w}$ (excess returns, no benchmark) is unchanged when $w$ is scaled by any positive number.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Which objective.} Mean--variance maximises return for a variance target, or return minus a variance penalty; maximising the Sharpe ratio selects one direction on the frontier (with cash and leverage, the tangency portfolio, Narang's Exhibit~6.7, p.~122, page image). These are different problems with different solutions. Expected drawdown is not part of the mean--variance model: Narang lists it among practical inputs that set a risk level (p.~105) and names a return-to-drawdown objective as a different choice (p.~124). The legacy ``desired risk level (volatility or expected drawdown)'' and ``maximising return of portfolio relative to volatility'' put both inside one model. \emph{Unconstrained optimisation.} Narang calls it ``a quirk of unconstrained optimizers'' that they ``often'' put everything in the instrument with the highest risk-adjusted return (sec.~6.2.2.1, p.~114); the legacy made this ``might''. It is not a property of mean--variance: the unconstrained solution is proportional to $\Sigma^{-1}$ terms and generally holds every asset, and his own unconstrained two-stock example has its maximum Sharpe ratio at about 85 percent CVX and 75 percent NFLX (p.~106; Exhibit~6.2, p.~107, page image). Interpretation: a single-asset answer arises when only expected return is maximised under a budget with no shorting, a bounded problem whose optimum is the asset with the highest expected return. Ignoring risk without such bounds does not give one asset: with shorting allowed and expected returns not all equal, the budget-constrained return $\mu^\top w$ is unbounded, since any direction $d$ with $\mathbf 1^\top d=0$ and $\mu^\top d>0$ can be scaled without limit (checked with an example). Box limits on each weight make it bounded again, with the solution at the limits. Because the Sharpe ratio is scale-free, the point he marks is one of a ray of equally good portfolios within his $\pm100$ percent search box (checked: $\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ and three times it have the same ratio).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Inputs.} Expected returns come from the alpha model; a sign-only forecast can be used by setting all positive and all negative forecasts equal (p.~108). For volatility Narang calls GARCH, with Bollerslev's 1986 paper, ``the most widely used such technique (particularly in equity trading)'', and adds that a simpler EWMA ``may perform just about as well'' (pp.~108--110); he cites no evidence for the prevalence. Boyd et al.\ list EWMA, dynamic conditional correlation and iterated EWMA as covariance estimators that work well in practice (sec.~3.4, p.~22). For correlations Narang shows instability: S\&P~500 against Nikkei~225 rolling one-year correlations from $+0.01$ to $+0.66$ (pp.~110--111, Exhibit~6.4 by page image), and XOM--CVX steadier than XOM--NFLX (Exhibit~6.5, p.~112), and says grouping similar instruments, ``in the definition of \emph{relative} in a relative alpha strategy and/or in the specification of the risk model'', improves stability (p.~111). \emph{Constraints} limit positions and groups, and can end up driving the portfolio: with 100 names and a 1.5 percent cap the best position is only 1.5 times the average (sec.~6.2.2.2, p.~114). Risk penalties can rise faster than linearly, and costs enter as per-stock or generic impact functions (pp.~114--115); Boyd et al.\ use a spread term plus a $\lvert z\rvert^{3/2}$ impact term (sec.~2.4, eq.~(10), p.~17), the form in \hyperref[mod:portfolio-transaction-cost-function]{cost functions}. Industry-neutral constraints can turn a positive forecast into a short, and the substitution effect can prefer a cheaper correlated stock (sec.~6.2.3, pp.~120--121). The output is a target portfolio; the trades are its difference from the current one (sec.~6.3, pp.~122--123). \emph{Solvers.} Convex formulations, including nondifferentiable terms, are solved reliably and fast; integer shares, cardinality limits and minimum position sizes make the problem nonconvex and much slower (Boyd et al., secs.~1.4 and~4.3, pp.~9--10 and 27--28). Narang's ``10{,}000 assets \dots\ in about one second'' (p.~120) matches Boyd et al.'s Table~2 only with a factor model: 0.65--0.89 seconds for 10{,}000 assets and 50--100 factors with one commercial solver (sec.~5.6, p.~40). \emph{Size limits} (slice~29). Narang's risk-model chapter classifies limits by how size is limited, how risk is measured and what is limited \citep[chap.~4, ``Risk Models'', sec.~4.1, pp.~73--76]{s7-narang}. A hard constraint draws a line, such as no position above 3 percent; because ``why is a 3.01 percent position so much worse?'', some quants use a penalty that lets a position pass the level only for a much larger expected return, increasingly hard the further past it (pp.~73--74). Boyd et al.\ describe the same device as softening: the hard constraint $f\le f_{\max}$ is removed and a penalty $\gamma(f-f_{\max})_+$ is subtracted from the objective, with $\gamma$ a priority parameter; softening preserves convexity, and limits that are strict or mandated, such as long-only or no borrowing, stay hard (sec.~4.2, pp.~25--27). A penalty therefore replaces a limit; it does not permit breaching a separate hard cap on the same quantity. Limit levels come from theory, set and adjusted by testing (his 5 percent limit revised to 3 percent), or from data (p.~74); tuning them on a backtest invites \hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{overfitting}. Limits can apply to positions, groups such as sectors, factor exposures (\hyperref[mod:factor-risk-model]{factor risk model}) and leverage (p.~75).

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Estimation error.} Boyd et al.\ agree that the basic method is sensitive to its inputs, and answer with regularisation and robust optimisation: long-only or leverage limits, Black--Litterman's shrinkage of the mean towards market-implied returns, and covariance shrinkage (sec.~1.3, pp.~8--9; sec.~3.4, p.~22). \emph{Black--Litterman.} Narang credits it with a default portfolio and a blend of ``an investor's expectations with a degree of confidence about those expectations with the historical precedent evident in the data'' (sec.~6.2.2.3, p.~117). In Boyd et al.'s account it acts on the expected returns, towards the market-implied return, not on correlations. The legacy ``adjusts historically observed correlation levels by utilising investor's forecast'' is in neither source and is not adopted. Black and Litterman's paper is unread (stub); Narang's note~5 dates it 1982, his text and the catalogue 1992. \emph{Resampled efficiency.} Narang presents Michaud's method as reordering the observed returns by Monte Carlo ``to reduce the estimation error inherent in the inputs'' (sec.~6.2.2.5, pp.~118--119). His own remark that average return and volatility ``remain the same across all these alternate histories'' concerns one series, the S\&P~500: reordering a single series leaves its mean and variance unchanged. That does not show that every optimiser input is unchanged. Interpretation, checked with a small example: applying one common permutation to the rows of a multivariate return sample also leaves its covariances unchanged, but permuting each asset's returns independently changes them; for $x=(1,2,3,4)$ and $y=(1,2,3,4)$, reversing $y$ alone keeps both means (2.5) and variances (1.25) but turns their covariance from $+1.25$ into $-1.25$. What Narang describes, then, does not settle what Michaud's procedure changes. His example concerns the robustness of a trading strategy, not optimiser inputs. What Michaud's procedure does and whether it improves out-of-sample results were not checked (stub). \emph{Machine learning.} The legacy ``data-mining approaches: supervised learning or genetic algorithms, as MVO involves searching many possible portfolios'' is not in the third edition. There Narang says ``we do not see many strictly `machine learning' models for portfolio construction'' and that the state of the art remains Markowitz-type optimisation (sec.~6.2.2, pp.~113--114). Interpretation: three tasks are being run together. \emph{Forecasting} estimates inputs such as expected returns or covariances, and supervised learning is commonly used for it. \emph{Learning allocation weights} maps data directly to portfolio weights by training a model on a portfolio objective; that is possible and has its own literature, which was not read here. \emph{Optimisation} chooses weights for given inputs and constraints; for a convex problem, solvers reach the optimum, so the size of the search space is no reason to use a generic heuristic such as a genetic algorithm, though such heuristics have a place in nonconvex problems. The legacy item's reason, that mean--variance optimisation ``involves searching many possible portfolios'', supports none of these, and the item has no support in the source read. Not adopted. \emph{Narang's details not adopted.} He describes convex functions as ``smooth and continuous'' with ``a single highest or lowest point'' (p.~120); convex functions can have kinks, such as $\lvert x\rvert$ or the costs above, and flat sets of minima. He calls GARCH a stochastic-volatility model and says a negative autoregression ``implies \dots\ trending behavior'' (pp.~108--110); neither is adopted. His 1{,}998{,}000 distinct correlations for 2{,}000 instruments (p.~113) should be $2000\times1999/2=1{,}999{,}000$ (checked). His grouping evidence is two pairs over one sample, not a test of a method; factor and shrinkage estimators are stubbed. \emph{Volatility and covariance (slice~29).} Narang names two ``generally accepted'' measures of risk: longitudinal volatility, and dispersion, the cross-sectional standard deviation of returns or the correlation among instruments, a higher dispersion making the market ``less risky'' (sec.~4.1.2, pp.~74--75). Interpretation: these are not alternatives. A covariance risk model contains both volatilities, on its diagonal, and correlations, off it, and a portfolio's variance $w^\top\Sigma w$ needs both; cross-sectional dispersion in one period is a different statistic from covariance over time, and a high reading can reflect large idiosyncratic moves rather than safety. His inference is not adopted. \emph{VaR-based leverage.} He describes VaR models as setting leverage inversely to measured risk, criticises the practice, and says ``most VaR models calculate what a daily single standard deviation move in portfolio returns will be'' (pp.~75--76). That last description is not adopted: VaR is usually defined as a loss quantile at a stated confidence level; its estimation belongs to \texttt{risk-tail-systemic}, whose readings are unread (stub there). The deleveraging mechanism is in \hyperref[def:common-investor-contagion]{common-investor contagion}.

\begin{qfmethod}[Signal-portfolio allocation: weighting simulated factor portfolios rather than positions, as Narang describes Grinold and Kahn's approach]\label{meth:signal-portfolio-allocation}
\qffield{Statement} Narang describes an optimiser ``directly aimed at building a portfolio of signals, whereas most optimizers try to size positions'', attributing it to Grinold and Kahn's \emph{Active Portfolio Management} \citep[chap.~6, sec.~6.2.2.4, pp.~117--118]{s7-narang}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item build one \emph{factor portfolio} per type of alpha forecast (momentum, value, growth), usually rule-based, ``very often equal-weighted or equal risk-weighted'';
\item simulate each historically to obtain a return series;
\item treat those series as the instruments of an optimiser, with the risk model, transaction cost model, portfolio size and risk target as inputs;
\item set each position as the sum, over factors, of its weight in the factor portfolio times that factor's signal times the factor portfolio's weight.
\end{enumerate}
His example: 100 equally weighted stocks, factor weights 60 and 40 percent, signals $+1$ and $-1$ for one company, give $1\%\times(+1)\times60\%+1\%\times(-1)\times40\%=+0.2\%$ (p.~118, page image; checked).

\qffield{Conditions} The number of factor portfolios is ``typically much more manageable, usually not more than about 20, corresponding to the number of individual factors in the alpha model'' (p.~117). That number is Narang's practitioner statement, with no source; it depends on how many signals a model has. The factor-portfolio returns are simulated, so the optimiser's inputs inherit the backtests' assumptions and their overfitting risk (\hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting}).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: the method trades a large position-level estimation problem for a small one over a handful of return series, ``an easier hurdle to clear in terms of the amount of data needed'' (p.~117). The position weights that result are linear in the factor weights, so position limits and costs at stock level are not enforced unless they are added back.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Whether this is Grinold and Kahn's own procedure, and in what form, was not checked: their book (\texttt{alpha-grinold}, a reading of the section overview) is unread. The legacy ``what is optimised is then a handful of factor portfolios'' and ``not more than 20'' repeat Narang's account as fact.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:mean-variance-portfolio-optimisation]{mean--variance optimisation}; \hyperref[meth:rule-based-position-sizing]{rule-based position sizing} (how each factor portfolio is weighted); \hyperref[meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics]{backtest evaluation diagnostics}.

\begin{qfmodel}[PCA risk-distribution weights: allocating a target variance across the principal components of a covariance matrix]\label{mod:pca-risk-distribution-weights}
\qffield{Statement} Given an $N\times N$ covariance matrix $V$ of an invariant (returns, yield changes, volatility changes) and a target distribution of risk $R$ across $V$'s principal components, López de Prado computes the allocation $\omega$ as follows \citep[chap.~2, sec.~2.4.2, p.~35]{alpha-afml}. Decompose $VW=W\Lambda$ with eigenvalues in descending order. For any $\omega$, with $\beta=W'\omega$ its projection on the orthogonal basis,
\[ \sigma^2=\omega'V\omega=\beta'\Lambda\beta=\sum_{n=1}^N\beta_n^2\Lambda_{n,n},\qquad R_n=\frac{[W'\omega]_n^2\Lambda_{n,n}}{\sigma^2},\qquad \sum_nR_n=1. \]
To deliver a chosen $R$ at risk $\sigma$, set $\beta_n=\sigma\sqrt{R_n/\Lambda_{n,n}}$ and $\omega=W\beta$; rescaling $\omega$ rescales $\sigma$ and keeps $R$ (p.~35). With no $R$ given, Snippet~2.1 puts all risk on the smallest-eigenvalue component, so $\omega$ is the last eigenvector scaled to $\sigma$ (p.~36).

\qffield{Notation} $\omega$ is in the units of the invariant (for example weights on returns); $\sigma$ is the target standard deviation (\texttt{riskTarget}); $R_n\ge0$.

\qffield{Conditions} López de Prado frames $V$ as the covariance of an IID multivariate Gaussian process with mean $\mu$ (p.~35); interpretation, the mean plays no part, and the construction needs only $V$. It needs $\Lambda_{n,n}>0$ for every component given risk: a zero eigenvalue cannot carry risk, and a near-zero one, where estimation error is largest, produces very large weights, which the snippet does not guard against. The decomposition does not fix signs: each $\beta_n$ with $R_n>0$ can be $\pm\sigma\sqrt{R_n/\Lambda_{n,n}}$, so with distinct eigenvalues $2^k$ allocations share one risk distribution, $k$ being the number of components given risk (two for the snippet's default); the snippet's positive roots, and the arbitrary signs of the computed eigenvectors, are conventions. With repeated eigenvalues the eigenvectors, and so the allocations, are not unique at all. The weights are those of an estimated $V$; with an estimated covariance, the lowest-variance component is the one most likely to be noise.

\qffield{Practice} Checked on a random positive-definite $4\times4$ matrix (\nolinkurl{afml-checks.py}, in the artefact folder of \texttt{statistical-learning-foundations}): the construction delivers the target $R=(0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4)$ and $\sigma$ exactly, and flipping the sign of one $\beta_n$ with $R_n>0$ leaves $R$ unchanged. López de Prado's Figure~2.2 contrasts an inverse-variance portfolio, whose risk is spread over nearly all components, with the PCA portfolio, whose risk is all in the lowest-variance component (pp.~35--36). In his chapter the weights feed the ETF trick for a spread or hedge.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes wrote $\sigma^2=(\Lambda^{1/2}\beta)'(\Lambda^{1/2}\beta)'$, with a stray transpose, and $R_n=[W'n]^2_n\Lambda_{n,n}\sigma^{-2}$ for $[W'\omega]_n^2$. Allocating all risk to the smallest eigenvalue is a hedging choice, not a return forecast; it says nothing about expected return.

\begin{qfmodel}[Black--Litterman: equilibrium returns implied by market weights, combined with views and their confidence into posterior expected returns]\label{mod:black-litterman}
\qffield{Statement} In Black and Litterman's mathematical appendix \citep[Goldman Sachs Fixed Income Research, October 1991, Appendix, points~1--8, pp.~37--38]{alpha-black}:
\begin{itemize}
\item excess returns are normal with covariance $\Sigma$, and the \emph{equilibrium} risk premiums are $\Pi=\delta\Sigma W$, with $W$ the market weights (bonds and equities by capitalisation, currencies by a universal hedging constant) and $\delta$ a proportionality constant (point~6);
\item the unobservable expected return $E[R]$ has a distribution ``proportional to a product of two normal distributions'': one centred at $\Pi$ with covariance $\tau\Sigma$, and one expressing $k$ views $PE[R]=Q+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon\sim N(0,\Omega)$ and $\Omega$ diagonal (point~7);
\item the result is normal with mean (point~8)
\[ \overline{E[R]}=\big[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1}+P'\Omega^{-1}P\big]^{-1}\big[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1}\Pi+P'\Omega^{-1}Q\big], \]
which is then used ``as the vector of expected excess returns'' in the optimiser.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} $n$ assets; $W$ market weights; $\Sigma$ the return covariance; $\tau$ a scalar for the uncertainty of the equilibrium mean; $P$ ($k\times n$) the view portfolios, $Q$ their expected returns, $\Omega$ the view variances (lower variance, more confidence).

\qffield{Conditions} The posterior mean is the precision-weighted combination of two normal sources. Own checks (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/portfolio-construction/batch50-black-litterman-check.py}), on a three-asset example:
\begin{itemize}
\item it equals the conditional mean of $E[R]$ given the views by Gaussian conditioning;
\item with no confidence in the views ($\Omega\to\infty$) it returns $\Pi$;
\item with certainty ($\Omega\to0$) it satisfies $P\overline{E[R]}=Q$;
\item a view on one asset moves correlated assets' expected returns. A 3-point view on asset~1 with 2-point standard error raises asset~2's by 1.77 points.
\end{itemize}
$\tau$ and $\Omega$ are inputs the appendix does not calibrate.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: the equilibrium gives the optimiser a neutral starting mean, so that portfolios without views are the market portfolio rather than the extreme positions an unconstrained optimiser produces from noisy means (\hyperref[mod:mean-variance-portfolio-optimisation]{mean--variance optimisation}, whose estimation-error pitfall cites this shrinkage via Boyd et al.).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy description, which ``blends investor expectations with a degree of confidence \dots\ and these with historical precedent evident in the data'' and ``adjusts historically observed correlation levels'', misdescribes the model on two counts. The prior is the market-implied equilibrium $\Pi=\delta\Sigma W$, not historical average returns. The formula changes the mean, not $\Sigma$, so correlations are not adjusted; correlations instead carry a view on one asset to the others. The copy read is the October 1991 Goldman Sachs research paper on a Duke University course page, not the 1992 \emph{Financial Analysts Journal} article that the catalogue lists; the two were not compared.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:mean-variance-portfolio-optimisation]{mean--variance optimisation}; \hyperref[meth:portfolio-covariance-estimation]{covariance estimation} (the other input); \hyperref[meth:forecast-scaling-ic]{forecast scaling} (another way of shrinking views towards zero).

\qffield{Further reading} He and Litterman's intuition paper and the 1992 \emph{FAJ} article, not read.

\qffield{Index} Black--Litterman; equilibrium returns; implied returns; reverse optimisation; views; view confidence; tau; Bayesian combination; posterior expected returns; universal hedging

Stability: draft. Currency: model of 1991--1992.
\end{qfmodel}
