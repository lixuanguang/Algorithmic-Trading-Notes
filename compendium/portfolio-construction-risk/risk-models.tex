\section{Risk models: factor exposures and their control}

\begin{qfmodel}[Factor risk model: factor returns, exposures, residuals and the factor covariance, with economic and statistical factors and their use in limiting unintended exposures]\label{mod:factor-risk-model}
\qffield{Statement} In Narang's framing a \emph{risk} is an exposure that accompanies the pursuit of alpha but is not intentionally forecast: ``risk exposures are those that are not intentionally sought out by the nature of whatever forecast the quant is making in the alpha model''. A risk model measures such exposures, highlights them and provides input to the portfolio construction model, which decides whether to accept, limit or eliminate each \citep[chap.~4, ``Risk Models'', pp.~71--73]{s7-narang}. Risk \emph{management}, in his words, ``is about the intentional selection and sizing of exposures to improve the quality and consistency of returns'' (p.~71). The factor model that underlies most risk models, as Boyd, Johansson, Kahn, Schiele and Schmelzer write it, decomposes the $n$ asset returns into $k$ factor returns and idiosyncratic returns \citep[author version of 5 Jan.\ 2024, sec.~3.3, eq.~(14), pp.~20--21]{wide-boyd-markowitz-seventy}:
\[ r=Ff+\epsilon,\qquad \Sigma=F\Sigma_fF^\top+D,\qquad \sigma^2=(F^\top w)^\top\Sigma_f(F^\top w)+w^\top Dw, \]
with $F$ the $n\times k$ loading (exposure) matrix, known when the portfolio is built, $f$ the factor returns with covariance $\Sigma_f$, and $\epsilon$ the idiosyncratic returns with diagonal covariance $D$ (positive entries), uncorrelated with $f$. The means of $\epsilon$ are the assets' alphas.

\qffield{Notation} $F$, $f$, $\Sigma_f$, $\epsilon$, $D$, $w$ as in Boyd et al.; $F^\top w$ is the portfolio's vector of factor exposures; $\sigma^2$ the portfolio variance. Usually $k\ll n$, so $\Sigma$ is low rank plus diagonal.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Assumptions.} The decomposition is exact only under its assumptions: factor and idiosyncratic returns uncorrelated, and idiosyncratic returns uncorrelated across assets ($D$ diagonal). Interpretation, checked numerically: under them, an equally weighted portfolio of $n$ assets with unit loading on one factor of 15 percent volatility and residuals of 30 percent has volatility 33.5, 17.8, 15.3 and 15.0 percent for $n=1$, 10, 100 and 1{,}000, so the residual part diversifies away and the factor part does not. This is what Narang means by systematic risks that ``cannot be diversified away'' (p.~77). A diagonal $D$, with bounded residual variances and equal weights, is sufficient for this, not necessary. What matters is how much residual covariance remains when it is summed over pairs (Interpretation, checked numerically). With a constant residual correlation of 0.2 between every pair and residual variance 0.09, equal-weighted residual variance is $0.09\,(0.2+0.8/n)=0.018+0.072/n$, which stays above 0.018 however large $n$ is: that common correlation behaves like an omitted factor. With the same correlation of 0.2 only within disjoint pairs of assets, it is $0.108/n$, which still vanishes as $n$ grows. In these examples correlation confined to small groups still diversifies, while a correlation shared by every pair leaves a floor. \emph{Two kinds of factor.} Theory-driven or economic factors are named in advance on an economic argument: for equities the market, sectors and market capitalisation; for bonds, interest rates (pp.~77--78). Boyd et al.\ give market, industry and style portfolios such as Fama--French for equities and yield-curve, rate and spread factors for bonds, which are ``interpretable''. Empirical or statistical factors come from historical returns by methods such as principal component analysis; apart from the first, ``which typically is close to the market return, these factors are less interpretable'' (Boyd et al., p.~20). Narang says the first principal component of Treasury yields usually corresponds to the level of rates, and in equities the market comes first and then sectors (p.~78). Interpretation: a principal component is defined only up to sign and can change order and composition from one sample to the next, so its economic label is an after-the-fact reading, not a property of the method. Narang notes that statistical models can be ``fooled'' into factors that do not persist or into spurious exposures (p.~78).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Uses} (pp.~72 and~82--83): measuring which systematic exposures a portfolio takes and how large they are; explaining profits and losses; and, inside an alpha model, forecasting the residuals of returns against the risk model's factors. \emph{Limiting exposures.} Narang's general rule is ``to eliminate any unintentional exposures, since there should be no expectation of being compensated sufficiently for accepting them'' (p.~76); his examples are a value strategy's incidental sector and small-cap bets (pp.~72 and~77) and a view that CVX would beat XOM, expressed as a long-only CVX trade that also bets on the market, which buying CVX and shorting an equivalent amount of XOM ``substantially eliminated'' (p.~76). Interpretation: equal dollar amounts hedge the market exactly only if the two stocks have equal market betas; otherwise the pair keeps a net market exposure of the beta difference times the dollar size, so the hedge is approximate, and exact neutrality needs beta-weighted sizes under a stated risk model. Exposure limits, such as a $\pm5$ percent net market exposure, apply to risks the alpha model does not forecast (p.~75). He prefers, ``to the extent possible'', to build exposure decisions into the alpha model's bet structure, for example forecasting stocks relative to their sectors, rather than relying on one risk model in an optimiser (pp.~76--77 and~80--81). Boyd et al.\ express neutrality to factor $i$ as zero covariance of the portfolio with that factor, $(\Sigma_fF^\top w)_i=0$, a linear constraint (sec.~3.3, eq.~(15), p.~21). Interpretation, checked numerically: with correlated factors this differs from zero exposure, $(F^\top w)_i=0$. For three assets with loadings $(1,0.5)$, $(1,-0.5)$, $(0.8,1)$, factor variances 0.04 and 0.01 and factor covariance 0.012, the weights $(0.5,-0.5,0)$ have exposures $(0,0.5)$ but a covariance of 0.006 with the first factor. \emph{Choosing a model.} Most quants use theory-driven models for their interpretability; empirical ones promise adaptiveness but lag a regime change and trade adaptiveness against estimation error. With two years of daily data each new day changes two of about 520 observations, while shorter windows lose statistical power. Intraday data is one partial remedy. The two kinds can be combined, and commercial models (BARRA, Northfield, Qontigo/Axioma, Wolfe) are mostly theory-driven and for equities (pp.~78--80). Boyd et al.\ add that a factor model can estimate a large covariance better than direct estimation and cuts the solve cost from $O(n^3)$ to $O(nk^2)$ (p.~21).

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{Risk model against decision.} The legacy opening, ``risk model concerns the intentional selection and sizing of exposures'', moves to the model what Narang says of risk \emph{management}; in his black-box schematic (Exhibit~4.1, p.~83, page image) the risk model is an input to the portfolio construction model, which selects and sizes. \emph{Factor, exposure, signal or premium?} The legacy listed market, size, value and momentum as ``the most common factors'' of a risk model, with size and value described as risks. That list is not in Narang's risk chapter, which names market, sector and market-capitalisation risk; value is his example of an alpha (pp.~71--72), and whether such a factor is a risk to limit, a signal to forecast or a premium expected to be paid depends on the strategy's forecasts, not on the factor. The same factor can play several roles. \emph{Unintended is not the same as uncompensated.} Narang's rule is that ``there should be no expectation of being compensated sufficiently'' for unintentional exposures (p.~76): not that they earn nothing, but that any expected reward is not enough for the risk. Whether a given exposure is compensated, and by how much, is an empirical question about risk premia, not settled by its being unintended (Interpretation; not checked against the factor-premia readings). \emph{Empirical, not adaptive by default.} Updating an empirical model with new data is not evidence that its forecasts improve; Narang himself describes the lag and the loss of power (p.~79). The legacy ``empirical risk models \dots\ used by statistical arbitrage traders'' repeats his ``most commonly found among'' (p.~78) as a definition.

\begin{qfmethod}[Estimating a large covariance matrix: why the sample matrix fails when assets are many relative to observations, and linear and nonlinear shrinkage]\label{meth:portfolio-covariance-estimation}
\qffield{Statement} Ledoit and Wolf's review starts from the case ``when the number of variables is of the same magnitude as the number of observations'', where ``the sample covariance matrix certainly will not do'' \citep[UZH ECON WP~323, version of February 2020, abstract, p.~1]{wide-ledoit-wolf-review}. \emph{Linear shrinkage} to the identity solves $\min_{\rho_1,\rho_2}E\|\hat\Sigma-\Sigma\|_F^2$ over $\hat\Sigma=\rho_1I+\rho_2S$, with the scaled Frobenius norm $\|A\|_F^2=\operatorname{Tr}(A'A)/N$ (eqs.~(2.1)--(2.5), pp.~4--5). The solution is
\[ \Sigma^*=\gamma^*\mu I+(1-\gamma^*)S,\qquad \gamma^*=\frac{\beta^2}{\delta^2}, \]
with $\mu=\langle\Sigma,I\rangle$ the average variance, $\alpha^2=\|\Sigma-\mu I\|_F^2$, $\beta^2=E\|S-\Sigma\|_F^2$ and $\delta^2=E\|S-\mu I\|_F^2=\alpha^2+\beta^2$ (eqs.~(2.6)--(2.7), p.~5). It is a convex combination, positive definite and invertible ``even when $N>T$''. The optimal intensity depends on unknown population quantities, so a feasible estimator is derived under large-dimensional asymptotics, with $N/T\to c$ (sec.~2.2, p.~6).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Other targets.} Linear shrinkage can also target the single-factor matrix or the constant-correlation matrix.
\item \emph{Their own comparison.} In their 2004 back-test of a tracking-error-constrained manager, the single-factor target, the constant-correlation target and a five-principal-component factor model all ``dominated the sample covariance matrix, but there was no clear winner'': constant correlation was best for $N\le100$ and the single factor for $N\ge225$ (sec.~3.3, p.~14).
\item \emph{Nonlinear shrinkage} keeps the sample eigenvectors and shrinks each eigenvalue differently. It ``can deliver another level of performance improvement, especially if overlaid with'' time-varying co-volatility or factor models (abstract; secs.~4--6).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} $N$ assets, $T$ observations, $S$ the sample covariance, $c=N/T$ the concentration; the norm divides by $N$, which is not the standard Frobenius norm.

\qffield{Conditions} Section~2 assumes i.i.d., mean-zero observations; demeaned data and dynamic (DCC) and factor extensions are treated later (Remark~2.1; secs.~5--6). Optimality is in Frobenius loss, which is not the same as the out-of-sample variance of an optimised portfolio, though the review's applications are mostly Markowitz problems.

\qffield{Practice} Own simulation with a known one-factor population of 100 assets and 120 observations (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/portfolio-construction/batch50-shrinkage-check.py}):
\begin{itemize}
\item $\alpha^2+\beta^2=\delta^2$ holds (0.2035 against 0.2031);
\item the oracle intensity is 0.045, and the Frobenius loss falls from 0.00910 to 0.00871;
\item the true variance of the global minimum-variance portfolio falls from 0.0252 using $S$ to 0.0089 using $\Sigma^*$, against an optimum of 0.0044.
\end{itemize}
The small loss gain and the large portfolio gain illustrate that the two criteria differ. Narang's grouping of similar instruments (\hyperref[mod:factor-risk-model]{factor risk model}) is a structured target of this kind, without a rule for the intensity.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The feasible intensity's formula and the nonlinear estimator were not transcribed; the working-paper version read may differ from the 2022 \emph{Journal of Financial Econometrics} version. Connor, Goldberg and Korajczyk's book on factor-model estimation (\texttt{wide-connor-goldberg-korajczyk}) and the EWMA and DCC estimators listed by Boyd et al.\ were not read.

\qffield{Further reading} \texttt{wide-connor-goldberg-korajczyk}.

\begin{qfmethod}[Estimating VaR and ES: historical simulation, filtered historical simulation, parametric dynamic models and Monte Carlo as overlapping choices, and the coverage backtest]\label{meth:var-es-estimation-methods}
\qffield{Statement} In Christoffersen's textbook \citep[2nd ed., 2012]{wide-christoffersen-elements} the methods are treated as layers, not as exclusive categories:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Historical simulation} (HS) applies today's holdings to past prices. It assumes ``the distribution of tomorrow's portfolio returns \dots\ is well approximated by the empirical distribution of the past $m$ observations'' (chap.~2, sec.~2.1, PDF pp.~34--35). His conclusion is that ``HS is problematic for computing VaR'', which motivates dynamic models (chap.~2, overview). Weighted HS, which weights recent observations more, is an extension.
\item \emph{Filtered historical simulation} (FHS) ``combines model-based methods of dynamic variance, such as GARCH, with model-free methods of distribution''. It scales the empirical distribution of standardised returns by the model's current volatility (chap.~6, PDF p.~134).
\item \emph{Monte Carlo} and FHS are both used to simulate a dynamic risk model forward, for VaR and ES at horizons longer than a day, where closed forms are lacking. Monte Carlo ``relies on artificial random numbers'' from the model's assumed distribution, FHS ``uses historical random shocks'' (chap.~8, overview, PDF p.~179).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Backtesting.} With $I_{t+1}$ the indicator of a VaR violation at coverage rate $p$, $T_0$ and $T_1$ the numbers of non-violations and violations, the unconditional coverage test is
\[ LR_{uc}=-2\ln\big[(1-p)^{T_0}p^{T_1}\big/\big((1-T_1/T)^{T_0}(T_1/T)^{T_1}\big)\big]\sim\chi^2_1, \]
rejected at 10 percent above 2.7055. An independence test and a combined conditional-coverage test follow (chap.~13, PDF pp.~303--304).

\qffield{Notation} VaR at coverage $p$: the loss exceeded with probability $p$; ES: the expected loss given that VaR is exceeded; $m$ the HS window.

\qffield{Conditions} HS needs no distributional assumption but assumes the window's returns are draws from tomorrow's distribution: a stationary, independent sample over $m$ days. FHS needs a correct variance model and i.i.d.\ standardised returns. Monte Carlo needs a fully specified distribution. The $\chi^2$ backtest is asymptotic and assumes independent violations under the null.

\qffield{Practice} Own synthetic check: the script is \nolinkurl{batch50-var-backtest-check.py} in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/risk-tail-systemic/}. Returns are simulated from a GARCH(1,1) with Student-$t$ shocks over 20{,}000 days, and 1 percent HS and FHS VaR are backtested, both on a 250-day window.
\begin{itemize}
\item HS violations occur on 1.62 percent of days, with $LR_{uc}=66$. Their probability the day after a violation is 5.8 percent: they cluster.
\item FHS violations occur on 1.38 percent of days, with $LR_{uc}=25$, the 250-day empirical 1-percent quantile being imprecise. They do not cluster after a violation.
\end{itemize}
The legacy claim that the methods are ``useful for dynamically updating risk assessments'' therefore needs this kind of evidence: forecasts with better coverage and independence. That a method can be updated is not enough. Here the evidence is only synthetic, in a world where the GARCH model is true.
