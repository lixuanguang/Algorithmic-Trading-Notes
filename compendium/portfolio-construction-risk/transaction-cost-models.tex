\section{Transaction cost models as inputs to portfolio construction}

\begin{qfmodel}[Transaction cost functions for portfolio construction: flat, linear, piecewise-linear and quadratic forms, with units and size conventions]\label{mod:portfolio-transaction-cost-function}
\qffield{Statement} Narang's transaction cost model quantifies ``the cost of making a trade of a given size'' so that the portfolio construction model can weigh it against the alpha and risk models when choosing the portfolio to hold \citep[chap.~5, ``Transaction Cost Models'', p.~85]{s7-narang}. It is ``not designed to minimize the cost of trading, only to inform the portfolio construction engine''; minimising cost is the execution algorithm's job (p.~86), and cost is reduced in two phases, first by the portfolio construction model using the cost estimate and then by execution (sec.~5.3, p.~96). In his framing there are ``only two reasons to make a trade'', a better reward from the alpha model or a lower risk from the risk model, and a trade's benefit must ``clear the hurdle of the cost of transacting'' (p.~85). He names four basic forms, each describing the total cost of a trade against its size, with commissions and fees as the baseline (sec.~5.2, pp.~91--96, Exhibits~5.1--5.4 by page image):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{flat}: the same cost whatever the size; reasonable when the size traded is nearly always about the same and liquidity is fairly constant, ``rarely correct'' otherwise (pp.~92--93);
\item \emph{linear}: cost rising with a constant slope in size, overestimating at small sizes and underestimating at large ones in his exhibit (pp.~93--94);
\item \emph{piecewise-linear}: linear in ranges, with a steeper line beyond each breakpoint; ``rather popular among quants as a happy medium between simplicity and accuracy'' (p.~94);
\item \emph{quadratic}: cost rising ever faster with size; in his words the most ``accurate'' of the four in the exhibits and the most computationally intensive (p.~95).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} Interpretation: state the units and the size convention. Let $q$ be the signed trade size in shares (contracts, or currency), $P$ the price, $V$ a volume scale such as average daily volume, and $C(q)\ge0$ the \emph{total} cost of the trade in currency. The \emph{per-unit} cost is $c(q)=C(q)/\lvert q\rvert$ in currency per share, or $c(q)/P$ as a fraction of price (times $10^4$ for basis points). The forms, with $a$ in currency, $b$ in currency per share and $k$ in currency per share squared, are:
\[ C_{\mathrm{flat}}=a\ \text{if }q\neq0\ (0\text{ otherwise}),\qquad C_{\mathrm{lin}}=b\lvert q\rvert, \]
\[ C_{\mathrm{pwl}}=\max_i\bigl(b_i\lvert q\rvert-d_i\bigr)\ \ (b_1<b_2<\dots),\qquad C_{\mathrm{quad}}=b\lvert q\rvert+k q^2. \]
A total cost linear in size is a constant per-unit cost; a quadratic total cost is a per-unit cost linear in size, $c(q)=b+k\lvert q\rvert$. A per-share commission of, say, \$0.001 (Narang's example, p.~87) is therefore a linear, not a flat, cost; only a charge per ticket is flat. A per-unit cost proportional to $(\lvert q\rvert/V)^{1/2}$, as in \hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{the square-root law}, is a total cost proportional to $\lvert q\rvert^{3/2}$. Numerical illustration (own, checked): with a per-share cost of $0.5\times2\%\times(q/V)^{1/2}$ of a \$50 price and $V=10^6$ shares, 10{,}000 shares cost 10~bp per share, \$500 in total; 20{,}000 shares cost 14.1~bp, \$1{,}414; doubling the size multiplies the per-share cost by $2^{1/2}$ and the total by $2^{3/2}\approx2.83$. Using the peak-impact law as a cost scale is a simplification: \hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{the evidence entry} gives an execution shortfall per unit of about two-thirds of peak impact.

\qffield{Conditions} The ranking in the legacy block is not an established result. Narang's exhibits are schematic, with unlabelled axes and no data; the ``true'' cost curve in each is drawn convex and starting at zero (Exhibits~5.1--5.4, pp.~93--95, page images), although his text puts fixed charges under it as a floor (p.~91). Against an assumed convex curve, the closeness of each form follows from the shape drawn, not from estimation. His own summary is conditional: simpler models suit traders whose sizes are roughly constant or small, more complex ones those who may trade significant or variable quantities quickly, and ``any of the four models described here can be valid in the right set of circumstances'' (sec.~5.3, p.~96). His statement that a quadratic shape ``is generally accepted by the quant community'' (p.~92) is unsourced, and his note~2 qualifies it: ``there is good evidence that, at least for U.S. equities, cost per share scales with an exponent of 1.5, or in other words, as the square root of trade size'' (n.~2, p.~97, page image). That sentence conflates two conventions: a per-share cost scaling as the square root of size is a total cost with exponent 1.5 (Notation). Both a quadratic and a 3/2-power total cost are convex, so ``the cost gets higher ever more quickly'' as size grows (p.~92) does not distinguish them; which exponent fits is an empirical question owned by \texttt{price-impact-execution} (\hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{square-root impact}, \hyperref[meth:almgren-impact-estimation]{direct estimation}, whose fitted temporary-impact exponent of 0.6 per unit implies, at a fixed duration, a total exponent of about 1.6).

\qffield{Practice} Narang describes models built separately for each instrument, ``highly empirical'', evolving with the quant's own recorded executions and refreshed to keep up with market conditions; a less liquid small-cap stock costs more to trade than a mega-cap (pp.~91--92 and~96). His Exhibit~5.4 contrasts the expected cost, specified before trading, with what is ``observed empirically after trading''; the differences come from changes in liquidity, volatility and the mix of traders (p.~95). Interpretation: the realised cost observed after trading is measured against a benchmark price, and what is observed is a cost, not the impact component; how ex-ante cost forecasts are tested against realised costs is in \hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}. Interpretation, not from the sources read: inside an optimiser the cost enters the objective jointly with the alpha forecast and the risk penalty, so a trade is not tested against a separate hurdle one by one; and trades can also be forced by flows, margin or constraints, which Narang's two reasons leave out. The cost of underestimating or overestimating costs (too much turnover, or positions held too long, p.~86) is the subject of \hyperref[pit:backtest-cost-short-assumptions]{backtest cost and short assumptions}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} \emph{No universal ranking of accuracy or computation.} The legacy ``better estimate'', ``better accuracy than flat or linear models'' and ``most computationally intensive, but also most accurate'' repeat Narang's comparisons with his drawn curve as if they held generally; they are not adopted. Which form is most accurate depends on the instrument, the size range traded and the true shape, which Narang's own note~2 says may not be quadratic. Computational cost depends on how the form enters the optimiser, not on how many terms it has. Interpretation, not checked against the pathway's readings: a convex piecewise-linear cost can be written with linear constraints, a quadratic cost fits a quadratic programme, and a 3/2-power cost is convex but not quadratic; a flat cost per trade, the ``simplest'' form, is discontinuous at zero and needs integer variables to switch trades on and off. Narang's own wording is hedged (``this perhaps more accurate one is more computationally intensive'', n.~2, p.~97). \emph{Units.} A cost function stated without its convention (total or per unit; shares, currency or fraction of volume) cannot be compared with an impact law; say which. \emph{Benefit and cost.} The trade-off is between expected benefit and expected cost over the same horizon; comparing a forecast return with a cost in different units or horizons misstates the hurdle.
