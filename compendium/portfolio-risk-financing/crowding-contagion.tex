\section{Crowding and common-investor contagion}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Common-investor (contagion) risk: crowded positions and forced exits through what else their holders own, with Narang's account of August 2007]\label{def:common-investor-contagion}
\qffield{Statement} Contagion, or common-investor, risk is the risk a strategy bears ``not because of the strategy itself but because other investors hold the same strategies'' \citep[chap.~``Risks Inherent to Quant Strategies'', sec.~10.4, p.~202]{s7-narang}. Narang splits it in two:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{crowding}: how many others hold the same positions;
\item the \emph{ATM effect}: what else those holders own that could force them out. When highly levered investors take losses on an illiquid holding and face margin calls, they sell what they can, the liquid strategy, to raise cash. Losses in one strategy thus cause the liquidation of an unrelated one (p.~202).
\end{itemize}
His examples are August 1998, in which LTCM's losses on Russian debt forced it to sell positions such as Royal Dutch against Shell, whose spread widened from 8--10 percent to over 20 percent (pp.~202--203), and August 2007 (sec.~10.4.1, pp.~203--209).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Narang's account of August 2007, a practitioner's explanation.} He names four drivers (pp.~203--205):
\begin{enumerate}
\item the size and popularity of quant long/short equity strategies from 2004 to 2007. He estimates ``likely'' hundreds of billions of capital and about \$1 trillion of gross positions, mostly in large-cap U.S. stocks; despite diverse models, the overlap was enough to crowd individual trades;
\item weak returns before August, with value lagging growth from about the end of May 2007, and multi-strategy and proprietary desks that cut whatever had done poorly;
\item the practice, ``in my view, a critically important one'', of banks' proprietary desks and multi-strategy funds cross-collateralising strategies explicitly or implicitly. When credit strategies lost heavily in July 2007, the liquid quant books were sold to raise cash;
\item risk targeting: leverage set inversely to measured risk, usually VaR. Falling volatility from 2003 to 2006 called for one and a half to two times more leverage, so a four-times gross strategy became six or eight times. Higher returns were also sought by levering up. When volatility and correlations jumped together in late July 2007, VaR rose and many players had to cut leverage at once.
\end{enumerate}
In his sequence, multi-strategy funds and desks began deleveraging the most liquid book, U.S. quant long/short. Statistical arbitrageurs first supplied liquidity, then took losses and joined the selling. By 8--9 August the losses had spread across strategies and to Japan, while U.S. indices were roughly flat for the first ten days of August (pp.~205--209). He calls the losses ``solely the result of market impact'' (p.~208), reports losses of $-5$ to $-45$ percent at funds (p.~208), and concludes that ``for the first time, crowding became a risk of quant trading strategies'' (p.~209). Phrases such as ``it is likely'', ``it appears'' and ``in my view'' mark these as his inferences: the chapter gives no data on positions, leverage or flows.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: the entry separates two channels. In \emph{common positions}, crowding, the same trades are held by many, so any one holder's exit moves the others' prices. In \emph{common funding}, the ATM effect, an unrelated loss forces a holder to sell. Cross-collateralisation is what joins the second channel to a liquid strategy, and volatility targeting can make many holders sell at the same moment. The same mechanics in arbitrage, where staying power fails under leverage, are in \hyperref[res:arbitrage-risks]{arbitrage risks}. Leverage and margin terms are in \hyperref[mech:futures-margin-settlement]{futures margin and settlement} and \hyperref[def:securities-lending-fee-rebate]{lending fees and rebates}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The account is one practitioner's reconstruction. It is not causal evidence that the four drivers produced the losses, nor a measure of their weights; the pathway's evidence readings (stubs) are unread. Narang's Pulte Homes illustration (Exhibit~10.6, p.~206, checked by page image) does not support all its words. By the table, the stock fell 22.0 and then 12.5 percent (31.75 percent together) and then rose 15.6 percent, which recovers about a third of the lost price, not ``about half''. Over his 44-day and four-day periods that is roughly four to five times the earlier pace, not ``about 20 times''. He also calls the episode ``an interesting illustration of the quadratic nature of market impact'' (p.~206). Interpretation: the table cannot test that. Aggregate returns and average daily volume over a crisis period do not identify a metaorder impact exponent: they give no metaorder sizes or execution schedules, the volume mixes many traders' buying and selling, and the price that would have prevailed without the liquidation is not observed. The illustration is therefore evidence neither for a quadratic nor against the square-root law for metaorders (\hyperref[ev:square-root-impact]{square-root impact}), which is estimated from identified metaorders. His ``as much as 15 percent in market impact'' on 9 August is likewise unsourced in the chapter. A separate question stays open in \texttt{backtest-inference}'s stub \texttt{ev:signal-decay-crowding}: whether faster decay of a delayed signal identifies crowding. Common positions and forced liquidation, the subject here, do not answer it. The legacy ``quant liquidation criss'' is the 2007 crisis; its four causes match Narang's list.

\begin{qfmodel}[Market liquidity and funding liquidity: the margin constraint, destabilising margins, and the margin and loss spirals of Brunnermeier and Pedersen]\label{mod:margin-liquidity-spiral}
\qffield{Statement} Speculators who supply immediacy to customers arriving at different times must finance positions by collateralised borrowing, and ``the total margin on all positions cannot exceed a trader's capital'' \citep[RFS Advance Access version of 10 December 2008, p.~1]{exp-brunnermeier-pedersen-liquidity}:
\[ \sum_j\big(x_t^{j+}m_t^{j+}+x_t^{j-}m_t^{j-}\big)\le W_t, \qquad W_t=W_{t-1}+(p_t-p_{t-1})'x_{t-1}+\eta_t \quad\text{(eqs.~(4)--(5), p.~9)}, \]
with financiers setting each dollar margin so that an adverse price move larger than it has probability $\pi$, a value-at-risk rule (eqs.~(6)--(7), p.~9). Market illiquidity is the gap between price and fundamental value; funding liquidity is the speculators' shadow cost of capital (p.~2). Margins are \emph{destabilising} if they can rise with illiquidity, which happens when financiers cannot tell fundamental news from liquidity shocks and volatility is time-varying (p.~2; Proposition~3, p.~13). Then two \emph{liquidity spirals} amplify a shock to speculator capital (sec.~3.2, pp.~18--19): a \emph{margin spiral}, in which lower liquidity raises margins, tightening funding and lowering liquidity further; and a \emph{loss spiral}, in which speculators holding a position opposite to the customers' demand pressure lose as prices move away from fundamentals and must sell more. In a stable illiquid equilibrium with customer selling pressure the price sensitivity to a wealth shock is
\[ \frac{\partial p_1}{\partial\eta_1}=\frac{1}{\frac{2}{\gamma(\sigma^2)^2}m_1^+ +\frac{\partial m_1^+}{\partial p_1}x_1-x_0}\quad\text{(eq.~(24), Proposition~5(ii), p.~18)}; \]
a margin spiral arises if $\partial m_1^+/\partial p_1<0$ (margins rise as prices fall), and a loss spiral if the speculators' previous position is opposite to the demand pressure, $x_0Z_1>0$; together the spirals' effect ``is greater than the sum of their separate effects'' (p.~19).

\qffield{Notation} $x_t^{j\pm}$ long and short holdings of asset $j$; $m_t^{j\pm}$ dollar margins per share; $W_t$ speculator capital, $\eta_t$ an independent wealth shock; $Z_1$ the demand shock of customers present at time~1; $\gamma$ customers' risk aversion and $\sigma^2$ the fundamental volatility at time~2 in the model's notation; $p_1$ the time-1 price. Signs: with $Z_1>0$ customers are selling.

\qffield{Conditions} Three trading dates before a time-3 payoff; risk-neutral speculators, CARA customers who arrive sequentially with probability $a$; fundamentals follow $v_{t+1}=v_t+\sigma_{t+1}\varepsilon_{t+1}$ with ARCH-type volatility $\sigma_{t+1}=\bar\sigma+\theta\abs{\Delta v_t}$ (eqs.~(1)--(2), p.~8); speculators cannot raise new capital before time~2, ``slow moving capital'' (p.~9). The mechanism of eq.~(24): with constant margins and no inventory a \$1 wealth loss cuts the position by $1/m_1$ and moves the price by $1/(2m_1/(\gamma(\sigma^2)^2))$; writing the denominator as $k-l$, the expansion $1/(k-l)=1/k+l/k^2+l^2/k^3+\cdots$ has one term per loop around the spiral, and the effect is amplified when $l>0$ (p.~19). Own check of that expansion and of the super-additivity on illustrative numbers: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/funding-crowding/batch40-spiral-checks.py} (an illustration, not a calibration). Spirals can also start from shocks to liquidity demand, fundamentals or volatility (p.~19). With destabilising margins or large existing positions there can be multiple equilibria, so liquidity is \emph{fragile} and can dry up discontinuously (p.~3; Proposition~4, p.~14).

\qffield{Practice} Implications the authors draw (pp.~2 and~5): speculators' mark-to-market capital and volatility are state variables for market liquidity and risk premiums; a loss of capital reduces liquidity more when capital is already low and for high-margin securities; across assets the ratio of illiquidity to margin is common, which gives commonality and flight to quality. They show S\&P~500 futures margins rising in the liquidity crises of 1987, 1990, 1998 and 2007 (Figure~1, p.~3). Interpretation for a funding ladder (the pathway checkpoint): margin and haircut schedules belong in the stress scenario as functions of the shocked prices and volatility, not as constants, and a portfolio's existing positions relative to the crowd's flow decide whether a loss spiral applies (\hyperref[def:common-investor-contagion]{common-investor risk}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The spirals are conditional: with slack capital the price equals fundamental value and does not respond to small wealth shocks (Proposition~5(i), p.~18), and with informed financiers margins can be stabilising (Proposition~2, p.~12); the mechanism is a model result, not a description of every sell-off. The version read is the journal's Advance Access PDF hosted on Brunnermeier's page, paginated 1--38; the issue pagination (\emph{RFS} 22(6), 2201--2238) was not compared, so locators are to the Advance Access pages.
