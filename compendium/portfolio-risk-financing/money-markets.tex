\section{Money markets and reference rates}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Money-market instruments and reference rates: bills, commercial paper, repo, CDs and bankers' acceptances; fed funds, SOFR, LIBOR's end, EONIA and \texteuro{}STR, SONIA; and the LIBOR--OIS spread as a historical stress gauge]\label{def:money-market-reference-rates}
\qffield{Statement} Money markets are short-term, liquid credit instruments: commercial paper, Treasury bills, agency and municipal paper, bankers' acceptances, time deposits, interbank loans and repurchase agreements \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``Funding/Money Markets'', PDF pp.~246--253]{s7-gliner}. Gliner's details: commercial paper is unsecured, mostly 90 to 270 days, exempt from SEC registration if under 270 days, and cheaper for issuers than bank borrowing (p.~246); time deposits (CDs) run from 30 days to five years, those of a year or less counting as money-market instruments (p.~252); bankers' acceptances are short-term obligations guaranteed by a bank, mainly for trade (pp.~251--252); a repo is ``a sale of securities with a simultaneous agreement by the seller to repurchase them at a later date, which is usually done overnight'', mostly against Treasuries or GSE debt (p.~252). \emph{Fed funds} are unsecured overnight loans of reserve balances, and the effective rate serves as the reference for OIS (p.~257). \emph{LIBOR}, in the BBA's definition, was the rate at which a panel bank could borrow ``by asking for and then accepting interbank offers in reason [sic] market size, just prior to 11:00 A.M. London time'', computed as a trimmed mean of 16 banks' submissions (p.~254). The LIBOR--OIS spread compares unsecured term bank borrowing with the compounded overnight rate; it ``skyrocketed'' in 2008, rose with European banks' dollar funding needs in the euro crisis, and ``does not automatically indicate a crisis'' but is ``a useful barometer for credit risk'' (pp.~261--262).

\qffield{Notation} SOFR the Secured Overnight Financing Rate; EFFR the effective federal funds rate; \texteuro{}STR the euro short-term rate; SONIA the Sterling Overnight Index Average.

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Current benchmarks.} SOFR measures the cost of overnight borrowing collateralised by Treasuries, and the EFFR is a volume-weighted median of overnight federal funds transactions, both published by the New York Fed \citep[SOFR description; EFFR and OBFR methodology]{exp-sofr-data}. The U.S.\ dollar LIBOR panel ended after 30 June 2023 \citep[retrieved 2026-10-16]{arrc-sofr-transition}, with synthetic settings to 30 September 2024 \citep[sec.~2, n.~11]{cbot-23-214}. EONIA was discontinued on 3 January 2022 \citep[retrieved 2026-10-16]{emmi-eonia}; the ECB first published \texteuro{}STR on 2 October 2019 \citep[retrieved 2026-10-16]{ecb-estr}. A LIBOR--OIS spread therefore exists only historically; its function is now served by spreads between unsecured term rates or commercial paper and OIS, and by repo and funding-market data, which this entry has not examined.

\qffield{Practice} The legacy's T-bill maturities (4, 13, 26 and 52 weeks) were not checked against Treasury's current offering schedule (Gliner gives the history of bill auctions, p.~247). ``Money market stress \dots\ often precedes broader financial stress'' is a plausible but unsourced generalisation.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy's LIBOR, ``Phased out post-2021 due to manipulation scandals'', compresses two things: the manipulation cases undermined confidence, but the cessation followed the lack of underlying unsecured transactions and the FCA's 2021 announcements (\hyperref[meth:policy-path-ois-futures]{policy path}); U.S.\ dollar panel settings continued to 30 June 2023. ``EURSTR replaced EONIA in 2022'': \texteuro{}STR began in 2019 and EONIA ended on 3 January 2022. ``SONIA: Sterling overnight rate'' omits that SONIA was reformed in 2018 to cover more transactions (not checked here). Gliner describes a pre-2013 LIBOR calculation by the BBA; administration passed to ICE Benchmark Administration later (not checked here).
