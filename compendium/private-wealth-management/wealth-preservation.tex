\begin{qfmethod}[Taxes and inflation in individual investment decisions: types of taxes on individuals, accrual, deferral and wealth taxes, after-tax and real returns, with US and UK rates]\label{meth:private-client-tax-inflation}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Types of tax.} Individuals pay tax on income (wages, interest, dividends), on realised capital gains, on consumption, on property, sometimes on wealth, and on transfers at death or by gift (Interpretation). For investors, the relevant 2026 rates are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{United States} (federal): ordinary income up to 37 percent above USD~640,600 single or 768,700 joint; long-term gains and qualified dividends at 0, 15 or 20 percent, the 20 percent rate above USD~545,500 single or 613,700 joint \citep[secs.~4.01, 4.03]{irs-rev-proc-2025-32}; plus a 3.8 percent net investment income tax on investment income above modified adjusted gross income of USD~200,000 single or 250,000 joint \citep[sec.~1411]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}.
\item \emph{United Kingdom} (2026--27, outside Scotland): personal allowance GBP~12,570, basic rate 20 percent on the next 37,700, higher 40 percent, additional 45 percent above 125,140; dividends above a 500 allowance at 10.75, 35.75 and 39.35 percent \citep[tables]{govuk-income-tax-rates-2026}; gains above a 3,000 allowance at 18 percent within the basic-rate band and 24 percent above it \citep[rates page]{govuk-cgt-rates-2026}.
\end{itemize}

\emph{How taxes compound.} With return $r$ over $n$ years:
\begin{itemize}
\item tax on accrual each year at $t$: $(1+r(1-t))^n$;
\item deferred gains taxed once at sale: $(1+r)^n(1-t)+t$ per unit invested;
\item a wealth tax at $t_w$ on value each year: $[(1+r)(1-t_w)]^n$.
\end{itemize}
Deferral lowers the effective annual rate below the statutory rate; tax-exempt accounts remove it. \emph{Inflation} raises the effective tax on real returns, because nominal income and gains are taxed even when they only keep pace with prices (Derivation).

\qffield{Practice} (original; \texttt{private-wealth-checks.py}, part 2). One hundred invested at 6 percent for 20 years:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
Treatment & Ending value \\
\midrule
Tax-exempt & 320.71 \\
US interest taxed yearly at 40.8\% (37\% + 3.8\%) & 200.99 \\
US gains deferred, taxed at 23.8\% at the end & 268.18 \\
UK dividends taxed yearly at 39.35\% & 204.39 \\
UK gains deferred, 24\% at the end & 267.74 \\
1\% annual wealth tax & 262.31 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The deferred US gain's effective annual tax rate is 15.7 percent. With 2.5 percent inflation, interest taxed at 40.8 percent earns 1.03 percent real, against 3.41 percent before tax: the tax takes 70 percent of the real return. \emph{Decision}: for a top-bracket client, hold income-producing assets in tax-exempt or deferred accounts and growth assets in taxable accounts; prefer inflation-linked instruments in tax-advantaged accounts, where the inflation uplift is not taxed (\hyperref[meth:tax-in-planning]{tax management}).

\qffield{Conditions} Federal US and non-Scottish UK rates for the tax years starting in 2026; state, local and Scottish taxes, phase-outs, allowances for savings income and treaty credits are ignored. The chapter uses these two jurisdictions because they contrast citizenship- with residence-based taxation, estate with inheritance tax and two pension designs; it is not advice to any real person.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Comparing pre-tax returns across accounts with different tax treatment. Ignoring inflation when judging a tax rate. Assuming deferral is worth nothing because the rate is the same at the end.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:allocation-constraints-taxes-revision]{taxes in allocation}; \hyperref[meth:tax-in-planning]{tax management}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Taxes in goals-based planning and tax management across asset types: deferral, exemption, rate differences, asset location, loss harvesting, holding periods and wrappers, in the United States and the United Kingdom]\label{meth:tax-in-planning}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Principles} (Derivation, from the formulas in \hyperref[meth:private-client-tax-inflation]{taxes and inflation}): plans should be built on after-tax cash flows; tax deferral is valuable even at an unchanged rate; exempt accounts remove tax on growth; differences between current and future rates and between income and gains create opportunities; and the basis rules at death or gift change what is worth realising.

\emph{Tax efficiency by asset type}: interest and non-qualified income are taxed at ordinary rates each year and are least efficient in taxable accounts; qualified dividends and long-term gains are taxed at lower rates, gains only when realised; inflation-linked bonds are taxed on the inflation uplift; real estate may offer depreciation; and municipal bonds in the US are exempt from federal tax (Interpretation).

\emph{Strategies}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Asset location}: hold income-heavy assets in deferred or exempt accounts and growth assets in taxable accounts.
\item \emph{Loss harvesting}: realise losses to offset gains, avoiding the US wash-sale rule, which disallows a loss if substantially identical securities are bought within 30 days before or after the sale \citep[sec.~1091(a)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}. The value is the deferral, unless the replacement is held until death, when US basis steps up to fair market value \citep[sec.~1014(a)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}.
\item \emph{Holding periods and timing}: defer gains, realise them in low-income years, and use annual allowances (in the UK the 3,000 capital-gains allowance).
\item \emph{Wrappers}: US 401(k) and IRA, UK pensions and ISAs (GBP~20,000 a year in 2026--27, \citep[overview]{govuk-isa-2026}).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Practice} (original; \texttt{private-wealth-checks.py}, part~7). A US top-bracket client (not the Okafors) holds 500,000 of bonds yielding 4.5 percent and 500,000 of stocks, split between a taxable account and an IRA whose withdrawals will be taxed at 24 percent. Both placements use one convention for the stocks: each year the price rises 5 percent of the opening value, a dividend of 2 percent of the opening value is paid at year-end, and the dividend (after tax, in the taxable account) is reinvested. A reinvested after-tax dividend adds to basis. In the first year, the taxable account's 10,000 dividend bears 2,380 of tax at 23.8 percent; 7,620 is reinvested, giving a value of 532,620, basis 507,620 and an unrealised gain equal to the 25,000 price rise. The IRA reinvests the whole dividend (535,000).
\begin{itemize}
\item bonds in the IRA and stocks in the taxable account end after 20 years with USD~2,454,619 after tax; the reverse location with 2,316,409, so the recommended location adds 138,211. With every tax set to zero, both placements end with 3,140,699: location alone cannot change gross performance;
\item harvesting a 50,000 loss saves 11,900 at 23.8 percent; if the lower basis is taxed at the same rate in 10 years, the benefit is the deferral, worth 5,255 at 6 percent, and if the position is held until death, the full 11,900;
\item a repurchase 31 days after the sale keeps the loss; one 30 days after loses it.
\end{itemize}
For a UK client the analogue is to use the ISA allowance each year (``bed and ISA''), realising gains within the 3,000 allowance, and to hold dividend-paying assets in the ISA, since dividends above 500 are taxed at up to 39.35 percent.

\qffield{Conditions} 2026 US federal and 2026--27 UK rates; the IRA withdrawal rate and returns are assumptions. The taxable stocks are liquidated at the end, with the gain over basis taxed at 23.8 percent; holding them until death would remove that tax through the step-up and widen the advantage.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Letting the tax tail wag the investment dog: harvesting into an unsuitable replacement. Ignoring a spouse's or the client's own purchases in other accounts under the wash-sale rule.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:allocation-constraints-taxes-revision]{taxes in allocation}; \hyperref[meth:retirement-across-jurisdictions]{retirement accounts}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Preserving purchasing power: inflation-protection strategies for spending and investments, and how exchange rates affect allocation and plans, with ways to reduce the risk]\label{meth:purchasing-power-fx}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Inflation.} Spending needs are real, but many assets and incomes are nominal. A level nominal pension or annuity loses purchasing power steadily; taxes on nominal returns make it worse (\hyperref[meth:private-client-tax-inflation]{taxes and inflation}). Strategies: inflation-linked bonds such as TIPS, whose principal is indexed to CPI-U \citep[web page]{treasurydirect-tips}; equities and real assets, which tend to protect over long horizons but not reliably over short ones; inflation-indexed or escalating annuities and state pensions; holding inflation-linked assets in tax-advantaged accounts; and planning spending in real terms with a margin (Interpretation, with \hyperref[def:tips-breakevens]{TIPS}).

\emph{Exchange rates.} A client who spends in one currency and holds assets in others bears currency risk on those assets. Hedging foreign bonds removes most of their risk, since currency returns are almost uncorrelated with bond returns; for global equities some currencies (in 1975--2005 the US dollar, euro and Swiss franc) moved against equity markets and reduced risk, so a full hedge is not always risk-minimising \citep[NBER WP 13088, pp.~1--3]{campbell-viceira-currency-hedging-2007}. Practical steps: match the currency of assets to the currency of planned spending, especially for near-term goals; hedge foreign bonds; decide a hedge ratio for foreign equities; and for clients with multi-currency lives (expatriates, retirees abroad), hold separate currency buckets for each spending stream (Interpretation; \hyperref[meth:currency-hedging-programme]{currency programme}).

\qffield{Practice} (original; \texttt{private-wealth-checks.py}, part~10).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Inflation}: a level nominal pension of 50,000 is worth 27,700 in today's money after 20 years of 3 percent inflation, a 45 percent loss. For the Okafors' essential retirement spending above Social Security, a TIPS ladder bought gradually in their 401(k)s matches real spending and avoids tax on the inflation uplift.
\item \emph{Exchange rates}: Margaret Hartley lives on sterling with 30 percent of her portfolio in US dollar assets. A 10 percent rise in sterling cuts the portfolio's sterling value by 2.7 percent. Recommendation: hedge her dollar bonds fully and half her US equities, keeping the rest unhedged for diversification, and hold three years of spending in sterling assets.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Inflation and currency figures are illustrative; Campbell and co-authors' results are for 1975--2005 and a one-quarter horizon.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating nominal bonds as safe for real goals. Hedging all foreign equities by reflex. Ignoring the currency of future spending for internationally mobile clients.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:lifetime-exposures]{longevity}; \hyperref[meth:residency-citizenship]{residency}.

\end{qfmethod}
