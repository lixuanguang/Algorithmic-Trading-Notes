\section{Advising the wealthy and transferring wealth}

\begin{qfmethod}[Citizenship, nationality and residency in private wealth planning: US citizenship-based taxation and exit tax, the US substantial presence test, the UK statutory residence test, the UK's residence-based regimes from 2025, Singapore's residence rule and treaty tie-breakers]\label{meth:residency-citizenship}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Citizenship against residence.} The United States taxes its citizens wherever they live; most countries tax by residence. A US citizen abroad therefore stays in the US system unless they renounce, and the UK--US treaty preserves that right (Art.~1(4)) \citep[Art.~1(4)]{uk-us-double-taxation-convention-2001}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{US residence for non-citizens}: a green card or the \emph{substantial presence test}, 31 days in the year and at least 183 days counting all days this year, a third of last year's and a sixth of the year before's, with a closer-connection exception for those present fewer than 183 days \citep[sec.~7701(b)(3)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}.
\item \emph{US exit tax}: a \emph{covered expatriate} is one with average annual net income tax above an indexed threshold (USD~211,000 for 2026), net worth of 2 million or more, or who fails to certify five years of compliance \citep[sec.~877(a)(2)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}; \citep[sec.~4.37]{irs-rev-proc-2025-32}. All their property is treated as sold the day before expatriation, with gains above an exclusion (USD~910,000 for 2026) taxed \citep[sec.~877A(a)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}; \citep[sec.~4.38]{irs-rev-proc-2025-32}.
\item \emph{UK residence}: the statutory residence test: 183 days or more is residence; otherwise automatic overseas tests (fewer than 16 days if resident in one of the last three years, fewer than 46 days if not, or full-time work abroad), automatic UK tests (a UK home, full-time UK work) and, failing those, a sufficient-ties test whose required number of ties falls as days rise \citep[secs.~2--4, Tables A and B]{hmrc-rdr3-2026}.
\item \emph{UK regimes from 6 April 2025}: domicile no longer matters for income tax; new arrivals who were not UK resident in the previous 10 years can claim relief on foreign income and gains for their first four years of residence \citep[policy summary]{govuk-nondom-reform-2024}. Inheritance tax on foreign assets follows long-term residence: 10 of the previous 20 tax years, with the tail after leaving lasting 3 to 10 years \citep[IHTM47020]{hmrc-ihtm-2026}.
\item \emph{Singapore}: a foreigner is tax resident after at least 183 days in the previous calendar year, or three consecutive years, among other tests \citep[residency rules]{iras-tax-residency-2026}.
\item \emph{Dual residence}: treaties assign residence by permanent home, then centre of vital interests, then habitual abode, then nationality, then mutual agreement (UK--US treaty, Art.~4(4)).
\end{itemize}
Nationality also determines passports, visas and the right to live and work in a place, and can be acquired through residence, descent or investment programmes (Interpretation).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Maya Chen} (original; \texttt{private-wealth-checks.py}, part~11), 45, US citizen, an executive in New York, is offered a five-year post in London and might later retire to Singapore.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{UK}: she has not been UK resident in the last 10 years, so in her first four UK tax years her foreign income and gains (for example, US investment income) can be relieved from UK tax if she claims; UK earnings are taxed. After 10 UK-resident years her worldwide estate would be in UK inheritance tax.
\item \emph{US}: she remains taxable as a citizen and claims credits for UK tax; renouncing would make her a covered expatriate (net worth above 2 million): with 3.5 million of unrealised gains, 2.59 million would be taxed, about 616,000 at 23.8 percent.
\item \emph{Days}: on leaving London, spending 100 days in the UK in a year would keep her UK resident if she kept two ties (Table~A); a returning US non-citizen spouse with 150, 120 and 120 days in successive years would meet the US test (210 weighted days).
\item \emph{Recommendation}: keep US citizenship (the exit tax and loss of US access outweigh savings); plan the London move to use the four-year relief, realising US gains before arrival only if UK rules make that useful; set a review before year 9 in the UK to decide on leaving before inheritance tax exposure; and consider Singapore later for residence, not citizenship, coordinating with US filing.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Statutes and guidance as published by 2026. The four-year regime is described from the government's August 2024 policy summary; the enacted legislation governs the details. Residence rules have many exceptions; investment-migration programmes change often.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Assuming that moving abroad ends US tax for a citizen. Counting days without checking ties and treaty tie-breakers. Overlooking the inheritance-tax tail after leaving the UK.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:retirement-across-jurisdictions]{cross-border pensions}; \hyperref[meth:wealth-transfer-strategies]{transfers}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Complex families, business owners and executives: blended and cross-border families, entrepreneurs' concentration and liquidity events, qualified small business stock, executives' equity awards and trading plans]\label{meth:complex-client-situations}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Complex families} (Interpretation): blended families (children from earlier marriages, the need to provide for a spouse and still protect children's inheritances); a non-citizen spouse (the US annual exclusion for gifts to such a spouse is USD~194,000 in 2026, not unlimited, \citep[sec.~4.42(2)]{irs-rev-proc-2025-32}); dependants with special needs; divorce and pre-marital agreements; and family members in several countries. Approaches: written wills and trusts that separate income for a spouse from capital for children, guardianship and support trusts, and early agreement on family rules (\hyperref[meth:advising-families]{family framework}).

\emph{Entrepreneurs and business owners}: most wealth sits in one illiquid, undiversified company whose fortunes are tied to the owner's human capital. Plans cover key-person and succession risk, separating personal from business assets, building liquid wealth outside the business, preparing for a sale or listing, and using tax provisions. In the US, gains on \emph{qualified small business stock} acquired after 4 July 2025 are excluded at 50, 75 or 100 percent after three, four or five years, up to the greater of USD~15 million and ten times basis per issuer \citep[sec.~1202(a)(1), (a)(5), (b)(1), (b)(4)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}.

\emph{Professionals and executives}: concentrated employer stock and options, vesting schedules, insider-trading restrictions and liability exposure. A Rule~10b5-1 trading plan gives an affirmative defence if adopted in good faith without material nonpublic information; for directors and officers trading may start only after the later of 90 days and two business days after the next 10-Q or 10-K, but no more than 120 days after adoption, and they must certify the absence of inside information \citep[para.~(c)(1)(ii)(B)]{sec-rule-10b5-1}.

\emph{Concentrated stock positions.} A position counts as concentrated when its low tax basis or the owner's attachment stands in the way of an efficient, diversified portfolio. It brings company-specific risk, an inefficient portfolio, liquidity risk in a large or private block and a large tax bill on sale. The choice of remedy turns on the degree of concentration, the stock's risk, the basis, liquidity, the tax rate, the horizon, any restrictions and the owner's attachment \citep[chap.~16, sec.~6.1, pp.~904--906]{portfolio-management-in-practice-v1-2020}. What each remedy keeps and gives up:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Sell and diversify}, often the best course: the tax is paid now and the exposure ends. \emph{Staged diversification} spreads sales, and tax, over several tax years but prolongs the exposure. A \emph{completion portfolio} is an index-based portfolio, funded by partial sales and built with a risk model, that gives the whole holding benchmark-like exposures: it underweights the stock's industries and can realise losses to offset the gains. For a client with 1 million of Exxon on a 100{,}000 basis, selling all would cost 225{,}000 at 25 percent; the stock's tracking error against the S\&P~100 is 19.8 percent, and the last 2 percent can be kept at no cost in risk because that is its index weight (pp.~907--908).
\item \emph{Hedge, then monetise}: hedge with puts, a collar, a forward, a swap or a short sale, then borrow against the hedged shares at a high loan-to-value ratio and invest the loan. The owner keeps the shares, the votes and the low basis; pays for financing and gives up upside; and relies on the counterparty for protection (pp.~908--911). A zero-premium collar locks a band of values for the options' life. A variable prepaid forward combines a collar with a loan, turning 70 to 90 percent of the value into cash and sharing part of any rise with the dealer \citep[Exhibit~11-4, p.~694]{managing-investment-portfolios-2007}. A hedge alone diversifies nothing: a collar turns the stock into something like a short-term bond for its life, and a prepaid forward diversifies only if its cash is invested, and must be rolled if the gain is never to be realised (Example~11-3, p.~695).
\item \emph{Exchange fund}: shares are contributed to a partnership holding other investors' shares, for a pro rata interest and, after a holding period, a basket of the fund's securities. Diversification is incomplete, fees are often around 2 percent a year, and the manager decides which shares to accept and which to distribute (MIP, p.~694; PMiP, pp.~911--912).
\item \emph{Charitable remainder trust}: an irrevocable gift of shares to a trust that sells and diversifies them, pays an income to named beneficiaries and leaves the remainder to charity (PMiP, p.~912).
\end{itemize}

\emph{US federal tax mechanics}, as published on 21 October 2026; rules, not advice.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Constructive sale.} An appreciated stock position is taxed as if sold when the holder, or a related person, enters into a short sale of the same or substantially identical property, an offsetting notional principal contract (one passing on substantially all of the gain and loss), or a futures or forward contract to deliver it. A forward contract here means one to deliver a substantially fixed amount of property for a substantially fixed price. Transactions with substantially the same effect are caught only to the extent regulations prescribe. A transaction closed within 30 days after the year end, followed by 60 days of unhedged holding, is disregarded \citep[sec.~1259(a), (c)(1), (c)(3), (d)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}. A collar is not among the listed transactions.
\item \emph{Prepaid forward.} Rev. Rul. 2003-7 found neither a current nor a constructive sale on specified facts. The holder received a fixed cash amount and agreed to deliver a number of shares that varied significantly with the price (80 to 100 in the ruling). The maximum was pledged to an unrelated trustee, with votes and dividends retained, and the holder was free to deliver cash or other shares instead and was not economically compelled to deliver the pledged ones. The ruling warns that legal or economic compulsion to deliver may change the result \citep[2003-5 I.R.B., pp.~363--365]{irs-rev-rul-2003-7}.
\item \emph{Exchange fund.} A contribution to a partnership is not taxed (sec.~721(a)), unless the partnership would be an investment company if incorporated (sec.~721(b), sec.~351(e)(1)). Broadly, that is a transfer that diversifies the transferors' interests into an entity more than 80 percent of whose assets are held for investment and are readily marketable stocks or securities, or interests in regulated investment companies or REITs; hence such funds hold at least a fifth of their assets outside those classes \citep[para.~(c)(1), (c)(5)]{us-treas-reg-1-351-1}. The seven-year holding period matches secs.~704(c)(1)(B) and 737: if the contributed stock goes to another partner, or other property to the contributor, within seven years, the contributor's precontribution gain is recognised. The basis carries over, so gain is deferred, not removed.
\item \emph{Charitable remainder trust.} Charitable remainder annuity trusts and unitrusts pay no income tax, but unrelated business taxable income bears an equal excise tax (sec.~664(c)). A sale inside the trust is therefore not taxed to the trust. The income beneficiary's payments, however, are treated first as ordinary income and then as capital gain to the extent of the trust's current and undistributed gains (sec.~664(b)), so the gain is spread over the payments rather than escaped. The payout must be 5 to 50 percent a year and the remainder worth at least 10 percent at funding (sec.~664(d)).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Practice} (original; \texttt{private-wealth-checks.py}, part~12).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Luis Rivera, founder}: holds shares in his company acquired after 4 July 2025 with a basis of 1 million. On a sale producing a 20 million gain after five years, 15 million is excluded (the greater of 15 million and 10 times basis), and the remaining 5 million is taxed at 23.8 percent: 1.19 million, against 4.76 million without the exclusion. Sold after three years, only 7.5 million would be excluded. \emph{Recommendation}: unless a buyer's offer is far better earlier, time the sale for the five-year mark; before it, build a liquid reserve outside the company and agree a succession plan through the family council.
\item \emph{An executive}: Tunde Okafor, if an officer, adopts a 10b5-1 plan on 4 March 2026. With the 10-Q filed 7 May, sales may start on 2 June; if the 10-Q were late (5 June), on 9 June; never after 2 July. The plan sells a fixed number of shares each month for 12 months.
\item \emph{A concentrated holding} (original): 100{,}000 shares at 50, basis 10, an assumed 20 percent tax on the gain, one year at 4 percent, volatility 30 percent, Black--Scholes values, before any tax on the hedged positions. A sale pays 800{,}000 of tax and leaves 4.2 million, 4.37 million a year later if held in cash, with no exposure. A zero-cost collar (long 45 put, short 62.04 call) raises no cash and leaves the holding worth between 4.50 and 6.20 million: protection. A prepaid forward specified like the ruling's contract (deliver all shares below 50, shares worth 5 million between 50 and 62.50, 80{,}000 shares above) advances 4.53 million now, 90.6 percent of value, with no dealer margin. The holder keeps nothing below 50, the rise from 50 to 62.50 and a fifth of any rise above: monetisation. The advance equals $5\text{m}\,e^{-rT}$ less a 50 put plus 0.8 calls at 62.50 on each share, a loan with a collar. At 30, 50 and 70 the totals a year later are 4.71, 4.71 and 6.11 million, against 3, 5 and 7 held unhedged. \emph{Exchange fund}, reproduced: on 1 million with a 100{,}000 basis, 25 percent tax and 10 percent a year for seven years, selling and reinvesting ends at 1{,}326{,}442 after tax and the fund at 1{,}486{,}538, if their returns are equal, which PMiP calls ``a big assumption'' (Exhibit~15, p.~912). Interpretation: a fee of 1.81 percent a year would remove the fund's advantage, close to the 2 percent MIP cites.
\item \emph{A blended family}: Margaret Hartley's son David has remarried; his wife has two children, and he wants her provided for while his own assets eventually pass to his children. Recommendation: a life-interest trust for his wife with the capital passing to his children, a letter of wishes, and coordinated wills. Assets left to her outright would be exempt from inheritance tax but could pass to her children; the trust's tax treatment needs UK legal advice.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} US federal law as amended in 2025 and SEC rules as in force in 2026; blended-family solutions depend on succession law in each jurisdiction. The concentrated-position tax notes are US federal income tax only, from the statutes, Treas. Reg. sec.~1.351-1 and Rev. Rul. 2003-7 as published on 21 October 2026. Not covered: state tax, other jurisdictions, the charitable deduction, any regulations under sec.~1259(c)(1)(E), later rulings and cases on prepaid forwards, and the tax on option premiums and dividends. The textbook statements that exchange funds or charitable trusts are ``tax free'' describe deferral. Collars and prepaid forwards are bespoke contracts, so the example specifies its own terms rather than describing the product.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Selling founder shares a few months before an exclusion improves. Treating a collar as diversification. Assuming every prepaid forward or collar has the result of Rev. Rul. 2003-7 whatever its terms. Calling an exchange fund or charitable trust tax-free. Ignoring the dealer's margin, financing, fees and counterparty risk. Adopting a trading plan while aware of inside information. Leaving everything outright to a second spouse and disinheriting children by accident.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:human-capital-risks]{human capital}; \hyperref[rule:standard-market-integrity]{Standard II}; \hyperref[meth:covered-call-protective-put]{covered calls and protective puts} (collars).

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Transferring wealth: lifetime gifts against bequests, US gift and estate tax and basis rules, UK inheritance tax and potentially exempt transfers, and philanthropy through trusts, donor funds and foundations]\label{meth:wealth-transfer-strategies}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{United States} (2026): gifts up to USD~19,000 per donee per year are excluded; above that, gifts use a unified lifetime exclusion that also shelters the estate, USD~15 million in 2026 and indexed afterwards, with the same amount exempt from generation-skipping tax \citep[secs.~3.14, 4.42]{irs-rev-proc-2025-32}. Estate and gift tax reaches 40 percent at the margin \citep[sec.~2001(c)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}. Assets inherited take a basis equal to market value at death; gifted assets keep the donor's basis \citep[secs.~1014(a), 1015(a)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}.

\emph{United Kingdom} (2026--27): inheritance tax at 40 percent above a GBP~325,000 nil-rate band; 36 percent if 10 percent of the net estate goes to charity; unused bands pass to a surviving spouse; transfers to a spouse or charity are exempt \citep[overview]{govuk-iht-2026}; \citep[thresholds table]{govuk-iht-thresholds-2026}. Gifts are exempt if the donor survives seven years; on death within seven years, tax on gifts above the nil-rate band tapers from 40 percent (under 3 years) to 32, 24, 16 and 8 percent; an annual exemption of 3,000, small gifts of 250 and regular gifts from income are exempt; gifts with reservation of benefit stay in the estate \citep[gifts]{govuk-iht-2026}. Gifts in the seven years before death use the nil-rate band before the estate does \citep[``Gifts and lifetime transfers'']{govuk-rnrb-guidance-2019}.

\emph{Residence nil-rate band.} A further allowance of up to GBP~175,000 (2020--21 to 2029--30, \citep[thresholds table]{govuk-iht-thresholds-2026}) applies when a home, or a share of it, passes to direct descendants on death; it is capped at the home's value and does not apply to lifetime gifts, but remains available to the estate when gifts have used the ordinary band. Above a taper threshold of 2 million it falls by 1 for every 2 of excess, measured on the net estate at death (assets less debts, before exemptions and reliefs, excluding lifetime gifts); unused band can transfer from a late spouse \citep[``Gifts and lifetime transfers''; ``How residence nil rate band tapers'']{govuk-rnrb-guidance-2019}; \citep[IHTM46023]{hmrc-ihtm46023-2026}. With 175,000 and nothing transferred, an estate of 2.1 million keeps 125,000 and one of 2.35 million or more keeps none.

\emph{Philanthropy across generations}. US options include outright gifts, donor-advised funds, private foundations, which must distribute about 5 percent of their non-charitable assets a year \citep[sec.~4942(e)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}, and charitable remainder trusts, which pay the donor a fixed 5--50 percent annuity (or a unitrust amount) for life or up to 20 years and leave the remainder to charity, the remainder being worth at least 10 percent at creation \citep[sec.~664(d)]{us-internal-revenue-code-26}. Foundations also serve as a vehicle for family governance and education (Interpretation).

\qffield{Practice} (original; \texttt{private-wealth-checks.py}, part~13).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Gift or bequest of a growth asset} (US). An asset worth 5 million with a 1 million basis is expected to be worth 10 million at death; the estate has 30 million of other assets. Kept, it is taxed in the estate: heirs receive 30.0 million after 10.0 million of estate tax, with the asset's basis stepped up. Given now, it uses 5 million of the exemption, estate tax falls to 8.0 million and heirs receive 32.0 million if they keep the asset, but 29.86 million if they sell and pay 23.8 percent on the 9 million carryover gain. \emph{Decision}: give it now only if the family will hold it or it will grow faster; in an estate below the exemption, keep it and let the basis step up.
\item \emph{Annual exclusions}: 19,000 to each of six grandchildren for ten years moves 1.14 million out of the estate without using exemption.
\item \emph{UK gift and estate} (Margaret Hartley, one chronology; her annual exemptions already used and no band transferred from a former spouse). She gives 500,000 to her son and dies 4.5 years later. The gift uses the whole 325,000 nil-rate band; the remaining 175,000 is taxed at the 4--5 year rate of 24 percent, 42,000, against 70,000 had she died within three years. Her 2.4 million estate, including a 900,000 home left to her children, has no nil-rate band left, and 400,000 above the taper threshold removes the whole residence band, so all 2.4 million is taxed at 40 percent: 960,000. The total, 1,002,000, compares with 1,030,000 had she kept the 500,000: the gift saved only its taper relief, 28,000. Had her estate been 2.1 million, 125,000 of residence band would remain and the estate tax would be 790,000. Leaving 10 percent of the net estate to charity lowers the rate to 36 percent.
\item \emph{Charitable trust} (US): a 1 million charitable remainder annuity trust paying 50,000 a year for 20 years: the payments are worth 623,000 at 5 percent, leaving a remainder of 377,000, above the 10 percent minimum.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} The US estate figure assumes a flat 40 percent above the exemption and no state tax; the remainder valuation uses a 5 percent rate rather than the IRS rate for the month. UK rules as on GOV.UK and in HMRC's manual in 2026 for deaths in 2026--27, outside any transitional rule; the guidance pages are not legislation (the residence band is in IHTA 1984 ss.~8D--8M).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Gifting low-basis assets to heirs who will sell them. Keeping a benefit in a gifted home. Treating charitable trusts as tax shelters rather than as gifts.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:residency-citizenship]{residence and inheritance tax}; \hyperref[meth:advising-families]{family governance}.

\end{qfmethod}
