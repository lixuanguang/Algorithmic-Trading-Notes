\section{Interest rates, discounting and rate risk}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Interest-rate measures: reference rates, compounding conventions, zero rates, bond yields and par yields, with time-value identities derived here]\label{def:interest-rate-measures}
\qffield{Statement} An interest rate states what a borrower promises to pay; the extra amount over a risk-free rate for credit risk is the \emph{credit spread} \citep[chap.~4, ``Interest Rates'', sec.~4.1, p.~98]{s7-hulloptions}. Hull's types of rate (pp.~98--99):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Treasury rates}, at which a government borrows in its own currency; ``it is usually assumed'' that a developed country will not default on such obligations;
\item \emph{overnight rates}, from banks lending reserves to one another overnight; in the United States the federal funds rate, which the Federal Reserve ``may intervene'' to move;
\item \emph{repo rates}, secured: securities are sold and bought back at a slightly higher price, the difference being the interest; overnight and term repos.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{$n$-year zero} or spot rate is earned on an investment with no intermediate payments; a bond's theoretical price discounts each cash flow at its own zero rate; its \emph{yield} is the single rate that reprices it to its market price; its \emph{par yield} is the coupon that prices it at par (secs.~4.5--4.6, pp.~104--106). An amount $A$ at rate $R$ compounded $m$ times a year grows to $A(1+R/m)^{mn}$ in $n$ years, and to $Ae^{Rn}$ with continuous compounding; with $m=1$ the rate is the \emph{equivalent annual} rate (sec.~4.4, pp.~101--103).

\qffield{Notation} $R_m$ a rate compounded $m$ times a year, $R_c$ the equivalent continuous rate: $R_c=m\ln(1+R_m/m)$ and $R_m=m(e^{R_c/m}-1)$ (eqs.~(4.3)--(4.4), p.~104). Interpretation, derived here and not from a source read: the \emph{effective annual rate} (EAR) is the annual-compounding equivalent, $\mathrm{EAR}=(1+R_m/m)^m-1=(1+r_p)^m-1$ for a periodic rate $r_p=R_m/m$, and $e^{R_c}-1$ in the continuous case; present value is $PV=FV_N(1+R_m/m)^{-mN}$ or $FV_Ne^{-R_cN}$. For a level payment $A$ at a periodic rate $r>0$ over $N$ periods:
\[ FV^{\mathrm{ord}}_N=A\,\frac{(1+r)^N-1}{r},\qquad FV^{\mathrm{due}}_N=FV^{\mathrm{ord}}_N(1+r),\qquad PV^{\mathrm{ord}}=A\,\frac{1-(1+r)^{-N}}{r},\qquad PV^{\mathrm{perp}}=\frac Ar, \]
where an ordinary annuity pays at the end of each period (first payment one period from now) and an annuity due at the start (first payment now).

\qffield{Conditions} The annuity factors need $r\neq0$; as $r\to0$ they tend to $N$ (each payment simply summed), and the perpetuity value $A/r$ is the limit of $PV^{\mathrm{ord}}$ as $N\to\infty$, which exists only for $r>0$. Checked: at $r=5$ percent and $N=10$, the ordinary and due future values of 100 a period are 1{,}257.79 and 1{,}320.68 both by formula and by summing the payments; the future-value factor tends to 10.46, 10.0045, 10.000005 for $r=10^{-2},10^{-4},10^{-7}$; perpetuity partial sums are 1{,}825.59, 1{,}999.88 and 2{,}000.00 after 50, 200 and 1{,}000 periods against $A/r=2{,}000$. Monthly compounding at 6 percent gives an EAR of 6.1678 percent, continuous 6.1837 percent. Hull's examples check: 10 percent semiannual is 9.758 percent continuous (p.~104); the two-year bond on his Table~4.2 zero curve prices at 98.39, yields 6.76 percent and has a par yield of 6.87 percent (pp.~105--106).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reference rates.} Hull's description of the effective federal funds rate as the volume-weighted \emph{average} of brokered transactions (p.~99) is out of date. The New York Fed changed the EFFR's data source and methodology with the rate for 1 March 2016; it is now a volume-weighted \emph{median}, the rate at the 50th percentile of dollar volume, of overnight federal funds transactions reported on the FR~2420 \citep[EFFR and OBFR methodology]{exp-sofr-data}. SOFR, ``a broad measure of the general cost of financing Treasury securities overnight'', is likewise a volume-weighted median, of tri-party repo, GCF Repo and FICC-cleared bilateral (DVP) Treasury repo transactions, with some specials filtered out \citep[SOFR description]{exp-sofr-data}; Hull calls it an important volume-weighted median of overnight repo rates (p.~99). Derivatives traders do not use Treasury rates as risk-free rates, because tax and regulatory factors make them artificially low; they use rates derived from overnight rates (sec.~4.3, p.~101), and the new overnight reference rates are backward-looking (p.~101).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy table called EAR the ``expected annual rate''; it is the effective annual rate, a conversion of compounding, not an expectation. The legacy repeated Hull's EFFR definition, superseded in 2016 as above. ``Treasury rates of developed countries are regarded as risk-free'' holds for default in their own currency, as Hull states it, not for price risk, and not as the rates used to discount derivatives. The legacy ``future value (continuous)'' used a ``stated'' rate: it must be the continuously compounded rate, not a rate quoted with another frequency. Compounding conventions must not be mixed within one calculation.

\begin{qfmodel}[Forward rates and forward rate agreements: forward rates from zero rates, the instantaneous forward, FRA payoff, value and timing, and theories of the term structure]\label{mod:forward-rates-fra}
\qffield{Statement} With continuously compounded zero rates $R_1,R_2$ for maturities $T_1<T_2$, the forward rate for the period between them is \citep[chap.~4, ``Interest Rates'', sec.~4.8, pp.~109--110, eqs.~(4.5)--(4.6)]{s7-hulloptions}
\[ R_F=\frac{R_2T_2-R_1T_1}{T_2-T_1}=R_2+(R_2-R_1)\frac{T_1}{T_2-T_1},\qquad R_F(T)=R+T\frac{\partial R}{\partial T}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\ln P(0,T), \]
the second being the instantaneous forward rate, with $P(0,T)=e^{-RT}$ the zero-coupon bond price. A \emph{forward rate agreement} exchanges a fixed rate $R_K$ for a reference rate observed at a future time, both applied to a principal $L$ for a period of length $\tau$; the principal is not exchanged (sec.~4.9, pp.~110--112). Derived from Hull's definitions (pp.~111--112), the party receiving fixed receives $\tau(R_K-R_M)L$ at the end of the period, where $R_M$ is the realised reference rate (negative when it pays), and the FRA is worth the \emph{present value} of $\tau(R_K-R_F)L$, with $R_F$ the current forward rate; the payer of fixed holds the negative (p.~112).

\qffield{Notation} Rates in $R_F$'s formula are continuously compounded; in the FRA, $R_K$, $R_F$ and $R_M$ are expressed with the compounding of the period $\tau$ (quarterly for a three-month rate, p.~111). $P(0,T)$ discount factor.

\qffield{Conditions} The FRA value rests on the result that an FRA can be valued by assuming the forward rate is realised; it needs the discount rate for the payment date (Hull's Example~4.3 discounts at the 2-year risk-free rate). Interpretation: this is a single-curve illustration, in which the forward reference rate and the discount rate come from consistent curves; current practice separates projection and discounting curves and, for backward-looking compounded overnight rates such as SOFR, the floating amount is known only at the end of the period (Hull p.~101). Settlement timing changes the amount: Hull's LIBOR FRA, known at the start of the period, was paid at the start as the present value of the end-of-period amount (p.~111, n.~4). Hull presents three term-structure theories, none established as a result: \emph{expectations} theory (forward rates equal expected future zero rates), \emph{market segmentation} (no necessary relation between maturities) and \emph{liquidity preference} (forward rates above expected future zero rates, consistent with upward-sloping curves being more common); Hull calls expectations and segmentation conjectures and finds liquidity preference ``most appealing'', consistent with the empirical observation (sec.~4.12, pp.~117--119).

\qffield{Practice} Checked: from zero rates of 3, 4, 4.6, 5 and 5.3 percent for 1--5 years, forward rates are 5, 5.8, 6.2 and 6.5 percent (Table~4.5, p.~109); the instantaneous forward of an illustrative curve $R(T)=0.03+0.005T$ at $T=2$ is 5.0 percent by finite difference of $-\ln P$ and by $R+TR'$. Hull's Example~4.3, receiving 5.8 against a forward of 5.0 percent (semiannual) on \$100 million for six months, discounted at 4 percent continuous for 2 years, gives \$369{,}247; Hull prints \$369{,}200 (p.~112). Locking in forward rates by borrowing and lending at different maturities, and the Orange County yield-curve plays, are Hull's illustrations of the risk of betting against forwards (pp.~110--111). \emph{Market notation} (merged from the legacy macro chapter, slice~37): a ``$3\times6$'' FRA starts in three months and ends in six, a three-month rate three months forward; Gliner's example is a $3\times6$ ``over 15th'' traded in March 2013, settling 15 June and maturing 15 September 2013 \citep[chap.~9, sec.\ ``Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs)'', PDF p.~263]{s7-gliner}. FRAs can be dated to straddle a policy meeting or a release, unlike quarterly futures bound to their expiry dates (p.~263); in U.S.\ dollars the reference is now SOFR rather than LIBOR (\hyperref[meth:policy-path-ois-futures]{LIBOR cessation}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Hull's own example has the payment direction reversed: Party A receives the fixed 3 percent and pays LIBOR, so with LIBOR at 3.5 percent A pays, rather than receives, \$125{,}000 (p.~111); his formula for the FRA's value (p.~112) has the correct sign; the author's errata were not checked. The legacy wrote the FRA value as $\tau(R_K-R_F)L$ without discounting; that is the payoff if the forward is realised, not its present value. The legacy liquidity-preference line said forward rates ``are'' greater than expected future spot rates; Hull presents this as what the theory implies. \emph{Term-premium evidence} (third batch of 2026-10-17). Adrian, Crump and Moench split model-fitted yields into a risk-neutral part, ``the time $t$ expectation of average future short rates'', and a term premium, ``the difference between the risk neutral yield and model-implied fitted yield'' \citep[Staff Report 340, rev.\ April 2013, sec.~2.4, PDF p.~12]{exp-adrian-crump-moench-term-structure}. In their five-factor model ``investors on average require a positive expected excess return for holding the level portfolio'', and the price of that risk ``varies significantly as a function of the slope and the fifth factor'' (sec.~3.3, PDF p.~21). The New York Fed's published estimates (monthly, June 1961 to September 2026, vintage retrieved 2026-10-17; own summary in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/rates-credit-mortgages/batch60-acm-term-premium-summary.py}) put the 10-year term premium at $+1.43$ points on average. It was positive in every month from 1970 to 2014, with a maximum of $+5.20$ in May 1984. It was negative in 101 of 784 months, first again in January 2015, and in 51 of the 81 months since 2020, reaching $-1.38$ in July 2020; it stood at $+0.89$ in September 2026. Read against the theories: the pure expectations theory's zero premium is rejected on average, but so is a constant positive liquidity premium. The premium is time-varying and sometimes negative. These are model estimates: they depend on the factor specification, are revised when the model is re-estimated, and are not observed prices. \emph{A second model} (fourth batch of 2026-10-17). Kim and Wright's three-latent-factor arbitrage-free model, fitted to Treasury yields from 1990 with survey forecasts of short rates in its Kalman filter, ``ascribes a large portion of the decline in long-term yields and distant-horizon forward rates since the middle of 2004 to a fall in term premiums'' \citep[FEDS 2005-33, abstract and sec.~2, PDF pp.~2--12]{rt-kim-wright-2005}. Own comparison of the Board's published KW 10-year zero-coupon term premium (daily, month-end) with ACM's (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/curve-term-structure/batch70-gsw-kw-acm.py}, vintages retrieved 2026-10-17): over 441 common months, January 1990 to September 2026, the correlation is 0.86 and KW is on average 0.24 points lower (range $-2.22$ to $+1.18$); KW was negative in 58 months and ACM in 98; in June 2020 KW was $-0.61$ against ACM's $-1.23$, and in September 2026 $+1.08$ against $+0.89$. The two models agree on the broad path and differ by up to two points in level, which is the size of the specification risk in any single estimate. Gliner calls the fixing date ``the date of the trade'' (p.~263); the fixing date is when the reference rate is observed, normally shortly before the start of the period, not the trade date. His remark that Eurodollar futures ``lack convexity'' refers to the convexity difference between daily-settled futures and FRAs, which makes futures rates exceed forward rates (Hull's convexity adjustment, not read here).

\begin{qfmodel}[FRA and swap valuation with separate projection and discounting curves, and the backward-looking compounded SOFR period rate]\label{mod:multi-curve-fra-swap-valuation}
\qffield{Statement} Hull values an interest rate swap as a portfolio of FRAs in three steps \citep[chap.~7, sec.~7.6, pp.~183--185]{s7-hulloptions}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item calculate forward rates for each unknown floating rate;
\item set the swap's cash flows on the assumption that those rates equal the forwards;
\item discount the net cash flows ``at the risk-free rate''.
\end{enumerate}
The risk-free zero curve comes from overnight indexed swaps. OIS rates to one year ``provide the risk-free zero rates that are equivalent to the underlying overnight rates''. Longer OIS rates define par bonds, and are bootstrapped like a par-yield curve (sec.~7.2, p.~175). The two curves can differ.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{An OIS or SOFR swap.} Projection and discounting both use the risk-free curve. The first floating rate ``is not really a forward rate. It is a blend of one-day rates already observed and the zero rate that corresponds to the remaining one-day rates in the period'' (p.~184).
\item \emph{A LIBOR (term-rate) swap.} The forwards are estimated from Eurodollar futures, with a convexity adjustment, and from swap rates that make par swaps worth zero, while the cash flows are still discounted at the OIS-based risk-free rate (p.~184).
\end{itemize}
In symbols (own derivation, corrected in the fourth batch of 2026-10-17 to name the settlement convention), take a receive-floating contract on reference index $j$ over $[T_1,T_2]$, with accrual $\tau$, fixed rate $K$, notional $N$ and fixing $L$ observed at $T_1$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{End settlement (a swaplet, paid at $T_2$):} the payoff $N\tau(L-K)$ is worth $N\tau\,(F_j(T_1,T_2)-K)\,P_{\mathrm{OIS}}(T_2)$, where $F_j$ is read from index $j$'s own projection curve. The formula is exact when $F_j$ is \emph{defined} as the expectation of $L$ under the $T_2$-forward measure of the OIS curve, which is what a projection curve calibrated to OIS-discounted swaps delivers. This is how Hull's swap cash flows are valued, each paid at the end of its period.
\item \emph{Start settlement (the conventional FRA, paid at $T_1$):} the end amount is discounted at the fixing itself, $N\tau(L-K)/(1+\tau L)$, as in Hull's footnote: ``the payment would be made at time two years and equal to the present value of \$125{,}000 discounted for three months at 3.5\%'' \citep[chap.~4, sec.~4.9, p.~111, n.~4]{s7-hulloptions}. Its value is $N P_{\mathrm{OIS}}(T_1)\,\mathbb{E}^{T_1}[\tau(L-K)/(1+\tau L)]$. If $L$ is deterministic, $L=F_j$, this is $N\tau(F_j-K)P_{\mathrm{OIS}}(T_1)/(1+\tau F_j)$.
\end{itemize}
The two conventions coincide in the single-curve case, the formula of \hyperref[mod:forward-rates-fra]{forward rates and FRAs}: there $1/(1+\tau L)$ is the discount factor from $T_2$ to $T_1$, so the start-settled payoff is replicated with bonds, $N[P(T_1)-(1+\tau K)P(T_2)]$, whatever $L$ does. With separate curves they differ: by $N\tau(F_j-K)[P_{\mathrm{OIS}}(T_1)/(1+\tau F_j)-P_{\mathrm{OIS}}(T_2)]$ even with deterministic rates. With a stochastic $L$, the deterministic substitution is no pricing theorem. The start-settled payoff is concave in $L$, and the $T_1$-forward mean of $L$ need not equal $F_j$, so its value carries a convexity adjustment that depends on $L$'s volatility and its correlation with OIS discount factors. Neither that adjustment nor the market's FRA conventions (ISDA FRA discounting, fixing and payment lags) were read in a source; \texttt{s6-brigo} and \texttt{wide-henrard-multicurve} are the catalogue readings that would. Bianchetti and Carlicchi's comparison table values a FRA as $NP_d(t,T_k)\omega[\tilde F_{x,k}(t)-K]\tau_{x,k}$, with the FRA rate $\tilde F_{x,k}(t)=E^{Q_d^{T_k}}[L_x(T_{k-1},T_k)\,|\,\mathcal F_t]$, the expectation of the fixing under the $T_k$-forward measure of the discounting curve, which ``is a martingale under such measure'' while the classical forward is not (p.~9, and Table~2, p.~11, page image) \citep[arXiv 1103.2567, version of 28 March 2012, Table~2, p.~11]{gap-rates-multicurve-sabr} (fourth batch of 2026-10-17). That is the end-settled form above, with $F_j$ defined as that expectation; their table does not treat payment at $T_1$. For a backward-looking rate compounded over the period, $\prod_i(1+r_id_i)$ of the overnight forwards implied by a curve telescopes to $P(T_1)/P(T_2)$. So its forward is the simple forward from discount-factor ratios, before convexity, day-count and lookback adjustments, which were not read.

\qffield{Notation} $P_{\mathrm{OIS}}(T)$ discount factor from the OIS curve; $F_j(T_1,T_2)$ the forward for reference index $j$ (the $T_2$-forward expectation of its fixing); $L$ the fixing at $T_1$; $\mathbb{E}^{T}$ expectation under the $T$-forward measure of the OIS curve; $r_i$, $d_i$ the overnight rate and its day fraction.

\qffield{Conditions} Hull's calculations ``are approximate because they do not take account of holiday calendars and day count conventions'' (p.~185). Discounting at the OIS rate presumes cash-collateralised trades earning the overnight rate; uncollateralised trades bring funding and credit adjustments, which are not covered here.

\qffield{Practice} Own checks, \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/rates-credit-mortgages/batch60-multicurve-check.py}. Hull's Example~7.1 is reproduced: paying 3 percent semiannually against six-month SOFR on \$100 million, with floating rates 2.50, 3.36 and 3.68 percent continuous (2.516, 3.388 and 3.714 semiannual), the swap is worth $+\$0.292$ million (p.~185). The first rate is $0.6\times2.3+0.4\times2.8=2.50$ percent, the blend of observed and expected overnight rates. With invented curves, a term reference 30 basis points above OIS and $K=3.4$ percent on \$100 million for a quarter starting in one year, an end-settled receive-floating contract is worth $-\$20{,}793$ on two curves and $-\$20{,}715$ discounted on the term curve. Projecting at OIS gives $-\$93{,}604$. The projection curve matters far more than the discounting curve for one period. Settled at the start under the deterministic substitution, the same contract is worth $-\$20{,}777.37$, against $-\$20{,}792.96$ settled at the end (\nolinkurl{batch70-fra-settlement-check.py}, which also shows the conventions coinciding on one curve and diverging on two). With a lognormal $L$ of 20 percent volatility, deterministic OIS rates and $\mathbb{E}[L]=F_j$ (an invented illustration), the start-settled value falls to $-\$21{,}044.50$: the concavity term is larger than the settlement-convention gap.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Hull's 11th edition still describes LIBOR forwards from Eurodollar futures, which ended with LIBOR (\hyperref[meth:policy-path-ois-futures]{LIBOR cessation}). Brigo and Mercurio (\texttt{s6-brigo}) and the SOFR-futures hedging reading (\texttt{gap-rates-sofr-futures-hedging}) were not read, so lookback, lockout and payment-delay conventions, and the futures convexity adjustment, are not covered here.

\qffield{Further reading} \texttt{s6-brigo}; \texttt{gap-rates-sofr-futures-hedging}.

\begin{qfmodel}[Duration and convexity: Macaulay and modified duration, convexity, the second-order price approximation, and the parallel-shift assumption]\label{mod:duration-convexity}
\qffield{Statement} For a bond paying $c_i$ at $t_i$ with price $B=\sum_ic_ie^{-yt_i}$ at continuously compounded yield $y$, the duration is the present-value-weighted average time of payment \citep[chap.~4, ``Interest Rates'', sec.~4.10, pp.~112--115, eqs.~(4.7)--(4.13)]{s7-hulloptions}:
\[ D=\sum_i t_i\frac{c_ie^{-yt_i}}{B},\qquad \frac{\Delta B}{B}\approx-D\,\Delta y,\qquad C=\frac1B\frac{d^2B}{dy^2}=\frac{\sum_ic_it_i^2e^{-yt_i}}{B},\qquad \frac{\Delta B}{B}\approx-D\,\Delta y+\tfrac12C(\Delta y)^2. \]
With $y$ compounded $m$ times a year, $\Delta B\approx-BD^*\Delta y$ with modified duration $D^*=D/(1+y/m)$ (pp.~114--115); dollar duration is $BD^*$ (p.~115). Convexity is from sec.~4.11 (pp.~116--117).

\qffield{Notation} $D$ Macaulay duration (years); $D^*$ modified duration; $C$ convexity (years squared); $\Delta y$ a change in the bond's own yield. For continuous compounding $D^*=D$.

\qffield{Conditions} The duration relation is a first-order approximation, accurate for small changes; convexity adds the second-order term. For a portfolio, the weighted-average duration assumes all yields change by about the same amount, which for bonds of widely different maturity means a small parallel shift of the zero curve; matching durations protects against small parallel shifts only, and matching convexity as well against larger parallel shifts, but neither against non-parallel shifts (pp.~115--117).

\qffield{Practice} Checked on Hull's Table~4.6 bond (3-year 10 percent coupon, yield 12 percent continuous): $B=94.213$, $D=2.653$ years and, computed here, $C=7.570$. For $\Delta y=+0.001$ the exact price is 93.9634 against 93.9631 from duration, as in Hull's Example~4.4 (p.~114). For $\Delta y=+0.02$ the exact price is 89.354 against 89.214 from duration and 89.357 with convexity; for $-0.02$, 99.357 against 99.212 and 99.355. With the yield expressed semiannually (12.3673 percent), $D^*=2.499$, and a 10~bp rise gives 93.978 both exactly and by modified duration (Example~4.5, p.~115). \emph{DV01 and the macro trader's usage} (merged from the legacy macro chapter, slice~37; derived here, not from Hull or Gliner): $\mathrm{DV01}=D^*B/10{,}000$, the price change for 1~bp; for a 10-year 4 percent annual-coupon bond at par, $D^*=8.111$, convexity 80.8 and DV01 0.0811 per 100 of face, and a 100~bp rise gives $-7.722$ percent exactly against $-7.707$ percent from $-D^*\Delta y+\tfrac12C(\Delta y)^2$ (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/slice37-checks.py}, section~10). Curve trades weight legs by DV01 to neutralise level moves (\hyperref[meth:curve-trades-carry-rolldown]{curve trades}). \emph{What positive convexity does and does not mean} (corrected in the slice-37 follow-up). Positive convexity makes the price \emph{better than the linear (duration) approximation} for a move either way; it does not make an unhedged bond gain from moves either way: the same bond loses 7.722 percent for $+100$~bp (8.111 by duration alone, so convexity adds back 0.389 points) and gains 8.530 percent for $-100$~bp (0.419 more than duration). The legacy's ``Long convexity = long volatility'' analogy applies to a \emph{duration-hedged} position: long the 10-year and short a DV01-matched amount (4.30 times the face) of a 4 percent 2-year bond, which has much less convexity, gains 0.274 per 100 of 10-year face for a parallel $+100$~bp move and 0.302 for $-100$~bp, and only 0.018 for $+25$~bp, since the gain grows with $(\Delta y)^2$ (slice-37 follow-up check script, section~1). Qualifications (the carry sentence revised in the second slice-37 follow-up): the hedge holds only for parallel moves of both yields (a curve twist can swamp the convexity gain), and it must be rebalanced as durations change. \emph{Carry is not determined by convexity.} In this example the curve is flat at 4 percent and both bonds are at par; if the long 10-year is financed and the short 2-year's proceeds earn the same 4 percent, the annual carry per 100 of 10-year face is $4-4k+4(k-1)=0$ for any hedge ratio $k$ (here $k=4.30$; second follow-up check script, section~2), and with no yield change there is no rolldown either. Holding-period P\&L then comes only from yield moves, through the $(\Delta y)^2$ gain above, and from rebalancing. That a flat curve moving only in parallel would give a riskless gain to a long-convexity position is itself a sign that real curves cannot behave that way; where convexity is ``paid for'', it shows up as a curve shape in which more convex (longer) bonds yield less than they otherwise would, and so as negative carry relative to the hedge. Whether a given hedged position has positive or negative carry depends on the curve's slope and shape, on repo rates against the rebate on the short (specials, haircuts and balance-sheet costs make them differ), on the financing period and on the costs of rebalancing; positive convexity alone does not settle the sign. The analogy with a long-gamma option, which loses time value, holds only when such a carry cost is present. \emph{Negative convexity.} Callable bonds and MBS can be negatively convex where the issuer's call or the borrowers' prepayment option is near the money: as yields fall, prepayments or a call cap the price rise. Far from that region (a callable bond when rates are well above its coupon) the convexity can still be positive; MBS convexity depends on a prepayment model and on the path of rates, so ``effective convexity'' figures are model outputs, not observed quantities.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy wrote the duration and convexity relations as equalities and wrote $\Delta B=\frac{dB}{dy}\Delta y$ without the approximation; Hull marks them as approximate. A change in a bond's yield is not the same as a shift in the zero curve. Interpretation, derived and checked here: under a parallel shift $\Delta$ of the zero curve the bond's yield moves by exactly $\Delta$, for every $\Delta$, if and only if the zero rates at all of the bond's payment dates equal its yield. A flat curve is therefore sufficient but not necessary: a zero-coupon bond has $y=R(T)$ and moves one for one on any curve, and the curve may take any shape between payment dates. For a coupon bond on a curve that is not level across its payment dates, the yield moves by only about $\Delta$: 0.9996$\Delta$ for Hull's Table~4.2 bond and 0.986$\Delta$ for a 10-year 5 percent annual-coupon bond on a curve rising 40~bp a year from 1.4 percent. Duration against the bond's own yield, $-B^{-1}\,\partial B/\partial y$, and sensitivity to a parallel zero-curve shift, $-B^{-1}\,\partial B/\partial\Delta$, differ by that factor: 8.13 against 8.01 years for the 10-year bond. Modified duration applies only when $y$ uses the stated compounding; mixing a continuously compounded $D$ with an $m$-compounded $\Delta y$ misstates the change. The legacy macro chapter wrote the convexity adjustment as $\Delta P\approx-\text{Duration}\times\Delta y+\tfrac12\times\text{Convexity}\times(\Delta y)^2$, dropping the price: the right side is the \emph{proportional} change $\Delta P/P$, as above; and its modified duration as ``Approximate percentage price change for 1\% yield change'' holds only to first order.

\begin{qfconvention}[U.S. day count and price quotation: actual/actual, 30/360 and actual/360; Treasury bill discount quotes; Treasury bond prices in thirty-seconds; clean and dirty prices]\label{conv:us-rate-quotation}
\qffield{Statement} A day count $X/Y$ gives interest between two dates as the days between them, counted by $X$, over the days in the reference period, counted by $Y$, times the interest for the reference period \citep[chap.~6, ``Interest Rate Futures'', sec.~6.1, pp.~152--153]{s7-hulloptions}. In the United States, Hull lists actual/actual (in period) for Treasury bonds, 30/360 for corporate and municipal bonds and actual/360 for money-market instruments. Treasury bills are quoted as a discount rate on face value: $P=\frac{360}{n}(100-Y)$, with $P$ the quoted price, $Y$ the cash price per \$100 and $n$ the days to maturity. Treasury bonds are quoted in dollars and thirty-seconds per \$100 face, and the quote is the \emph{clean} price; the cash or \emph{dirty} price adds interest accrued since the last coupon (p.~154).

\qffield{Conditions} These are U.S. conventions as Hull gives them; conventions differ by country and instrument (money-market instruments on actual/365 in Australia, Canada and New Zealand; euro and sterling bonds usually actual/actual, p.~153). Futures quotes have their own conventions (sec.~6.2, p.~155).

\qffield{Practice} Checked with Hull's examples: \$4 earned March~1 to July~3 on an 8 percent \$100 bond is $\frac{124}{184}\times4=2.6957$ on actual/actual and $\frac{122}{180}\times4=2.7111$ on 30/360 (p.~153); a 90-day bill at a cash price of 99 is quoted at $\frac{360}{90}(100-99)=4$ (p.~154); 120-05 is $120\tfrac{5}{32}$, \$120{,}156.25 for \$100{,}000 face; accrued interest of $\frac{54}{181}\times5.50=1.64$ makes a clean price of 155.50 a dirty price of 157.14 (p.~154).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy wrote ``$P$ quoted price, $Y$ cash price'' correctly but described the discount rate only as ``interest as a percentage of final face value''; Hull adds that the true return for the period is on the price paid, 2.064 percent rather than 2.022 percent in his example (p.~154). Hull notes that SOFR compounding assumes 360 days a year (p.~100); day counts for SOFR and other reference rates are set per product, not by the bond conventions above.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:interest-rate-measures]{interest-rate measures}; \hyperref[def:fixed-income-market-structure]{fixed-income market structure}.

\qffield{Further reading} The legacy file is kept verbatim in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/volatility-options/}.

\begin{qfdata}[The Federal Reserve's fitted Treasury curve (Gürkaynak, Sack and Wright): the Svensson form, its published parameters and how to recompute the yields]\label{data:gsw-treasury-curve}
\qffield{Statement} Gürkaynak, Sack and Wright fit off-the-run Treasury notes and bonds each day with the Svensson extension of Nelson--Siegel: the zero yield at maturity $n$ is $y_t(n)=\beta_0+\beta_1\frac{1-e^{-n/\tau_1}}{n/\tau_1}+\beta_2[\frac{1-e^{-n/\tau_1}}{n/\tau_1}-e^{-n/\tau_1}]+\beta_3[\frac{1-e^{-n/\tau_2}}{n/\tau_2}-e^{-n/\tau_2}]$, continuously compounded; the parameters minimise duration-weighted squared price errors, approximately the squared yield errors (eq.~(22), p.~15, page image) \citep[FEDS 2006-28, sec.~3, pp.~13--15]{wide-gsw-yield-curve}. The Board publishes the daily parameters ($\beta_0$ to $\beta_3$, $\tau_1$, $\tau_2$) with zero yields (SVENY), par yields, instantaneous forwards and one-year forwards \citep[file \texttt{feds200628.csv}, 14 June 1961 to 9 October 2026, retrieved 2026-10-17]{fed-gsw-data}.

\qffield{Notation} $n$ maturity in years; $\beta_3=0$ and no $\tau_2$ in the early years, when the three-term Nelson--Siegel form is used.

\qffield{Conditions} The file is a staff research product, ``subject to delay, revision, or methodological changes without advance notice'' (file header); its yields are fitted values, smooth by construction, not traded prices; the fit excludes bills and on-the-run issues, as described in the paper.

\qffield{Practice} Own check (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/curve-term-structure/batch70-gsw-kw-acm.py}): recomputing the 10-year zero yield from each day's parameters reproduces the published SVENY10 on all 13{,}759 days with a 10-year yield to within 0.00005 points (rounding of the published value); 2{,}084 of those days use the three-term form. On 9 October 2026 both give 5.2514 percent. For the \texttt{curve-term-structure} checkpoint this curve is a dated, reproducible input, not a substitute for bootstrapping one's own.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The paper describes the method as of 2006; later changes to the sample or filters, which the file header allows, were not traced.

\qffield{Further reading} \texttt{wide-nelson-siegel}; \texttt{wide-hagan-west-interp}.

\begin{qfmechanism}[OTC derivatives clearing and collateral: central counterparties, bilateral ISDA/CSA clearing, variation and initial margin, and post-crisis reforms as Hull describes them]\label{mech:otc-clearing-collateral}
\qffield{Statement} An OTC trade can be presented to a central counterparty (CCP) or cleared bilaterally \citep[chap.~1, ``Introduction'', sec.~1.2, p.~25]{s7-hulloptions}. A CCP accepting a trade between A and B becomes counterparty to both and takes on the credit risk of each; its members post initial margin and daily variation margin and contribute to a guaranty fund, and non-members clear through members (chap.~2, sec.~2.5, pp.~54--55). In the CPSS--IOSCO principles a CCP ``interposes itself between counterparties'', ensuring the performance of open contracts through novation or an open-offer system, and has ``the potential to reduce significantly risks to participants'' through multilateral netting and risk controls \citep[para.~1.13, p.~9]{exp-pfmi}. Bilaterally cleared trades are covered by a master agreement, usually ISDA's, with a credit support annex (CSA) requiring collateral, typically valued daily (p.~55).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Reforms, as Hull reports them} (pp.~26 and~55--56): standardised OTC derivatives between U.S. financial institutions must, ``whenever possible'', trade on swap execution facilities; most parts of the world require CCP clearing of most standardised derivatives between financial institutions; trades must be reported to a repository; and from 2016 regulations required initial and variation margin for bilaterally cleared transactions between financial institutions, with initial margin held by a third party. Hull notes that ``financial institutions'' include banks, insurers, pension funds and hedge funds, while most non-financial corporations and some FX transactions are exempt (p.~56, n.~4). \emph{The U.S. primary rules} (third batch of 2026-10-17, eCFR text for 15 October 2026; the clearing and execution rules are in \hyperref[def:fixed-income-market-structure]{fixed-income market structure}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Reporting.} A swap executed on a SEF or designated contract market is reported to a swap data repository by the facility, and an off-facility swap by its reporting counterparty, ``as soon as technologically practicable after execution'' for public dissemination \citep[17 CFR 43.3(a)]{ecfr-17cfr-cftc-swaps}. Full creation data follow ``not later than the end of the next business day'' after execution, or the second business day for a reporting counterparty that is not a swap dealer, major swap participant or clearing organisation (45.3(a)--(b)).
\item \emph{Uncleared margin.} The CFTC's rules bind ``covered swap entities'', swap dealers and major swap participants without a prudential regulator; bank dealers fall under the prudential regulators' parallel rules, not read (23.151). They do not apply where the counterparty qualifies for the end-user clearing exception or the cooperative exemption (23.150(b)). Variation margin is exchanged daily with swap entities and \emph{financial end users} (23.153). Initial margin is collected from \emph{covered counterparties}, which are swap entities and financial end users with \emph{material swaps exposure} (an average month-end notional above \$8 billion). It is posted to financial end users with material swaps exposure (23.152, 23.151). It is held by a custodian that is neither party nor an affiliate, and may not be rehypothecated (23.157). The definitions set a \$50 million initial-margin threshold amount, whose use in the calculation (23.154) was not read. No transfer is required below a combined minimum transfer amount of \$500{,}000 (23.152(b)(3), 23.153(c), 23.151).
\item \emph{Phase-in} (23.161). Both margins applied from 1 September 2016 where both groups' average daily notional exceeded \$3 trillion. Variation margin applied to all covered entities from 1 March 2017. Initial margin phased down through \$2.25 trillion (2017), \$1.5 trillion (2018), \$0.75 trillion (2019) and \$50 billion (1 September 2021), reaching all remaining covered counterparties on 1 September 2022.
\end{itemize}
Hull's ``from 2016'' is therefore the first phase only; initial margin reached the smallest covered counterparties six years later. His ``held by a third party'' matches 23.157. His exemption of ``most non-financial corporations'' corresponds to the end-user exception and to the definition of financial end user. The international BCBS--IOSCO framework was not read: bis.org returned a web page instead of the PDF. \emph{Interest.} Initial margin in cash usually earns interest; futures variation margin does not, being daily settlement, while OTC variation margin in cash does, because OTC trades are not settled daily (p.~57). Securities used as collateral are subject to haircuts (p.~57).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: clearing and collateral mitigate counterparty risk rather than eliminate it. Margin is sized to cover most, not all, losses on a default (Hull's clearing-house example aims at about 99 percent certainty, p.~54), a guaranty fund mutualises the rest, and the CCP itself concentrates risk; Hull cites Duffie and Zhu for the dependence of the benefit on the number of CCPs and the share of trades cleared (p.~57, n.~5). Collateral also creates funding and liquidity demands: LTCM had to post collateral on both legs of its trades in 1998 and, being highly leveraged, had to close out positions (Business Snapshot~2.2, p.~56).

\emph{The uncleared-margin framework} (fourth batch of 2026-10-17; BCBS--IOSCO, April 2020 revision, read through an Internet Archive capture because bis.org served web pages in place of PDFs). Covered entities must exchange full variation margin (a zero threshold) and two-way initial margin with a threshold ``not to exceed \texteuro50 million'' at the level of the consolidated group, with a minimum transfer amount of at most \texteuro500{,}000 (Requirement~2, PDF p.~14). Initial margin was phased in by group notional of non-centrally cleared derivatives (month-end average for March--May): above \texteuro3.0 trillion from 1 September 2016, then \texteuro2.25 trillion (2017), \texteuro1.5 trillion (2018), \texteuro0.75 trillion (1 September 2019 to 31 August 2021), \texteuro50 billion (1 September 2021 to 31 August 2022), and on a permanent basis from 1 September 2022 above \texteuro8 billion; variation margin applied to all covered entities from 1 March 2017; only new contracts are covered (Requirement~8, pp.~23--24) \citep[April 2020, Requirements~2 and~8, printed pp.~10 and~23--24]{bcbs-iosco-margin-2020}. These are the international standards; the U.S. prudential regulators' rule and EMIR implement them in their own terms, which were not read.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy said the clearing house ``takes care of the credit risk''; Hull's phrase is that it does so ``by requiring \dots\ margin'' (p.~25), which reduces the risk without removing it. The legacy reform list dropped Hull's ``whenever possible'' and ``in most parts of the world'' qualifiers. Futures-specific margin rules (maintenance margin, margin calls, interest on futures balances) are in \hyperref[mech:futures-margin-settlement]{futures margin and settlement}.
