\subsection{Strategy research and backtest evaluation}

\begin{qfmethod}[Hypothesis-first strategy research: observation, theory, deduced consequences and attempted falsification, with Narang's four sources of ideas]\label{meth:hypothesis-driven-strategy-research}
\qffield{Statement} Narang describes quant research as following the scientific method \citep[chap.~``Research'', sec.~9.1, pp.~161--163]{s7-narang}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item observe something in the market that might be explainable, a pattern;
\item form a theory that explains it;
\item deduce the theory's consequences, for example that a trend should give a better-than-random forecast from past returns alone;
\item test the theory by looking for evidence against those consequences (Popper's falsification), not for proof. A theory not yet disproved is accepted only for the moment. At best the researcher gains enough confidence that the tests were rigorous to ``warrant risking some capital on the validity of this theory'' (p.~163).
\end{enumerate}
Because markets change, through regulation, investor psychology and competition for alpha, quants must keep researching rather than treat a validated theory as permanent (p.~163).

Narang's four common sources of ideas \citep[sec.~9.2, pp.~163--165]{s7-narang}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{observing the markets}, the source closest to the scientific method; his example is Donchian's two-week breakout rule for futures;
\item \emph{academic literature}: many quant firms spend significant time on journals, working papers and conference presentations; literature from astronomy, physics or psychology is also used;
\item \emph{migration} of researchers or portfolio managers between firms, despite noncompete and nondisclosure agreements. Investors who have seen inside one firm can also carry ideas to another;
\item \emph{lessons from discretionary traders}, such as ``ride winners and cut losers'' formalised as a stop-loss policy.
\end{itemize}
For machine-learning alphas he paraphrases practitioners: feature selection and engineering draw on the same sources (p.~165).

\qffield{Conditions} A practitioner's account of how ideas arise and are screened, in a 2024 book, not a statistical protocol. It does not say how many hypotheses were tried, how significance is adjusted for them, or what evidence suffices to risk capital: those are treated in \hyperref[meth:multiple-testing-control]{multiple-testing control}.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: stating the theory and its deduced consequences before testing fixes what would count as evidence against it. That is the step that separates a test from a search. Narang's out-of-sample discussion makes the same point from the other side: refitting after looking at out-of-sample data, or choosing models already known to have survived a period, ``burns'' that data (sec.~9.3.4, pp.~183--185; \hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Hypothesis-driven research needs explicit predicted consequences and attempted falsification, which Narang treats as its core (p.~162). Of discretionary traders' rules, Narang notes that systematic versions of chart patterns have mostly failed. That may be because the theory is invalid or because human practice is less rule-based than it seems, and ``not all successful traders have skill'' (p.~165). An idea's source is not evidence for it. Ideas that move with staff raise contractual and legal questions; Narang describes a dispute over noncompete agreements.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics]{backtest evaluation diagnostics}; \hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting} (what testing guards against); \hyperref[pit:backtest-cost-short-assumptions]{cost and short-availability assumptions}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Backtest evaluation diagnostics: the profit curve, return, variability and lumpiness, drawdowns, predictive power, win rates, risk-adjusted ratios, fit with other strategies, and time decay]\label{meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics}
\qffield{Statement} Narang lists statistics quants use to judge a model, in sample and out of sample, illustrated on his own daily S\&P~500 strategy. Its signal is an adjustment to the equity risk premium, the S\&P~500's earnings yield less the 10-year Treasury note's yield, computed each day: a higher stock yield is read as a signal to be long, a lower one as a signal to be short.

It ran on daily closes from June 1982 to December 2000, was built in the mid-1990s and was never traded \citep[chap.~``Research'', sec.~9.3.2, p.~168]{s7-narang}. It does not trade every day: it gives a signal only when the model judges the opportunity attractive enough to trade at all, and its signal is zero on 65 percent of days (sec.~9.3.2.6, p.~173). Narang does not state the adjustment or the threshold for a non-zero signal. Each diagnostic, with his example's value:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{cumulative profit graph}: shows whether, how smoothly and with what downside the strategy made money. His example is ``lumpy'', with years of inactivity (late 1989 to early 1995), sharp losses and steep gains, which he reads as ``real problems'' (sec.~9.3.2.1, p.~168);
\item \emph{average rate of return}: 746 percent cumulative, 12.1 percent a year, before costs and fees (sec.~9.3.2.2, p.~168);
\item \emph{variability of returns}: ``in general, the less the variability for a given level of returns, the better'' (sec.~9.3.2.3, p.~169); 21.2 percent annualised. His firm also uses \emph{lumpiness}, the share of total return earned in periods significantly above average, as a measure of consistency, while noting that consistency is not always a primary goal;
\item \emph{worst peak-to-valley drawdowns} and recovery times: lower drawdowns are better and long recoveries are disliked; several drawdowns are measured, not only the worst. His example's worst is $-39.7$ percent, short before the October 1987 crash (sec.~9.3.2.4, pp.~169--170);
\item \emph{predictive power}: the $R^2$ of the target on the signal, 0.0085 for his example in Exhibit~9.2 (sec.~9.3.2.5, p.~171). Returns are also bucketed by quantiles of the forecast: returns rising monotonically across buckets indicate reliable predictive power, and non-monotonic buckets may mean the strategy works by accident (p.~172);
\item \emph{percentage of winning trades or periods}, a measure of consistency. His example has a zero signal 65 percent of the time, wins 19 percent and loses 16 percent, so about 54 percent of non-zero days win (sec.~9.3.2.6, pp.~172--173);
\item \emph{ratios of return to risk}, as Narang defines them (sec.~9.3.2.7, pp.~173--174): the Sharpe ratio, average periodic return above the risk-free rate divided by its variability; the information ratio, the same with ``any arbitrary benchmark return---most often zero'' in place of the risk-free rate (0.57 in the example); the Sterling ratio, average return over the variability of below-average returns (0.87); the Calmar ratio, ``average return divided by the worst peak-to-valley drawdown'' (0.31); and the Omega ratio, ``the sum of all positive returns divided by the sum of all negative returns'' (1.26). Taken literally, both of Narang's quotients have a negative denominator, but he reports both ratios as positive. Interpretation: the explicit conventions are
\[ \text{Calmar}=\frac{\bar R}{\lvert \mathrm{MDD}\rvert},\qquad \Omega(0)=\frac{\sum_t \max(R_t,0)}{\bigl\lvert\sum_t \min(R_t,0)\bigr\rvert}, \]
with $\bar R$ the average (annualised) return, $\mathrm{MDD}$ the worst peak-to-valley drawdown, a negative number, and $R_t$ the periodic returns. With his example's $\bar R=12.1$ percent and $\mathrm{MDD}=-39.7$ percent, the first gives $12.1/39.7\approx0.3048$, which rounds to $0.30$, while the literal quotient gives $-0.30$. Narang reports a Calmar ratio of $0.31$ (pp.~173--174); the recomputation from his printed inputs does not reproduce that second digit, and the passage does not explain the difference. For returns of $+2$, $+1$, $-1.5$ and $-0.5$ percent, the second gives $3/2=1.5$, while the literal sum quotient gives $-1.5$. The Sterling and Omega values cannot be recomputed without the strategy's returns;
\item \emph{relationship with other strategies}: a correlation with the existing book, or better, the existing strategy's results with and without the new idea (sec.~9.3.2.8, p.~174);
\item \emph{time decay}: the strategy's returns when trades start one, two, three or more days after the signal. This shows its sensitivity to timely information and, Narang says, ``some information about how crowded the strategy is'', since crowding would mean faster moves to a new equilibrium (sec.~9.3.2.9, pp.~174--176).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Practitioner account as of 2024; the S\&P example covers 1982--2000. One author's list and one illustrative strategy, not a validated scorecard. Narang says ``a significant amount of discretion is used'' in judging models (p.~176). None of the diagnostics is adjusted for the number of strategies or parameter sets tried, and none comes with a standard error.

\qffield{Practice} Narang's time-decay example uses analyst downgrades of Microsoft announced before the open on 28 April 2006. The stock opened about 11.1 percent down and underperformed the S\&P~500 by about 11.4 percent that day. A simulation must not assume a fill before the open. Selling at the close on 28 April, 1 May or 2 May made money over two weeks; selling from 3 to 12 May lost (pp.~174--175). Delay can also help: entering his S\&P strategy one day late raised the total return from 746 to 870 percent (12.9 percent a year), which he takes as a bad sign, because the signal is early (pp.~175--176).

Interpretation: most of his example figures agree within rounding: $12.1/21.2=0.57$, $19/35=0.54$, and 746 and 870 percent over about 18.6 years compound to about 12.2 and 13.0 percent a year. The Calmar ratio is the exception: $12.1/39.7\approx0.3048$ from his printed inputs, against his reported $0.31$; the difference is unexplained.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Interpretation, qualifying the heuristics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Smooth profits.} Narang reads lumpiness as a problem in his example and says consistency is not always the goal. A smooth curve can come from stale or managed valuations rather than skill (\hyperref[meth:relative-performance-evaluation]{relative performance evaluation}), and from short-volatility payoffs whose losses the sample did not contain. Narang's own convertible-arbitrage example (1990--1997, then 1998) shows this (p.~170). The rule ``if the profit profile is not smooth \dots\ the model may have issues'' is his reading of one example, not a test.
\item \emph{Lower volatility.} His rule holds return fixed (``for a given level of returns''); the ``the less variable \dots\ the better'' dropped that condition. His ``67 percent of the time within one standard deviation'' assumes roughly normal annual returns (about 68 percent for a normal distribution).
\item \emph{Drawdowns.} A worst drawdown from a favourable sample understates risk. Narang's convertible-arbitrage example is ``sample bias'', and his remedy, shuffling the historical returns to get many paths, still draws only from the sample (pp.~170--171). Shuffling single returns also destroys serial dependence, which drives drawdown depth; that point is not in Narang. A synthetic check shows its size. For AR(1) monthly returns with autocorrelation 0.3 over ten years, the model's 95th percentile of maximum drawdown is 0.99 in log units. The median shuffle-bootstrap estimate from one sample is 0.53. Resampling blocks of geometric length (mean 12) gives 0.85. Politis and Romano's original stationary-bootstrap paper is not covered; the method is restated below.

White restates it: resample blocks ``of random length, distributed according to the geometric distribution with mean block length $b$'', which ``delivers valid bootstrap approximations for means of $\alpha$-mixing processes, provided $b$ increases appropriately with $n$'' \citep[sec.~2, p.~1104]{catalogue-0731}. That validity statement covers means, and statistics built from them, not a path functional such as the maximum drawdown. So the 0.85 above is an own simulation result, not something a theorem guarantees (\hyperref[meth:multiple-testing-control]{multiple testing}).

\emph{Block length}. The authors of the method later corrected the automatic block-length rule of Politis and White (2004): after Nordman (2008) found an error in Lahiri's variance calculation for the stationary bootstrap, the variance constant becomes $D_{SB}=2g^2(0)$, the circular block bootstrap's optimal mean-squared error is $(2/3)^{2/3}\approx0.763$ of the stationary bootstrap's, and the earlier simulation tables are replaced; with the correction the estimated block sizes average 90 to 110 percent of the optimal ones in their AR(1) designs \citep[secs.~1--2, pp.~372--373]{patton-politis-white-2009}. That bears on choosing $b$ for a mean; whether the same choice suits path statistics such as the maximum drawdown is not addressed there.
\item \emph{The $R^2$ of 0.05.} ``A superb $R^2$ in our industry is 0.05 (out of sample)'', with two colleagues' remarks (0.15 too good to be legitimate, ``rich off a 0.02''), is a practitioner's judgement with no horizon, asset, sample or design stated (p.~171). An $R^2$ depends on the horizon, on whether the regression is a time series or a cross-section, and on the target's noise. Treat it as an order of magnitude for one context, not a benchmark; his own example is a daily, single-index time series.

\emph{Published magnitudes}. For annual U.S. equity-premium forecasts with forecasts beginning twenty years into each sample, Welch and Goyal's out-of-sample adjusted $R^2$ (in percent) is negative for every variable in their Table~1 (p.~1470). The dividend--price ratio has in-sample 0.49 and out-of-sample $-2.06$, the earnings--price ratio 1.08 and $-1.78$, the term spread 0.16 and $-2.42$ \citep[Table~1]{s7-welch}. Their abstract concludes that the models ``predicted poorly both in-sample (IS) and out-of-sample (OOS) for 30 years'' (p.~1455). An out-of-sample 0.05 would be far above these for that target and horizon.

\emph{Monthly forecasts with restrictions}. Campbell and Thompson forecast monthly U.S.\ excess returns, evaluated from 1927 or twenty years after each series starts. Their restricted forecasts set a coefficient to zero when it has the ``wrong'' sign, and a forecast of the equity premium to zero when it is negative. With both restrictions, 3 of 4 valuation ratios and 5 of 7 interest-rate variables have a positive out-of-sample $R^2$, against 2 of 4 and 3 of 7 without \citep[Table~1, panels A--D, pp.~6--7]{campbell-thompson-2005-nber}. They judge such an $R^2$ against the squared Sharpe ratio: a mean--variance investor's proportional gain in expected return from the predictor is about $R^2/S^2$ (eqs.~(8)--(9), pp.~9--10).

With a monthly Sharpe ratio of 0.108, so $S^2=1.2$ percent, the earnings--price ratio's out-of-sample $R^2$ of 0.25 percent raises the average monthly return by $0.25/1.2=21$ percent. That is about 3 percent a year for unit risk aversion and 1 percent for risk aversion three (p.~10). So a monthly out-of-sample 0.05 would be twenty times their earnings--price figure, against an $S^2$ of about 0.012. The paper was published in the \emph{Review of Financial Studies} (2008).
\item \emph{Bucket study.} On p.~172 the text gives the two most bearish quintiles average next-day returns of $-2.35$ and $-0.19$ percent. In Exhibit~9.3 those figures label the signal buckets on the horizontal axis, and the bars, the next-day returns, run from about $-0.03$ to $+0.13$ percent. The monotonic ordering holds in the figure; the text's return figures do not match it.
\item \emph{Ratios.} Narang's information ratio with a zero benchmark is return over volatility, not active return over tracking error, and his Sterling ratio, the variability of below-average returns, is a downside-deviation measure. Other sources define both differently.
\item \emph{Time decay and crowding.} A fast decay shows that the strategy depends on timely information. It does not identify crowding: in Narang's Microsoft example the move is a public announcement priced at the open, and a signal that is early improves with delay. His crowding inference is an interpretation without evidence in the chapter. The two crowding studies available at abstract level measure crowding from common holdings and infer it from fire-sale dislocations; neither uses the decay of a delayed signal (\hyperref[def:common-investor-contagion]{common-investor risk}).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:hypothesis-driven-strategy-research]{hypothesis-first research} (what is being tested); \hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting} (sensitivity to parameters, the next diagnostics); \hyperref[pit:backtest-cost-short-assumptions]{cost and short-availability assumptions} (what the returns assume); \hyperref[meth:relative-performance-evaluation]{relative performance evaluation} (the same statistics applied to managers, and smoothed returns); \hyperref[meth:nested-forecast-tests]{nested forecast tests} and \hyperref[meth:restricted-forecast-value]{restricted forecasts} (testing and valuing the equity-premium forecasts quoted above).

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfpitfall}[Parameter overfitting: an isolated optimum, a model that explains its sample too closely, and the balance against underfitting]\label{pit:parameter-overfitting}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{The error.} A parameter set is chosen because it performed best in a sample, although nearby values perform very differently. A model with many parameters or signals explains the past ``so closely \dots\ that it might be useless for predicting the future'' \citep[chap.~``Research'', secs.~9.3.2.10--9.3.3, pp.~176--179]{s7-narang}.

\emph{How it arises.} Training searches parameters and keeps the best (p.~167). In Narang's terms, overfitting is ``expecting too much from the data you have'', treating an unrepresentative sample as the population. An isolated peak, his point~D in Exhibit~9.5, is likely a ``spurious coincidence in the fitting period'' (pp.~177--178). Re-using out-of-sample data, by refitting after seeing it or by building models already known to work in that period, turns it into in-sample data (``burning data'', pp.~183--184).

\emph{Checks.} Vary each parameter slightly. In Narang's P/E example, a cheap threshold of 12 against 11 and an expensive threshold of 50 against 49 should give similar results, and if they do not, he would distrust and use neither (sec.~9.3.2.10, p.~176). Plot outcome against parameter value (Exhibit~9.5, p.~178). Compare the out-of-sample $R^2$ with the in-sample $R^2$: a ratio of about half or better ``is considered a good thing'' (p.~183).

\emph{Remedies.} Choose a value with a margin of safety on a broad plateau, his point~B, over the plateau's highest point near a cliff (C) or a lonely peak (D) (pp.~177--179). Prefer parsimonious models, since models with many parameters or signals carry more risk of overfitting (p.~179). Separate research from strategy selection, or withhold data from researchers, to limit burning (pp.~184--185).

\qffield{Conditions} Practitioner view as of 2024. In the third edition Narang presents overfitting as one side of a balance. Overfitting is a Type~I error and underfitting a Type~II error; as easy alpha became scarce from about the early 2010s, and as data and methods improved, narrower and more fitted models became reasonable (pp.~180--182). How much fitting is warranted depends on the competition for alpha and on the strength of the prior relative to the data; he believes the industry may overestimate the value of more fitting, especially beyond the shortest horizons (pp.~182--183). Einstein's ``as simple as possible, but not simpler'' is his caution against oversimplifying (p.~179).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: smooth performance across nearby parameters is a sign of robustness to the parameter, not evidence that the strategy has an edge. A whole family of rules, each stable in its own parameters, can be the survivor of a search over many families. The adjustment for the number of trials is in \hyperref[meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio]{the deflated Sharpe ratio}. Narang's ``flat'' criterion is local: his preferred point B lies on a plateau in a curve that has cliffs, so it does not require a curve without jumps.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Fewer assumptions do not guarantee a better model: parsimony must be balanced against underfitting and the role of priors. His claim that simpler theories are better because more easily tested is attributed to Popper (p.~179) .

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics]{backtest evaluation diagnostics} (the statistics being optimised); \hyperref[meth:hypothesis-driven-strategy-research]{hypothesis-first research} (theory before fitting).

\end{qfpitfall}

\begin{qfpitfall}[Backtest cost and short-availability assumptions: costs set too high or too low, and shorts the strategy could not have borrowed]\label{pit:backtest-cost-short-assumptions}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{The error.} A backtest assumes trading costs, or the ability to short, that the strategy would not have had. A backtest has no record of what the strategy would have cost, because it did not trade, so costs, especially market impact, must be assumed \citep[chap.~``Research'', sec.~9.3.5, p.~185]{s7-narang}.

\emph{How it arises, costs.} Narang's example: a model right 55 percent of the time, making \$0.01 a share, and wrong 45 percent, losing \$0.01, earns \$0.10 per 100 shares if trading is free. At an all-in cost of \$0.01 a share it breaks even on its winners and loses \$0.02 on its losers, $-\$0.90$ per 100 shares (pp.~185--186). Overestimating costs makes a quant hold positions longer than is likely optimal; underestimating them makes the portfolio turn over too fast and ``bleed'' costs (p.~186).

\emph{How it arises, shorts.} In a U.S. market-neutral or long/short equity backtest, the best short candidates may be on hard-to-borrow lists, stocks the broker generally restricts from shorting because it cannot locate shares to borrow. The live strategy then cannot place those shorts and substitutes inferior ones, so the short book's backtested value may not exist (p.~186).

\emph{Checks and remedies.} Narang advises erring towards overestimating costs if one must err, though ``it is always preferable to get the cost estimation approximately right'' (p.~186). He notes that making a model aware of past hard-to-borrow status is not trivial, because such historical data are hard to come by (p.~186). Interpretation: compare the backtest's cost assumptions with pre-trade cost forecasts and with realised costs measured after trading (\hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis}). Test how the result changes as assumed costs vary and when hard-to-borrow names are excluded or charged their fees.

\qffield{Conditions} Practitioner account as of 2024; U.S. rules as cited in the linked entry. Narang's example ignores how often the position turns over and how costs scale with size; it shows only that a thin edge per share can be smaller than the cost. His short-availability point concerns U.S. equities. He says that shorting without locating shares ``would be considered a naked short sale, which is illegal in the United States'' (p.~186). That is broader than the rule: Regulation SHO bars a broker-dealer from accepting a short-sale order, or selling short for its own account, without a borrow, an arrangement to borrow or reasonable grounds to believe the security can be borrowed, with exceptions such as bona-fide market making, and imposes close-out duties for fails (\hyperref[mech:securities-lending-short-sale]{securities lending for short sales}).

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: Narang discusses whether shares can be borrowed, not what borrowing costs. A short backtest also needs the lending fee or rebate, which is high for securities on special (\hyperref[def:securities-lending-fee-rebate]{lending fees and rebates}), and allowance for recall and buy-in risk (\hyperref[mech:securities-lending-short-sale]{securities lending for short sales}). Neither is in the schematic comparison or in Narang's passage.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Historical hard-to-borrow lists are difficult to obtain; a reliable short-sale backtest requires point-in-time borrow data. ``Overestimation \dots\ hold positions for longer than optimal'' and ``underestimation \dots\ high turnover and bleed'' match Narang.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-analysis]{transaction cost analysis} (cost forecasts and measurement); \hyperref[def:transaction-costs]{transaction costs} (components); \hyperref[mech:securities-lending-short-sale]{securities lending for short sales} (locate and borrow); \hyperref[def:securities-lending-fee-rebate]{lending fees and rebates} (borrow cost); \hyperref[meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics]{backtest evaluation diagnostics}.

\end{qfpitfall}

\begin{qfmethod}[Designing and evaluating a strategy backtest: objectives, procedures (historical, walk-forward, cross-validated and simulated), metrics and visuals, and the problems that inflate results]\label{meth:backtest-design-evaluation}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Objectives}: a backtest estimates how a fully specified rule would have performed with the information, universe, costs and constraints available at each past decision; it tests a hypothesis stated in advance (\hyperref[meth:hypothesis-driven-strategy-research]{hypothesis-first research}) and is not a research tool for finding one \citep[pp.~151--154]{alpha-afml}.

\emph{Procedures} (Interpretation, organising \citep[pp.~161--166]{alpha-afml}): (1) fix the investment universe as it stood at each date, including names later delisted; (2) define signals using only data released before the decision, with release lags and point-in-time revisions; (3) set rebalancing frequency, portfolio construction rules and constraints; (4) model costs, borrow and capacity; (5) choose the evaluation scheme: a \emph{walk-forward} (historical) test that re-estimates on past data and records the following period, with an expanding or a rolling window; \emph{cross-validation}, purged and embargoed, which tests every period but not in time order; or \emph{simulation} of many synthetic paths, including combinatorial purged cross-validation, so that a strategy is judged on a distribution of outcomes rather than one path; (6) record every trial.

\emph{Metrics and visuals}: annualised return and volatility, Sharpe and Sortino ratios, maximum drawdown and time to recovery, hit rate, turnover and cost drag, exposures to known factors, and the number of trials; the cumulative return and drawdown (underwater) charts, rolling Sharpe ratios and return distributions (\hyperref[meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics]{diagnostics}).

\emph{Problems}: \emph{survivorship bias} (testing on today's constituents), \emph{look-ahead bias} (using data before its release), \emph{data snooping} or selection bias (reporting the best of many trials), ignoring transaction costs and short-sale constraints, outliers, the story told after the fact, and the shorting of instruments that could not be borrowed; L\'opez de Prado's ``seven sins'' (pp.~151--152). Remedies: develop models for asset classes or universes rather than single names, use bagging, record all trials and deflate the Sharpe ratio for them (\hyperref[meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio]{deflated Sharpe}), and backtest scenarios rather than a single history (p.~154).

\emph{Book accounting}. NAV is cash plus the signed market value of the positions. A long-short target holds $+1$ of NAV long and $-1$ short, a gross exposure of 2 and a net exposure of zero; short-sale proceeds stay in cash. The pre-trade holdings at a rebalance are the previous positions after their own returns, so unchanged target weights still require trades when assets have earned different returns. The cost is the cost rate times the traded notional, purchases and sales (cover-buys and short sales included), paid from cash; the first rebalance from cash pays for establishing the whole book. With targets set as fractions of post-trade NAV $V-f$, the fee solves $f=c\sum_i|w^*_i(V-f)-h_i|$. The net return of a month is then $(1-f/V)(1+w^{*\top}r)-1$. Maximum drawdown is measured on the wealth path that starts at 1, so a first-month loss counts.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Small ledgers} at $c=20$~bp. From 100 of cash, a 50/50 book pays $f=0.2/1.002=0.1996$ and leaves zero cash. After returns of $+10$ and $-10$ percent the holdings are 55 and 45; holding the targets at 50/50 means selling 5.01 and buying 4.99, a traded notional of 10 and a fee of 0.02, although successive target weights are identical. Swapping one of two longs and one of two shorts trades 2.0 of NAV and costs 0.004, leaving cash 0.996, longs 0.996 and shorts $-0.996$. Paths $(-10\%,+5\%)$ and $(-5\%)$ have drawdowns of 10 and 5 percent; a path that only rises has none.

The momentum book is established by trading 1.99 of opening NAV and then trades 1.09 of NAV a month, more than the 0.96 change in successive targets because drift is traded back; at 20~bp of traded notional the cost drag is 2.62 percent a year, and the net maximum drawdown is 50 percent against 32 percent gross. The in-sample winner's 0.83 exceeds the approximate expected maximum of 24 null Sharpe ratios over ten years (0.57), but it does not survive the next decade. Expanding-window estimates move less from year to year than five-year rolling ones.

\qffield{Conditions} Simulated data with no predictability; real markets add non-stationarity, regime changes and capacity limits that a backtest can only approximate. Cash, collateral and short rebates earn zero and there is no borrow fee or margin, so the returns are excess of cash; a proportional cost per unit traded ignores spreads that vary with size and market impact.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating a walk-forward on one path as conclusive. Reporting the best trial without the number of trials. Rerunning the out-of-sample test after changes until it passes.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting}; \hyperref[pit:backtest-cost-short-assumptions]{cost assumptions}; \hyperref[meth:purged-embargoed-cross-validation]{purged CV}; \hyperref[meth:multiple-testing-control]{multiple testing}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Standard errors of an estimated Sharpe ratio: IID returns, serial correlation and time aggregation]\label{meth:sharpe-ratio-standard-error}
\qffield{Statement} Lo derives the asymptotic distribution of the estimated Sharpe ratio $\widehat{SR}=(\hat\mu-R_f)/\hat\sigma$ by the delta method \citep[``IID Returns'', eqs.~(5)--(9), pp.~37--38]{wide-lo-sharpe}.

\emph{Lo's statement.} Lo asserts that under IID returns ``with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$'' the estimated variance satisfies $\sqrt T(\hat\sigma^2-\sigma^2)\to N(0,2\sigma^4)$, independently of $\hat\mu$ (eq.~(5), p.~38). From this he obtains $V_{\mathrm{IID}}=1+\tfrac12SR^2$ and $\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{SR})\approx\sqrt{(1+\tfrac12\widehat{SR}^2)/T}$, with a 95 percent interval $\widehat{SR}\pm1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$ (eqs.~(8)--(10), p.~38).

\emph{What the derivation needs.} Finite mean and variance are not enough for Lo's general argument, though they are for one special case.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Sufficient in general.} A finite \emph{fourth} moment and $\sigma>0$ give $\sqrt T(\hat\sigma^2-\sigma^2)$ a normal limit with variance $\nu_4-\sigma^4$, so the joint delta method applies to the smooth map $(\mu,\sigma^2)\mapsto(\mu-R_f)/\sigma$ at any $SR$.
\item \emph{Not necessary for every normal limit.} At $SR=0$, $\sqrt T\,\widehat{SR}=\sqrt T(\hat\mu-\mu)/\hat\sigma$. The central limit theorem for the mean and the consistency of $\hat\sigma$ (the law of large numbers), both of which need only a finite variance, give $\sqrt T\,\widehat{SR}\to N(0,1)$ by Slutsky's lemma. So centred IID Student-$t_3$ returns, whose fourth moment is infinite, still have a standard normal limit at $SR=0$. Away from zero the variance term enters, and without a fourth moment the $\sqrt T$-scaled variance has no finite limit (the $t_3$ check below at $SR=0.5$).
\item The general IID variance is
\[ V_{\mathrm{IID}}=1-\gamma_3\,SR+\frac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\,SR^2, \]
with $\gamma_3$ the skewness and $\gamma_4$ the kurtosis (not excess). Its middle term is the covariance of $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma^2$, which is $\nu_3/T$ and so zero only when the third central moment is zero. Its last term uses $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\sigma^2)\approx(\nu_4-\sigma^4)/T$.
\item Lo's $2\sigma^4$ needs $\nu_4=3\sigma^4$ and his asymptotic independence needs $\nu_3=0$. Normal moments ($\gamma_3=0$, $\gamma_4=3$) are sufficient for $V=1+\tfrac12SR^2$ but not necessary. The reduction holds exactly when $SR=0$ or $\gamma_4=3+4\gamma_3/SR$, so a symmetric non-normal law with kurtosis~3 gives the same $V$.
\end{itemize}
Lo himself uses the third and fourth moments $\nu_3,\nu_4$ for the robust estimator under IID returns and sets $\nu_3=0$, $\nu_4=3\sigma^4$ ``now suppose that returns are normally distributed'' (eqs.~(25)--(26), p.~42). The finite-variance wording at eq.~(5) is therefore an overstatement in the source; its formulas are quoted unchanged above. The general expression is the one in the \hyperref[meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio]{deflated Sharpe ratio}'s eq.~(2). With 60 observations the standard error is 0.188 at a true Sharpe ratio of 1.50 and 0.303 at 3.00, in the units of the observation period (p.~38).

For non-IID returns (serial correlation, conditional heteroskedasticity) the variance comes from a GMM estimator, $\mathrm{SE}\approx\sqrt{\hat V_{\mathrm{GMM}}/T}$, under stationarity, using Hansen's (1982) GMM results (eqs.~(13)--(15), pp.~39--40); Appendix~A estimates the covariance by Newey and West's (1987) procedure. \emph{Time aggregation}. Lo defines the $q$-period return as the \emph{sum} of one-period returns, ``ignor[ing] the effects of compounding'' (eq.~(16), p.~40); his note~7 (p.~51) adds that the sum is exact for continuously compounded returns, and says $SR(q)=\sqrt q\,SR$ ``is correct only under the assumption of IID returns'' (p.~40). For additive returns and a benchmark aggregated the same way, independence is not needed: a constant mean and variance with zero autocovariances suffice, since the variance of the sum is then $q\sigma^2$. For stationary returns with autocorrelations $\rho_k$,
\[ SR(q)=\eta(q)\,SR,\qquad \eta(q)=\frac{q}{\sqrt{q+2\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}(q-k)\rho_k}} \]
(eqs.~(17)--(20), p.~40), with a closed form for AR(1) returns (eq.~(22), p.~41). A monthly first-order autocorrelation of $-20$ percent gives an annual factor of 4.17, against 3.46 under IID returns and 2.88 at $+20$ percent (Table~2, p.~41). The scaling is exact only for these additive quantities. For compounded simple returns over a compounded benchmark it is an approximation: with IID lognormal monthly returns (log mean 0.8 percent, volatility 4.5 percent, benchmark 0.2 percent), the exact annual Sharpe ratio is 0.515, against $\sqrt{12}\times0.155=0.538$.

\qffield{Notation} $T$ the number of return observations; $SR$ the Sharpe ratio per observation period; $q$ the aggregation length (12 for monthly to annual); $\rho_k$ the $k$th autocorrelation of returns.

\qffield{Conditions} Timeless (statistics), published 2002. Asymptotic: the approximations are large-$T$ results \citep[p.~38]{wide-lo-sharpe}. The general $V_{\mathrm{IID}}$ is derived for IID returns with a finite fourth moment and $\sigma>0$; at $SR=0$ a finite variance suffices for the $N(0,1)$ limit. $1+\tfrac12SR^2$ holds under normal third and fourth moments, and more generally whenever $\gamma_4=3+4\gamma_3/SR$. The GMM version relaxes independence but needs stationarity and finite moments for its central limit theorem. The $\sqrt q$ rule needs additive returns, a consistently aggregated benchmark, a constant mean and variance, and zero autocovariances.

\emph{Counterexamples}, independent of the entry's algebra:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Skewed returns.} For IID two-point returns with Sharpe ratio $0.22$, skewness $-2.7$ and kurtosis $8.1$, Monte Carlo gives $T\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{SR})=1.70$, matching the general $1.68$, not $1+\tfrac12SR^2=1.02$.
\item \emph{Infinite fourth moment.} For Student-$t_3$ returns (finite variance, infinite fourth moment) with $SR=0.5$, $T\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{SR})$ rises from 2.5 to 10.0 as $T$ goes from 250 to 16{,}000, so it has no finite limit. Centred at $SR=0$, the same returns give $\operatorname{Var}(\sqrt T\,\widehat{SR})=0.99$ and a two-sided 5 percent tail frequency of 4.8 percent at $T=10{,}000$, as the zero-$SR$ limit predicts.
\item \emph{Normal moments not necessary.} The three-point law $\{-\sqrt3,0,\sqrt3\}$ with probabilities $\{1/6,2/3,1/6\}$ (skewness 0, kurtosis 3, not normal), shifted to $SR=0.5$, gives $T\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{SR})=1.11$ against $1+\tfrac12(0.5)^2=1.125$.
\item \emph{Dependent but uncorrelated returns.} An uncorrelated but dependent process, $e_t=z_tz_{t-1}$, obeys the $\sqrt{12}$ rule.
\end{itemize}
A finite fourth moment is sufficient for the general expansion, not necessary in every special case; the expression $1+\tfrac12SR^2$ is not exclusive to normal distributions. The scale factor needs the first $q-1$ autocorrelations, themselves estimated. $\eta(q)$ from eq.~(20) and eq.~(22) agree, and eq.~(22) reproduces the Table~2 cells to two decimals; eq.~(9) reproduces the 0.188 and 0.303.

\qffield{Practice} Positive serial correlation makes multi-period variance grow faster than $q$, so $\sqrt{q}$ annualisation overstates the annual ratio; Lo's hedge-fund example finds annual Sharpe ratios ``overstated by as much as 65 percent'' and rankings that ``can change dramatically'' once serial correlation is accounted for (abstract, p.~36). As a fraction of the ratio, the normal-moment standard error tends to $\sqrt{1/(2T)}$ as $SR$ grows (eq.~(11), p.~38); with general moments the limit is $\sqrt{(\gamma_4-1)/(4T)}$. Either way, a high Sharpe ratio is no more precisely known in relative terms.

\emph{Comparing two Sharpe ratios}. Ledoit and Wolf observe that the common test of Jobson and Korkie, as corrected by Memmel, ``is not valid when returns have tails heavier than the normal distribution or are of time series nature'', because its delta-method variance assumes i.i.d.\ normal returns (abstract and secs.~1--2, PDF pp.~2--4). They work with the means and uncentred second moments of the two return series, take a HAC standard error for $\widehat{Sh}_i-\widehat{Sh}_n$, and recommend instead a studentized circular block bootstrap interval, declaring the ratios different when zero lies outside it; the block size is calibrated by simulation (their Algorithm~3.1). The bootstrap resamples the observed pairs, so no null-restricted data are needed. This is the paired test for comparing two strategies; it is not a correction for having tried many strategies.

\qffield{Pitfalls} A standard error for one strategy is not a test of the best of many: selecting the maximum over trials needs \hyperref[meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio]{deflation}.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:backtest-evaluation-diagnostics]{backtest evaluation diagnostics} (where the point estimate is reported); \hyperref[meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio]{deflated Sharpe ratio}; \hyperref[def:return-measures]{return measures}.

\qffield{Further reading} Ledoit and Wolf's test of Sharpe-ratio differences; the stationary bootstrap.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[The deflated Sharpe ratio: the probability that the selected strategy's true Sharpe ratio is positive, given the number of trials, their dispersion and non-normal returns]\label{meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio}
\qffield{Statement} For $N$ independent trials whose estimated Sharpe ratios $\{\widehat{SR}_n\}$ are normal with mean $E[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]$ and variance $V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]$, the expected maximum for $N\gg1$ is approximately \citep[eq.~(1), p.~7]{alpha-dsr}
\[ E[\max\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]\approx E[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]+\sqrt{V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]}\Big((1-\gamma)Z^{-1}\big[1-\tfrac1N\big]+\gamma Z^{-1}\big[1-\tfrac1Ne^{-1}\big]\Big), \]
with $\gamma\approx0.5772$ the Euler--Mascheroni constant and $Z$ the standard normal distribution function. The deflated Sharpe ratio is the probabilistic Sharpe ratio evaluated at the threshold that this maximum takes when the true Sharpe ratio is zero (eq.~(2), p.~8):
\[ \widehat{DSR}=Z\!\left[\frac{(\widehat{SR}-\widehat{SR}_0)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1-\hat\gamma_3\widehat{SR}+\frac{\hat\gamma_4-1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\right],\quad \widehat{SR}_0=\sqrt{V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]}\Big((1-\gamma)Z^{-1}\big[1-\tfrac1N\big]+\gamma Z^{-1}\big[1-\tfrac1Ne^{-1}\big]\Big), \]
where $\widehat{SR}$, $T$, $\hat\gamma_3$ (skewness) and $\hat\gamma_4$ (kurtosis) belong to the selected strategy. ``Essentially, DSR is a PSR where the rejection threshold is adjusted to reflect the multiplicity of trials'' (p.~9).

\qffield{Notation} All Sharpe ratios in the formula are per observation period, not annualised; $\hat\gamma_4$ is kurtosis (3 for the normal), not excess kurtosis.

\qffield{Conditions} The threshold assumes the trials' Sharpe ratios are normally distributed around a common mean and that $N$ counts \emph{independent} trials \citep[p.~7]{alpha-dsr}; correlated configurations must be reduced to an effective number, which the formula does not supply. \emph{The authors' example} (pp.~9--10): $N=100$ independent trials, $V=\tfrac12$ (annualised), $T=1250$ daily returns, skewness $-3$, kurtosis 10 and a selected annualised Sharpe ratio of 2.5 give $\widehat{SR}_0\approx0.1132$ (daily, 250 observations a year) and $\widehat{DSR}\approx0.9004<0.95$; after only 46 trials it would have been 0.9505, and with normal returns 0.9505 after 88. All four numbers are reproduced.

\qffield{Practice} For a deflated Sharpe ratio over many recorded variants, the variance across trials and the trial count therefore have to be logged from the first experiment. The authors also suggest planning trials in advance and an optimal-stopping rule, sampling about 37 percent of the justifiable configurations first (pp.~10--11), which is a heuristic, not part of the statistic. They describe Harvey and Liu's false-discovery threshold as complementary and suggest computing the statistic on both thresholds (p.~9).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Parameter stability does not deflate anything (\hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting}). Interpretation: $\widehat{DSR}$ is a probability under the stated assumptions, not the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio; a large undisclosed search, or an $N$ that ignores discarded families, overstates it. The 2026 unification by López de Prado and Porcu (\nolinkurl{w4-lopezdeprado-porcu-dsr}, \emph{JPM}, online 26 September 2026) is not covered here. Locators refer to the author version of 31 July 2014, not the published \emph{JPM} version.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:sharpe-ratio-standard-error]{Sharpe-ratio standard errors}; \hyperref[meth:purged-embargoed-cross-validation]{purged cross-validation} (the other failure of reused test sets); \hyperref[meth:skill-luck-significance-tests]{tests of skill against luck}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{wide-bailey-sharpe-frontier}, on the probabilistic Sharpe ratio and the minimum track-record length.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Controlling false discoveries across a family of strategies: familywise error, Bonferroni and Holm, the Reality Check, and Romano and Wolf's stepwise method]\label{meth:multiple-testing-control}
\qffield{Statement} Romano and Wolf set up $K$ strategies compared with a common benchmark, testing $H_{0,k}:\theta_k\le0$ against $\theta_k>0$ for each, where $\theta_k$ is, for example, a difference in mean return or Sharpe ratio. They seek \emph{strong} control of the familywise error rate, the probability of rejecting at least one true null whatever the configuration of true and false nulls \citep[working paper of October 2003, sec.~2.2, pp.~6--7]{wide-romano-wolf-stepdown}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Bonferroni} rejects $H_{0,k}$ if $p_k<\alpha/K$.
\item \emph{Holm} orders the $p$-values and rejects $H_{0,(k)}$ if $p_{(j)}<\alpha/(K-j+1)$ for all $j\le k$. Both control the error rate but are conservative, ``assuming a worst-case dependence structure'' (sec.~2.3, pp.~7--8).
\item \emph{White's Reality Check} uses the $1-\alpha$ quantile of $\max_k(w_k-\theta_k)$, the largest deviation of the test statistics from their true values, to decide only whether the \emph{best} strategy beats the benchmark (p.~9).
\item \emph{StepM} (Algorithm~3.1, pp.~9--10) relabels strategies by descending statistic. It rejects every $H_{0,r_k}$ whose interval $[w_{r_k}-c,\infty)$ excludes zero, with $c$ that max-quantile over the strategies not yet rejected, and repeats on the remainder until nothing more is rejected. The quantiles are estimated by a bootstrap of the data (Algorithm~3.2), which for time series is a block or stationary bootstrap (Scenario~3.2, p.~11). A studentized version (Algorithm~4.1) is advocated when feasible.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Notation} $w_k$ the test statistic for strategy $k$ (for example $\sqrt T$ times an estimated difference); $\alpha$ the error level; FWE the familywise error rate; FDR the false discovery rate.

\qffield{Conditions} Methods published 1995--2005. StepM ``asymptotically controls the FWE'' and rejects every false null with probability tending to one, provided the sampling distribution of $\sqrt T(W_T-\theta)$ has a continuous non-degenerate limit and the bootstrap estimates it consistently (Assumption~3.1 and Theorem~3.1, pp.~10--11). With time series this needs stationarity, moment and mixing conditions, and a time-series bootstrap. The \emph{false discovery rate} is a less strict target that will ``discover'' more strategies, a known fraction of them falsely, as Romano and Wolf summarise it (p.~3).

\emph{The original papers}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Benjamini and Hochberg} define $Q=V/(V+S)$, ``the proportion of the rejected null hypotheses which are erroneously rejected'' ($Q=0$ when nothing is rejected), and the FDR as $E(Q)$. When all nulls are true the FDR equals the familywise error rate, so controlling it controls the FWER in the weak sense; otherwise it is no larger \citep[sec.~2.1, p.~291]{wide-benjamini-hochberg}. Their step-up procedure rejects $H_{(1)},\dots,H_{(k)}$ for the largest $k$ with $P_{(k)}\le(k/m)q^*$. Theorem~1 proves FDR control at $q^*$ ``for independent test statistics and for any configuration of false null hypotheses'' (sec.~3, eq.~(1), p.~293). Dependent statistics, the usual case for strategies on one market, are outside that theorem.
\item \emph{White} tests ``the null hypothesis that the best model encountered in a specification search has no predictive superiority over a given benchmark model'', posed as the intersection of $l$ one-sided hypotheses \citep[abstract and sec.~1, pp.~1097--1098]{catalogue-0731}. The statistic is $\max_k n^{1/2}\bar f_k$. Its null distribution is taken at the configuration ``least favorable to the alternative'', $E f_k=0$ for every $k$ (sec.~2, p.~1102). The Bootstrap Reality Check resamples with Politis and Romano's stationary bootstrap, blocks of geometric length with mean $1/q$ (p.~1104), with $q=1$ when the loss differentials are a martingale difference sequence under the null (sec.~3, p.~1109).
\end{itemize}
Both statements match Romano and Wolf's account above. One consequence is the entry's own reading, not White's text: pricing the null at $E f_k=0$ for all $k$ makes the test conservative when many clearly inferior strategies are included.

\qffield{Practice} Own Monte Carlo, with 20 equicorrelated statistics ($\rho=0.8$), 5 false nulls and $\alpha=5$ percent: the error rates are 0.014 (Bonferroni), 0.017 (Holm), 0.043 (single-step max) and 0.046 (StepM), all within the level, and the shares of false nulls rejected are 0.57, 0.58, 0.73 and 0.74. This is the power that dependence-aware methods gain, as the paper argues. For a research log, the family must be every strategy tried, including discarded ones, as for the \hyperref[meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio]{deflated Sharpe ratio}.

\emph{Hansen's test of superior predictive ability}. Hansen argues that pricing the null at the least favourable point, $\mu_k=0$ for all $k$, makes the Reality Check ``an asymptotically biased test'' that ``is sensitive to the inclusion of poor and irrelevant models'' \citep[abstract and sec.~1, PDF pp.~2--3]{hansen-spa-2001-wp}. His remedy keeps a model's estimated mean in the null distribution only if it is not clearly negative: $\hat\mu^c_k=\bar X_{n,k}$ if $\bar X_{n,k}<-A_{n,k}$ and $0$ otherwise, with thresholds that vanish but more slowly than the law-of-the-iterated-logarithm rate, so that poor models drop out as $n\to\infty$ and the $p$-values are consistent (sec.~``A Consistent Estimate of the Asymptotic Distribution'', printed pp.~25--26). This bears on the entry's own remark above: the conservatism from including clearly inferior strategies is the bias Hansen's correction removes.

\emph{How high a hurdle for a published factor} (\nolinkurl{alpha-harvey}, NBER WP~20592, October 2014). Harvey, Liu and Zhu count 316 factors in published work and argue that ``a newly discovered factor needs to clear a much higher hurdle, with a $t$-ratio greater than 3.0'' (abstract, PDF p.~2). The hurdle depends on the method and the count: a Holm adjustment gives 3.64 at 316 factors and 3.29 if the count is cut to 113 ``common'' factors (sec.~4, PDF p.~26); across their correlation specifications, $t$-ratios of 3.9 and 3.0 control the familywise error rate at 5 percent and the false discovery rate at 1 percent (PDF p.~34). These are thresholds for the population of published factors, which omits the unpublished trials; they do not replace counting a researcher's own trials.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Controlling the error rate across the strategies tested says nothing about strategies tried and not recorded. The guarantees are asymptotic and depend on the bootstrap's validity for the dependence present. Section and page numbers refer to the 2003 working paper, not the 2005 \emph{Econometrica} version.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:deflated-sharpe-ratio]{deflated Sharpe ratio}; \hyperref[meth:sharpe-ratio-standard-error]{Sharpe-ratio standard errors}; \hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting}; \hyperref[meth:skill-luck-significance-tests]{tests of skill against luck}.

\qffield{Further reading} Romano and Wolf's \emph{Econometrica} version of 2005 and Hansen's \emph{JBES} version of 2005.

\end{qfmethod}
