\section{Point-in-time reference and market data}

\begin{qfdata}[Trading reference data: universe, symbology, corporate actions, static and exchange data, condition codes, special days, futures and options reference, event calendars, related and composite instruments]\label{data:trading-reference-data}
\qffield{Statement} Velu, Hardy and Nehren call reliable reference data ``the key foundation of a robust quantitative strategy development'' and list what a research platform must hold \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.4.1, pp.~26--35]{s3-velu}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item the \emph{trading universe}, which changes daily with listings and delistings; knowing when a stock stopped trading avoids survivor bias (p.~26);
\item \emph{symbology mapping} (ISIN, SEDOL, RIC, Bloomberg tickers) and \emph{ticker changes}: the mapping ``needs to persist as point-in-time data and allow for historical `as of date' usage, requiring the implementation of a bi-temporal data structure'', handling changes backward and forward (pp.~26--27);
\item \emph{corporate actions}: dividends (announcement and payment dates), splits, reverse splits, rights offers, mergers, spin-offs, changes in free float or shares outstanding, suspensions; prices and volumes must be adjusted backward for splits, and suspensions leave gaps that affect backtests (pp.~27--28);
\item \emph{static data}: country, sector (GICS, ICB, TOPIX's 33 sectors), primary exchange, quotation currency and quote factor, with USD price $=$ local price $\times$ FX $\times$ quote factor and cent-quoted currencies such as GBp (pp.~28--29);
\item \emph{exchange data}: holiday calendars, session hours with auctions, lunch breaks and daylight-saving shifts, disrupted days, tick sizes, trade and quote lots, limit-up and limit-down rules and short-sale restrictions, whose values must be kept historically ``in order to run realistic historical backtests'' (pp.~29--30);
\item \emph{condition codes} on trades and quotes, mapped so that, for example, off-exchange trades reported for regulatory purposes are excluded from accessible liquidity and from daily volume, high and low (p.~31);
\item \emph{special-day calendars}: half days, local holidays and exam days, month and quarter ends, index rebalance days with volume skewed to the close, and expiry days (pp.~31--32);
\item \emph{futures and options reference data}: expiry calendars, the most liquid contract, roll dates, contract size and quotation, session phases, and option chains (pp.~32--34);
\item \emph{event calendars} for macro releases and company events, \emph{related tickers} (composite tickers, dual listings, ADRs and GDRs), \emph{composite assets} with their constituents, cash components and divisors, and \emph{latency tables} between data centres (pp.~34--35).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Every table that changes over time must be stored with the dates on which each value applied and was known, so that a backtest sees the universe, identifiers, corporate actions, tick sizes and rules in force at each decision time. Velu, Hardy and Nehren's reasons for special days are practical: a single special day moves a 30- or 60-day ADV, and low-order autoregressive models underperform on and after such days, so modellers ``often remove special days and model normal days first'' and model special days separately (p.~32). Futures liquidity moves between contracts at the roll (about 1.5 million E-mini contracts a day in the front month against 30,000 in the next before expiry, reversing in the last days, p.~33), so rolling-window statistics must blend contracts across roll dates.

\qffield{Practice} Velu, Hardy and Nehren's ABC$\to$DEF example (p.~27) shows the three queries a symbology table must answer: a 30-day history as of a date before the change (old ticker), after it (both tickers spliced), and a forward window for a position held across it. Examples they give: European equity volume 30--40 percent lower on U.S. holidays; Indonesia's longer Friday lunch break; U.S. daylight saving starting about two weeks before Europe's (pp.~29--30). These are illustrations as of the book's writing, not current calendars.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes merged Velu, Hardy and Nehren's dividend points, attributing the price jump to the announcement: they separate the drop in price when the dividend is paid from the volatility that may accompany the announcement (p.~27); interpretation, the price drop occurs on the ex-dividend date. The legacy said merger and spin-off data are ``hence used in'' merger arbitrage, where the source says such situations ``can also be exploited'' (p.~28), and dropped the requirement to keep exchange-rule histories. The book describes what to collect; it does not test how much results change when it is ignored. That evidence (delisting returns, history rewriting, survivorship) is in the pathway's readings.

\begin{qfdata}[Market data levels: Level I trades and quotes, Level II depth, Level III order messages, and the special order types that complicate them]\label{data:market-data-levels}
\qffield{Statement} Market data are sold ``in various levels of granularity (and price!)'', from exchanges as direct feeds or from vendors \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.4.2, p.~35]{s3-velu}. \emph{Level I} holds trades and top-of-book quotes, enough to rebuild the best bid and offer; quotes update roughly ten times as often as trades in liquid markets, and trade status and qualifiers (cancelled, late, odd lot, auction, intermarket sweep, average-price, venue) decide whether a print should update the last price and cumulative volume (pp.~35--36). \emph{Level II} adds every lit order-book update at every level and venue; many users keep five or ten levels (p.~36). \emph{Level III}, message data, gives each order an identifier tracked through execution, cancellation or amendment; once messages from all exchanges are ``consolidated into one single dataset ordered by timestamp'', a full book can be built at any moment (pp.~36--37). Their example of a U.S. Level~III record has timestamp (milliseconds after midnight), ticker, order identifier, message type (add buy, add sell, partial execution, partial cancel, full execution, delete, cross, non-displayed execution), shares, price (the last four digits decimals, so ``divide by 10000''), participant identifier and market centre (Table~1.10, p.~37).

\qffield{Conditions} Exchange timestamps are matching-engine times; databases also store a reception time, and the latency between them must be accounted for when opportunities are short-lived (p.~36). Special order types change identifiers and priority in venue-specific ways: price-sliding orders ranked at the locking price as hidden and displayed one tick away; pegged and midpoint-peg orders; reserve orders whose displayed part is replenished with a new timestamp; discretionary orders; intermarket sweep orders (pp.~37--38). Velu, Hardy and Nehren advise weighing the value of raw data against simpler binned data, and note that most practitioners outside high-frequency trading build strategies on binned data (pp.~36 and~39).

\qffield{Practice} With Level~III data one can model inter-arrival times, arrival and cancellation rates by distance from the touch, queue position and imbalance, and then ask about the impact of market orders, a limit order's chance of moving up the queue, the probability of earning the spread and the short-horizon direction of prices (p.~38).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes said to divide the price field by 1000; the table says 10000 (p.~37). They dropped the consolidation condition for a full book, and the reception-time latency. The Table~1.10 layout is the book's illustration; a real feed's specification, which changes, governs.

\begin{qfdata}[Derived market statistics: daily activity data, intraday microstructure statistics, pre-computed aggregates and binned data]\label{data:derived-market-statistics}
\qffield{Statement} Velu, Hardy and Nehren group the statistics most strategies derive from binned or daily data \citep[chap.~1, sec.~1.4.3, pp.~39--42]{s3-velu}. \emph{Daily activity}: open, high, low, close and previous close (adjusted for corporate actions), the last trade before the close, volume with its lit and dark split, auction volume and price, VWAP, short interest, days-to-cover and borrow utilisation, futures open interest and basis, index-level data, options open interest, Greeks and implied volatility, and cross-asset rates, CDS spreads and the dollar index (pp.~39--40). \emph{Intraday microstructure}: number and frequency of trades and quote updates, top-of-book size, depth, spread, trade size and ticking time, whose daily distributions can start an algorithm's day and be updated ``through online Bayesian updates'' (p.~41). \emph{Pre-computed aggregates}: $X$-day ADV and auction volume, volatility, beta (plain or asymmetric) and correlation matrices (p.~41). \emph{Binned data} discretise the day into bins of a few seconds to 30 minutes with the same features; volume and spread profiles ``are rarely built with a granularity finer than one minute'' to avoid noise from frictions (p.~42).

\qffield{Conditions} Aggregates ``need to fully support the peculiarities described in the Reference Data section'': special days skew distributions, and asynchronous markets distort beta and correlation (p.~41). Binned data allow discrete time-series methods and reduce dimension (390 one-minute rows per U.S. stock-day against millions of messages), but relationships stable on daily closes become noisier at higher frequency, and illiquid names leave empty bins (p.~42). Interpretation: each derived field must carry the time it became available, for example short interest only after its publication date, and pre-computed $X$-day aggregates must be computed through the decision date, not the end of the sample.

\qffield{Practice} Velu, Hardy and Nehren's uses are judgements to test, not results: the high--low range ``usually gives a better indication of market sentiment than the simple close-to-close return''; ``a large short interest usually indicates a bearish view from institutional investors''; days-to-cover sizes a potential short squeeze; futures basis against one's dividend estimates may show mispricing (pp.~39--40).

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy notes headed the daily-activity list ``Binned Data'', which in the source is a different passage (p.~42), and dropped the reference-data caveat on aggregates.
