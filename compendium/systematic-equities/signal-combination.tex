\section{Combining alpha signals}

\begin{qfmethod}[Combining alpha signals into one forecast: linear weights, conditional and rotational models, machine learning, or separate factor portfolios]\label{meth:alpha-signal-combination}
\qffield{Statement} Narang treats blending as the problem of turning several alpha models' forecasts for an instrument into one composite forecast, which then goes to portfolio construction \citep[chap.~3, ``Alpha Models: How Quants Make Money'', sec.~3.5, pp.~64--68]{s7-narang}. He names four approaches:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{linear models}, ``by far the most common'': each factor contributes additively, its weight usually set by multiple regression on past returns. In his example a yield factor forecasting $+20$ percent at weight 0.7 and a trend factor forecasting $-10$ percent at weight 0.3 give $0.7\times20+0.3\times(-10)=+11$ percent (pp.~65--66; checked). Equal weights, equal-risk weights and discretionary weights are special cases (p.~66);
\item \emph{nonlinear models}: \emph{conditional} models, where one factor's reading sets another's weight, for example acting on value only when momentum agrees, which in his example gives no signal, or $+20$ percent if the trend agreed (Exhibit~3.10, pp.~66--67, page image); and \emph{rotation} models, whose weights are refitted on recent data and ``usually'' favour the factors that have done best recently, ``a form of trend following in the timing of alpha factors'' (p.~67);
\item \emph{machine-learning} models that choose the weights, which he calls ``both more common and more successful'' than machine learning used to forecast markets (pp.~67--68);
\item not combining at all: building one portfolio per alpha model and combining those portfolios, as in \hyperref[meth:signal-portfolio-allocation]{signal-portfolio allocation} (p.~64).
\end{itemize}
Signal mixing produces one forecast per instrument; portfolio construction then sizes positions from forecasts, risk and costs (p.~68).

\qffield{Conditions} Narang's argument for blending is that two partly right forecasts can together be right more often (Exhibit~3.9, p.~65), and that ``any reasonably intelligent combination of alphas'' is likely to beat any one of them over time (p.~64). Interpretation: that holds when the forecasts each carry information and their errors are not highly correlated; combining a weak forecast with a strong one, or two near-duplicates, need not help, and weights fitted to past returns carry estimation error. His Exhibit~3.9 is a diagram, not evidence.

\qffield{Practice} Interpretation: ``linear'' describes the form of the combination, not the factors. A linear model assumes each factor's contribution does not depend on the others (no interaction), which is what Narang means by inclusion being ``independent'' (p.~65); it does not need the factors to be statistically independent. Correlated factors can be combined linearly, though highly correlated ones make regression weights unstable. A conditional rule is an interaction; a rotation rule is a time-varying weight. Combining signals into one forecast before optimising, or optimising on the signals separately, is the mixing-against-integrating choice that this pathway's checkpoint asks to compare.

\emph{Evidence on that choice} (batch of 2026-10-17). Fitzgibbons, Friedman, Pomorski and Serban, of AQR, contrast two long-only constructions \citep[\emph{Journal of Investing} 26(4), Winter 2017, AQR-hosted reprint, pp.~1, 7 and~9]{eq-fitzgibbons-2017}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the ``portfolio mix'' combines single-style portfolios;
\item the ``integrated portfolio'' first combines the style scores and builds one portfolio.
\end{itemize}
They test value and momentum on a universe similar to MSCI World, February 1993 to December 2015, rebalanced monthly. Both are held at about 4 percent tracking error, net of estimated transaction costs and gross of fees (Exhibit~5). The integrated portfolio earned 3.6 percent a year over the benchmark, against 2.5 for the mix, an information ratio of 0.9 against 0.6. Its transfer coefficient, the ex ante correlation between portfolio and model, was 0.6 against 0.3.

They attribute the gain to better capture of the styles, not to better stocks, and expect larger gains with negatively correlated styles, higher risk or more styles. The sample is the authors' own backtest, by a firm that runs such products. Their claim of ``highly statistically significant'' differences is not shown in the passages read, and a later critique arguing the approaches cannot be distinguished after multiple-testing adjustments was not read.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The legacy ``linear models: require independence of factors'' misreads Narang's additivity as statistical independence. Rotation by recent performance is a bet that factor performance trends; Narang gives no evidence for it, and refitting weights on recent data counts as further trials (\hyperref[pit:parameter-overfitting]{parameter overfitting}). His claim that machine learning works better for weighting than for forecasting is a judgement without data in the chapter. He refers to Chapter~7 for portfolio construction techniques (p.~64), but they are in Chapter~6; Chapter~7 is on execution. The legacy block's third item, on the cost of machine-learning strategies and ``microscopic alpha'', comes from López de Prado rather than Narang and is in \hyperref[meth:strategy-production-lifecycle]{the strategy production lifecycle}.

\begin{qfmethod}[Scaling a raw signal into a return forecast: alpha $=$ volatility $\times$ IC $\times$ score, and combining two correlated signals]\label{meth:forecast-scaling-ic}
\qffield{Statement} Grinold and Kahn's basic forecasting formula takes the refined forecast to be the change in expected return from observing the raw forecasts $g$, $E\{r\mid g\}-E\{r\}=\operatorname{Cov}\{r,g\}\operatorname{Var}^{-1}\{g\}(g-E\{g\})$, derived in the chapter's appendix \citep[chap.~10, eqs.~(10.1)--(10.2), pp.~262--263]{alpha-grinold}. For one asset and one forecast it becomes $\phi=\operatorname{Std}\{r\}\cdot\operatorname{Corr}\{r,g\}\cdot(g-E\{g\})/\operatorname{Std}\{g\}$ (eq.~(10.9), p.~265), the forecasting rule of thumb
\[ \text{refined forecast}=\text{volatility}\cdot\mathrm{IC}\cdot\text{score}\qquad\text{(eq.~(10.11), p.~266),} \]
where the score standardises the raw forecast by its own mean and standard deviation and the information coefficient (IC) is the correlation between forecast and return. The same result follows from a time-series regression of $r$ on $g$, with sample estimates in place of the true moments (eqs.~(10.12)--(10.15), pp.~266--267).

\qffield{Notation} $r$ excess (or residual) return over the forecast horizon; $g$ the raw forecast in its own units; score $=(g-E\{g\})/\operatorname{Std}\{g\}$; volatility $=\operatorname{Std}\{r\}$ over the same horizon. The IC and score are dimensionless and the volatility carries the units of return (p.~268).

\qffield{Conditions} \emph{Projection, not in general a conditional mean.} What the appendix proves, as its Proposition~1, is that the formula is the \emph{best linear projection}: among forecasts of the form $a+Bg$, it is the unbiased one with the smallest mean squared error \citep[chap.~10, Technical Appendix, pp.~287--289]{alpha-grinold}. This needs only finite second moments and a nonsingular $\operatorname{Var}\{g\}$. The book says it uses the notation $E\{r\mid g\}$ ``somewhat loosely'' (n.~1, p.~263).

Equality with the conditional expectation $E\{r\mid g\}$ needs that conditional mean to be affine in $g$. That holds when $\{r,g\}$ are jointly normal (the appendix's Proposition~2, which then also gives the conditional variance and minimum mean squared error among all unbiased estimates) or, more generally, elliptically distributed; otherwise it is an extra assumption.

The covariance conditions differ in strength. The projection's error is \emph{uncorrelated} with $g$, so $\operatorname{Cov}\{r-\phi,g\}=0$. The conditional mean's error is \emph{mean-independent} of $g$, so $E\{r-E\{r\mid g\}\mid g\}=0$, and is therefore uncorrelated with every function of $g$.

So $\mathrm{IC}=0$ makes the \emph{linear} refined forecast zero (p.~266); it does not mean $g$ carries no information. Counterexample: with $g\sim N(0,1)$ and $r=g^2-1+\varepsilon$, $\operatorname{Cov}\{r,g\}=E\{g^3\}=0$, so the IC is zero, yet $E\{r\mid g\}=g^2-1$ explains $2/(2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2)$ of the return variance, two thirds at $\sigma_\varepsilon=1$. Own check, by closed form and simulation: \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/equity-long-short-construction/batch50-projection-checks.py}.

The formula also needs the forecast's mean and standard deviation, the return volatility and the IC, all estimated in practice, and sample means of returns ``generally contain a great deal of sample error'' (n.~2, p.~267). In the binary-model example the quarterly volatility is 9 percent and the forecast shares 3 of its 16 unit components with the return, so $\mathrm{IC}=3/(9\cdot4)=0.0833$ and the refined forecast is $0.75\times$score: within $\pm0.75$ percent two quarters in three and outside $\pm1.50$ percent one quarter in twenty, if scores are normal (eqs.~(10.6)--(10.10), pp.~264--266). If $\mathrm{IC}=0$ the linear refined forecast is zero (p.~266).

\qffield{Practice} The scaling controls for expectations (the score subtracts the expected raw forecast), skill (the IC) and volatility: of two stocks with the same score and skill, the more volatile receives the larger alpha (p.~268). Cross-sectionally the scores should have mean near 0 and standard deviation near 1 at each date, as should each stock's scores over time (p.~268); Table~10.1 applies an IC of 0.09 to residual volatilities and simulated scores for the Major Market Index of December 1992 (p.~269). \emph{Two signals.} With a second forecast $g'$ ($\operatorname{Var}=25$, $\operatorname{Cov}\{r,g'\}=4$, $\operatorname{Cov}\{g,g'\}=5$) the regression weights are 0.1467 and 0.1307 and the combined IC is 0.1090 (pp.~269--271); for uncorrelated forecasts the refined forecasts add and the squared ICs add, and for perfectly correlated ones the second adds nothing (p.~270). Own check: the IC, the 0.75 multiplier, the two weights, the combined IC and Table~10.1's alphas to the rounding of the printed scores are recomputed in \nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/equity-long-short-construction/batch40-forecast-checks.py}.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Interpretation: ``bigger signal, bigger bet'' (\hyperref[meth:alpha-signal-combination]{signal combination}) needs this calibration first; a raw score is not a return forecast, and forecasts on different signals or volatilities are not comparable until scaled. The rule does not by itself size positions: portfolio construction still trades forecasts against risk and costs. The source read is the book's second edition (McGraw-Hill, 1999 copyright, local PDF); Grinold's 1994 paper (\texttt{eq-grinold-1994}), which the pathway names for this method, is behind the publisher's login and was not obtained, and his 1989 fundamental-law paper (\texttt{s7-grinold}) was not read.
