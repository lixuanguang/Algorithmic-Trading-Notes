\section{Option pricing and the implied volatility surface}

\begin{qfmodel}[The Black--Scholes--Merton model: the hedging argument, the differential equation and formula, the assumptions it needs, Merton's no-early-exercise result and the Greeks]\label{mod:black-scholes-merton}
\qffield{Statement} Black and Scholes value an option under ``ideal conditions'' \citep[``The Valuation Formula'', pp.~640--641]{s6-blackscholes}:
\begin{itemize}
\item a known, constant short rate;
\item a stock price following a random walk in continuous time with variance rate proportional to the squared price, so prices at any horizon are lognormal;
\item no dividends;
\item a European option;
\item no transaction costs;
\item borrowing any fraction of a security's price at the short rate, and short selling without penalty.
\end{itemize}
A position long one share and short $1/w_1$ options is then riskless over a short interval if continuously adjusted, so it must earn the short rate. That gives the differential equation and, with the payoff $w(x,t^*)=\max(x-c,0)$, the unique solution \citep[eqs.~(7), (8) and~(13), pp.~643--644]{s6-blackscholes}:
\[ w_2=rw-rxw_1-\tfrac12v^2x^2w_{11},\qquad w(x,t)=xN(d_1)-ce^{r(t-t^*)}N(d_2),\quad d_{1,2}=\frac{\ln(x/c)+(r\pm\frac12v^2)(t^*-t)}{v\sqrt{t^*-t}}. \]
The stock's expected return does not appear (p.~644). In Hull's notation, for a stock with continuous yield $q$, $c=S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$ and $p=Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2)-S_0e^{-qT}N(-d_1)$ \citep[chap.~15, eqs.~(15.20)--(15.21), p.~352, for $q=0$; the yield version through chap.~17 and Table~19.6, p.~439]{s7-hulloptions}.

\emph{Merton's bound and early exercise.} Without payouts to the stock over the option's life, or with protection against them, a European call satisfies $f(S,\tau;E)\ge\max[0,S-EP(\tau)]$, with $P(\tau)$ the price of a riskless discount bond (Theorem~1). Then ``an American warrant will never be exercised prior to expiration, and hence, it has the same value as a European warrant'' (Theorem~2) \citep[p.~144]{p2-merton}.

\emph{Greeks} for a European call on an asset with yield $q$ \citep[chap.~19, Table~19.6, p.~439]{s7-hulloptions}:
\begin{itemize}
\item delta $e^{-qT}N(d_1)$;
\item gamma $N'(d_1)e^{-qT}/(S_0\sigma\sqrt T)$;
\item vega $S_0\sqrt TN'(d_1)e^{-qT}$;
\item rho $KTe^{-rT}N(d_2)$;
\item theta $-S_0N'(d_1)\sigma e^{-qT}/(2\sqrt T)+qS_0N(d_1)e^{-qT}-rKe^{-rT}N(d_2)$.
\end{itemize}
Setting $q$ to an index yield, the foreign rate or $r$ gives index, currency and futures options (p.~439).

\qffield{Notation} Black and Scholes: $x$ stock price, $c$ exercise price, $t^*$ maturity, $v^2$ variance rate, $w_1$, $w_2$, $w_{11}$ partial derivatives. Hull: $S_0$, $K$, $T$, $\sigma$, $q$. Merton: $f$ European and $F$ American warrant values, $\tau$ time to expiry, $E$ exercise price.

\qffield{Conditions} Theory timeless (1973). The riskless hedge needs continuous rebalancing. Black and Scholes argue that a discretely adjusted hedge carries only diversifiable risk (p.~642), and Hull's list adds no riskless arbitrage, perfect divisibility, no taxes and a flat, constant rate. He notes that $\sigma$ and $r$ may be known functions of time, and rates stochastic if the terminal price stays lognormal (sec.~15.6, p.~348). Merton's Theorems~1--2 need no payouts and positive interest rates; with a large dividend yield the European call can fall below intrinsic value, and early exercise of an American call can then have value (for example, $S=150$, $K=100$, $q=10$ percent, $r=2$ percent, $\sigma=20$ percent, $T=2$ gives $c=29.98<50$).

\qffield{Practice} Numerical checks:
\begin{itemize}
\item Hull's Example~15.6 ($S_0=42$, $K=40$, $r=10$ percent, $\sigma=20$ percent, $T=0.5$) gives $c=4.76$ and $p=0.81$ (p.~356);
\item the formula equals the discounted risk-neutral expectation computed by quadrature;
\item it satisfies the differential equation by finite differences;
\item each Greek of Table~19.6 matches its finite difference;
\item $\Theta+(r-q)S\Delta+\frac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma=rc$, which is the differential equation restated in Greeks;
\item Merton's bound holds at several volatilities, and implied volatility is recovered by bisection.
\end{itemize}
Delta and delta--vega hedges can be compared on profit and loss. That needs the discrete-hedging error, costs and smile moves, which these checks do not cover.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The constant-volatility lognormal model is contradicted by the smile itself (\hyperref[def:implied-volatility-surface]{implied volatility surface}). The Greeks are partial derivatives at fixed $\sigma$, so a vega hedge under one implied volatility per option is a model-dependent hedge.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[def:derivative-contracts]{derivative contracts}; \hyperref[res:cost-of-carry-forward-price]{cost-of-carry forward price} (the yield $q$); \hyperref[def:implied-volatility-surface]{implied volatility surface}; \hyperref[def:brownian-motion-quadratic-variation]{Brownian motion}, \hyperref[res:ito-lemma-geometric-brownian-motion]{It\^o's lemma} and \hyperref[res:risk-neutral-pricing-feynman-kac]{risk-neutral pricing and Feynman--Kac} (the foundations of the hedging argument).

\qffield{Further reading} Merton's later sections (dividends, stochastic rates, the American put) and Hull's chaps.~19 and~21 on hedging practice and the Greeks of American options.

\end{qfmodel}

\begin{qfdefinition}[Implied volatility, the smile and an arbitrage-free surface: total implied variance, the calendar and butterfly conditions, raw SVI and the wing bound]\label{def:implied-volatility-surface}
\qffield{Statement} The \emph{Black--Scholes implied volatility} $\sigma_{BS}(k,t)$ of a European option is the volatility that, put into the Black--Scholes formula, reproduces its price, with $k=\ln(K/F_t)$ the log-moneyness against the forward. Gatheral and Jacquier work with the \emph{total implied variance} $w(k,t)=\sigma_{BS}^2(k,t)\,t$. They call the map $(k,t)\mapsto w$ the volatility surface and a fixed-$t$ curve a slice \citep[``Notations'', p.~2]{exp-gatheral-jacquier-svi}. A surface is free of \emph{static arbitrage} if and only if it is free of calendar-spread arbitrage and each slice is free of butterfly arbitrage (Definition~2.1, p.~3):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Calendar spreads.} With dividends proportional to the stock price, the surface is free of calendar-spread arbitrage if and only if $\partial_tw(k,t)\ge0$ for all $k$ and $t>0$: total variance at fixed log-moneyness cannot fall with maturity (Lemma~2.1, p.~3).
\item \emph{Butterflies.} A slice is free of butterfly arbitrage, a non-negative implied density, if and only if
\[ g(k)=\Big(1-\frac{kw'(k)}{2w(k)}\Big)^2-\frac{w'(k)^2}{4}\Big(\frac1{w(k)}+\frac14\Big)+\frac{w''(k)}2\ge0\ \ \text{for all }k,\qquad\lim_{k\to+\infty}d_+(k)=-\infty \]
(eq.~(2.1) and Lemma~2.2, p.~4).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Raw SVI} parameterises a slice as $w(k)=a+b\{\rho(k-m)+\sqrt{(k-m)^2+\sigma^2}\}$, with $b\ge0$, $|\rho|<1$, $\sigma>0$ and $a+b\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\ge0$ so that $w\ge0$ (eq.~(3.1), p.~5). It is convex in $k$, but its parameters alone do not exclude butterfly arbitrage. Their Example~3.1, Vogt's parameters $(a,b,m,\rho,\sigma)=(-0.0410,0.1331,0.3586,0.3060,0.4153)$ at $t=1$, gives a negative density (p.~10). Their SSVI family (sec.~4) gives sufficient conditions for a whole surface free of static arbitrage.

\emph{Wings.} Lee's moment formula bounds the tails model-free: the large-strike limsup of $\sigma_{BS}^2T/|x|$ is a number $\beta_R\in[0,2]$, with $\frac1{2\beta_R}+\frac{\beta_R}8-\frac12=\sup\{p:E\,S_T^{1+p}<\infty\}$, and a reciprocal formula holds for small strikes \citep[sec.~1, eq.~(1.1), p.~2; Theorems~3.2 and~3.4]{exp-lee-wings}. So total implied variance can grow at most linearly in $|k|$, with slope at most~2.

\qffield{Notation} $F_t$ the forward; $k$ log-moneyness; $w$ total implied variance; $d_\pm(k)=-k/\sqrt{w}\pm\sqrt w/2$; raw SVI parameters $a,b,\rho,m,\sigma$ (here $\sigma$ is a shape parameter, not a volatility).

\qffield{Conditions} The conditions assume a twice-differentiable surface. Observed quotes form a finite grid with bid--ask spreads, so ``arbitrage-free'' applies to the fitted surface, and the spread may admit several such fits. The calendar condition needs proportional dividends (Lemma~2.1).

\qffield{Practice} Numerical checks. The quantity $g/\sqrt{2\pi w}\,e^{-d_-^2/2}$ matches $K\,\partial^2C/\partial K^2$ of the undiscounted call by finite differences. An illustrative SVI slice has $g\ge0$ on $|k|\le1.5$, while Vogt's slice has $\min g=-0.033$. Two slices with the same shape and higher $a$ at the longer maturity satisfy the calendar condition.

\emph{Source discrepancy.} The proof of Lemma~2.2 (p.~5) writes the density as $p(k)=\partial^2C/\partial K^2$ at $K=F_te^k$ and then as $g(k)/\sqrt{2\pi w(k)}$ times $\exp(-d_-(k)^2/2)$. The second is the density per unit log-moneyness, $K$ times the first. The sign condition and the lemma are unaffected.

\emph{Earlier versions}: arXiv versions v1 and v2 carry the same two expressions in the same proof, there numbered Lemma~4.1 (PDF p.~8 of each), so the slip predates the 2024 revision.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Locators refer to the arXiv preprint as revised on 27 November 2024, not the 2014 journal version. No surface is fitted to market quotes here.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{Black--Scholes--Merton}; \hyperref[def:vix-variance-dividend-swaps]{the VIX and variance swaps} (variance from a strip of options).

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{s6-surface} (local and stochastic volatility, surface parameterisations).

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfmodel}[Heston's stochastic-volatility model: the closed-form price through characteristic functions, and Carr and Madan's FFT for many strikes]\label{mod:heston-stochastic-volatility}
\qffield{Statement} Heston lets the variance follow a square-root process correlated with the stock: $dS=\mu S\,dt+\sqrt{v}S\,dz_1$, $dv=\kappa(\theta-v)dt+\sigma\sqrt v\,dz_2$, $dz_1dz_2=\rho\,dt$. With $x=\ln S$, a call is $C=SP_1-Ke^{-r\tau}P_2$, where each $P_j$ is the probability, under a different measure, that the option ends in the money. Their characteristic functions are $f_j=\exp[C(\tau;\phi)+D(\tau;\phi)v+i\phi x]$ with $C$ and $D$ in closed form ($u_1=\tfrac12$, $u_2=-\tfrac12$, $a=\kappa\theta$, $b_1=\kappa+\lambda-\rho\sigma$, $b_2=\kappa+\lambda$), and $P_j=\tfrac12+\tfrac1\pi\int_0^\infty\operatorname{Re}[e^{-i\phi\ln K}f_j/(i\phi)]\,d\phi$ (eqs.~(10) and~(14)--(18), p.~331) \citep[\emph{RFS} 6(2), 1993, eqs.~(10)--(18), pp.~330--331]{s6-heston}.

Carr and Madan price a whole strike grid at once: they Fourier-transform the call price in log strike after multiplying by $e^{\alpha k}$, because the plain call price is not square-integrable, and invert with the FFT; a sufficient condition for the damped price to be integrable is that $E[S_T^{\alpha+1}]$ is finite, and they find ``one quarter of this upper bound serves as a good choice for $\alpha$'' (eqs.~(5)--(7), p.~64) \citep[\emph{Journal of Computational Finance} 2(4), 1999, author copy, pp.~63--64]{s6-carrmadan}.

\qffield{Notation} $v$ instantaneous variance; $\kappa$ speed of mean reversion; $\theta$ long-run variance; $\sigma$ volatility of variance; $\rho$ correlation; $\lambda$ the variance risk-premium parameter (zero in the checks); $\alpha$ the damping coefficient; $k=\ln K$.

\qffield{Conditions} Heston's form of $g$ and $\log(1-ge^{d\tau})$ can cross a branch cut of the complex logarithm at long maturities, which is the subject of \nolinkurl{vo-lord-kahl-2010}; the checks here use a half-year maturity. The FFT needs a damping $\alpha$ for which the moment $E[S_T^{\alpha+1}]$ exists; in Heston that moment can explode in finite time for some parameters.

\qffield{Practice} With a negligible volatility of variance and $v_0=\theta$ the formula equals Black--Scholes at $\sqrt\theta$ to $10^{-3}$; with $\kappa=1.5$, $\theta=v_0=0.04$, $\sigma=0.5$, $\rho=-0.7$ and $T=0.5$ it matches a 200{,}000-path Monte Carlo at strikes 90, 100 and 110 within three standard errors (5.705 against 5.708 at the money); the FFT with $\alpha=1.5$ reproduces Black--Scholes within 0.001 and the Heston integral within 0.002. Negative $\rho$ lowers out-of-the-money call prices relative to Black--Scholes at the same variance (1.41 against 2.47 at strike 110), the skew the model exists to produce.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The checks use illustrative parameters; the model is not calibrated to quotes.


\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{Black--Scholes--Merton}; \hyperref[def:implied-volatility-surface]{implied volatility surface}; \hyperref[mod:merton-jump-diffusion]{Merton's jump-diffusion}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{vo-lord-kahl-2010}; \nolinkurl{audit-duffie-pan-singleton}; \nolinkurl{s7-derman}.

\end{qfmodel}

\begin{qfmodel}[Merton's jump-diffusion option price: a Poisson mixture of Black--Scholes prices, and why jump risk cannot be hedged with the stock alone]\label{mod:merton-jump-diffusion}
\qffield{Statement} Merton adds to the diffusion a Poisson jump with intensity $\lambda$ that multiplies the price by $Y$. With jumps ``there is no way to construct a riskless portfolio of stock and options'', so the Black--Scholes argument fails; if the jump risk is diversifiable (uncorrelated with the market), the option price satisfies an equation in which only the interest rate appears, and ``one cannot `act as if' the jump component was not there'' (secs.~II--III, printed pp.~12--15). The solution is $F(S,\tau)=\sum_n\frac{e^{-\lambda\tau}(\lambda\tau)^n}{n!}\,\mathcal E_n[W(SX_ne^{-\lambda k\tau},\tau;E,\sigma^2,r)]$, with $W$ the Black--Scholes price and $X_n$ the product of $n$ jumps (eq.~(16), printed p.~16).

With lognormal jumps of log-variance $\delta^2$ and $E[Y]=1$, $F=\sum_n\frac{e^{-\lambda\tau}(\lambda\tau)^n}{n!}W(S,\tau;E,\sigma^2+n\delta^2/\tau,r)$ (eqs.~(18)--(19), printed p.~17); an option on a stock with jumps is worth more, other things equal, $\partial F/\partial\lambda>0$ at $\lambda=0$ (sec.~IV, printed p.~21) \citep[Sloan Working Paper 787-75, 1975, printed pp.~12--21]{wide-merton-jumps}.

\qffield{Notation} $\lambda$ jump intensity; $Y$ the jump multiplier, $k=E[Y]-1$; $\delta^2$ the variance of $\ln Y$; $W(S,\tau;E,\sigma^2,r)$ Black--Scholes with strike $E$.

\qffield{Conditions} The pricing equation rests on the jump risk being diversifiable (Merton's two derivations, printed pp.~13--20); with priced jump risk it does not hold. Merton notes he knows of no empirical test of that proposition (printed p.~20).

\qffield{Practice} With $\lambda=0$ the series is Black--Scholes; $\partial F/\partial\lambda>0$ at $\lambda=0$ at strikes 80, 100 and 120; with $\lambda=1$, $\delta^2=0.02$, $\sigma^2=0.04$ and $T=0.5$ it matches a Monte Carlo within three standard errors (7.214 against 7.212 at the money).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Locators refer to the 1975 MIT working paper, whose equation numbers may differ from the 1976 \emph{Journal of Financial Economics} article.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:heston-stochastic-volatility]{Heston}; \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{Black--Scholes--Merton}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{wide-bates-svj} (stochastic volatility with jumps).

\end{qfmodel}

\begin{qfmodel}[The SABR model and its implied-volatility expansion, in single- and multiple-curve form]\label{mod:sabr-smile}
\qffield{Statement} SABR lets a forward follow $dF=V F^\beta dW$ with lognormal volatility $dV=\nu V\,dZ$, $V(0)=\alpha$, $dW\,dZ=\rho\,dt$, $0\le\beta\le1$. Hagan et al.'s expansion gives the Black implied volatility as $\sigma(K)=\frac{\nu\ln(F/K)}{x(z)}\frac{A}{B}$, with $z=\frac\nu\alpha(FK)^{(1-\beta)/2}\ln\frac FK$, $x(z)=\ln\frac{\sqrt{1-2\rho z+z^2}+z-\rho}{1-\rho}$, a time correction $A=1+[\frac{(1-\beta)^2\alpha^2}{24(FK)^{1-\beta}}+\frac{\alpha\beta\nu\rho}{4(FK)^{(1-\beta)/2}}+\nu^2\frac{2-3\rho^2}{24}]\tau$ and $B=(FK)^{(1-\beta)/2}[1+\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{24}\ln^2\frac FK+\frac{(1-\beta)^4}{1920}\ln^4\frac FK]$. In the multiple-curve version the forward is replaced by the FRA rate and the measure by the $T_k$-forward measure of the discounting curve; ``the SABR volatility formula remains unchanged'' \citep[arXiv 1103.2567, version of 28 March 2012, sec.~4.5, Table~5, p.~18]{gap-rates-multicurve-sabr}.

\qffield{Notation} $F$ the forward or FRA rate; $\alpha$ initial volatility; $\beta$ the CEV exponent; $\rho$ correlation; $\nu$ volatility of volatility; $\tau$ time to expiry.

\qffield{Conditions} Formula of 2002 as restated in 2012. It is an asymptotic expansion, accurate for small $\tau\nu^2$ and not too far from the money; at low strikes it can imply a negative density, which the arbitrage-free version (\nolinkurl{rt-arbitrage-free-sabr-2014}) addresses.

\qffield{Practice} The formula is continuous at the money and flat at $\alpha$ when $\nu=0$, $\beta=1$; with $F=3\%$, $\beta=0.5$, $\rho=-0.3$, $\nu=0.4$ and one year to expiry, it is within 0.2 volatility points of a Monte Carlo of the dynamics at strikes 2.5, 3 and 3.5 percent (22.53 against 22.37 percent at 2.5). That is one parameter set, not an accuracy study.

\qffield{Pitfalls} The formula is from Bianchetti and Carlicchi's restatement of Hagan et al.\ (\nolinkurl{wide-hagan-sabr}), compared with a simulation.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:multi-curve-fra-swap-valuation]{multi-curve FRA and swap valuation}; \hyperref[def:implied-volatility-surface]{implied volatility surface}.

\qffield{Further reading} \nolinkurl{wide-hagan-sabr}; \nolinkurl{rt-arbitrage-free-sabr-2014}; \nolinkurl{s6-brigo}.

\end{qfmodel}
