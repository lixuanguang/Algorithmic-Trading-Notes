\section{Option valuation: value drivers, parity, binomial trees, Black's model and the Greeks}

These entries explain what an option's value is made of, the parity relations that link calls, puts, the underlying and forwards, binomial valuation over one and two periods, Black's model for currency, futures and interest-rate options, and how the Greeks are used to hedge. Payoffs and profit at expiry are in \hyperref[meth:option-payoff-profit]{option payoff and profit}; the Black--Scholes--Merton derivation and Greek formulas are in \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{the BSM model}. Numbers are checked in \texttt{notes/artefacts/option-valuation/option-valuation-checks.py}; rates are continuously compounded unless stated.

\begin{qfdefinition}[What determines an option's value: exercise (intrinsic) value, moneyness and time value, the six factors and their signs, and the arbitrage bounds]\label{def:option-value-drivers}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Exercise value and time value.} Exercise (intrinsic) value is what immediate exercise would pay, $\max(S-K,0)$ for a call and $\max(K-S,0)$ for a put; moneyness is the sign of that payoff (\hyperref[meth:option-payoff-profit]{in, at or out of the money}). The rest of the premium is time value.

\emph{Six factors.} Six factors affect a stock option's price: the stock price, the strike, the time to expiry, the volatility, the risk-free rate and expected dividends \citep[chap.~11, sec.~11.1, pp.~247--251, Table~11.1]{s7-hulloptions}.
\begin{itemize}
\item A call rises with the stock price and falls with the strike; a put does the reverse.
\item Both American calls and American puts gain, or at least do not lose, with longer life, because the holder keeps every exercise opportunity. A European option need not: a large dividend before the longer expiry can make the shorter call worth more.
\item Both gain with volatility, because the holder benefits from large moves and their loss is limited to the premium.
\item A higher rate, other things fixed, raises calls and lowers puts.
\item A dividend lowers calls and raises puts.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Bounds.} A call is worth no more than the stock, and an American put no more than $K$, a European put no more than $Ke^{-rT}$. Without dividends a European call is worth at least $S_0-Ke^{-rT}$ and a European put at least $Ke^{-rT}-S_0$; outside these bounds there is an arbitrage (sec.~11.3, pp.~252--254). An American call on a non-dividend-paying stock is never exercised early, while an American put may be (secs.~11.5--11.6, pp.~257--262).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced} (Hull).
\begin{itemize}
\item With $S_0=K=50$, $r=5$ percent, $\sigma=30$ percent and $T=1$, the call is worth 7.116 and the put 4.677, all of it time value (p.~248).
\item Bumping each input reproduces the signs of Table~11.1.
\item With $S_0=20$, $K=18$, $r=10$ percent and $T=1$, the lower bound is 3.71. A call quoted at 3.00 lets an arbitrageur short the stock, buy the call and invest 17, earning at least 0.79 at expiry (p.~253).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Original.} A dividend of 2 in six months lowers the 7.116 call to 5.947 and raises the put to 5.459. A European put struck at 50 with the stock at 30 is worth 17.865, below its exercise value of 20: its time value is negative, which an American put would avoid by early exercise.

\qffield{Conditions} The signs hold one factor at a time. In practice rates and stock prices tend to move in opposite directions, so a rate rise with its accompanying stock fall can lower a call (Hull, p.~250). Bounds assume no transaction costs, one tax rate and borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate (sec.~11.2).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Reading the whole premium as an expected profit; most of an at-the-money option's price is time value that decays. Assuming a European option always gains from a longer life.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[res:put-call-forward-parity]{put--call parity}; \hyperref[meth:greeks-delta-gamma-hedging]{the Greeks}.

\end{qfdefinition}

\begin{qfresult}[Put--call parity and put--call--forward parity: the relations, the arbitrage when they fail, synthetic positions, and bounds for American options]\label{res:put-call-forward-parity}
\qffield{Statement} For European options on a non-dividend-paying stock with the same strike and expiry, a call plus cash $Ke^{-rT}$ and a put plus one share both pay $\max(S_T,K)$, so
\[ c+Ke^{-rT}=p+S_0 \]
\citep[chap.~11, sec.~11.4, eq.~(11.6), p.~256]{s7-hulloptions}. With a forward or futures price $F_0$ for the expiry date the share is replaced by $F_0e^{-rT}$ (\emph{put--call--forward parity}):
\[ c+Ke^{-rT}=p+F_0e^{-rT},\qquad c-p=(F_0-K)e^{-rT} \]
\citep[chap.~18, sec.~18.4, eq.~(18.1), p.~405]{s7-hulloptions}. Parity holds only for European options. For American options on a non-dividend-paying stock, $S_0-K\le C-P\le S_0-Ke^{-rT}$ (eq.~(11.7), p.~256).

\emph{Uses.} Parity defines synthetic positions, the convertible-bond view (\hyperref[meth:convertible-bond-valuation]{convertibles}) and the arbitrage when quotes disagree. A long call and a short put at the same strike form a synthetic forward at $K$. A put plus the stock less a bond is a synthetic call. Long put plus short call is a short forward (\hyperref[res:synthetic-short-put-call]{synthetic short}).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced} (Hull, Table~11.3, p.~257). The stock is at 31 and the strike 30, with $r=10$ percent, three months to expiry and the call at 3.
\begin{itemize}
\item With the put at 2.25, $c+Ke^{-rT}=32.26$ and $p+S_0=33.25$. Buying the call and shorting the put and the stock raises 30.25, which grows to 31.02; whichever option is exercised, the share is bought for 30, a profit of 1.02.
\item With the put at 1, short the call and buy the put and the stock for 29, borrowed. Repaying 29.73 leaves 0.27.
\end{itemize}
\emph{American bounds} (Example~11.3, p.~256). With the call at 1.50, $S_0=19$, $K=20$, $r=10$ percent and five months, the put must lie between 1.68 and 2.50. \emph{Forward parity} (Example~18.5, p.~406). A commodity call at 0.56 with $K=8.50$, futures 8.00, 10 percent and six months implies a put of 1.04.

\emph{Original.} With $S_0=100$, $K=95$, $r=4$ percent, a dividend yield of 1.5 percent and six months, the forward is 101.2578. Black--Scholes prices satisfy $c-p=6.1339=(F_0-K)e^{-rT}=S_0e^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT}$. The synthetic call, the put plus the dividend-adjusted stock less the bond, equals the call to machine precision.

\qffield{Conditions} Same strike, expiry, underlying and settlement; European exercise; dividends known (replace $S_0$ by $S_0-D$ or $S_0e^{-qT}$). With borrowing costs and short-sale constraints parity becomes a band, and violations line up with scarce borrow (\hyperref[res:synthetic-short-put-call]{evidence}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Applying European parity to American options. Using the spot price in put--call--forward parity when the forward embeds carry and dividends. Ignoring the bid--ask spread when declaring a violation.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:arbitrage-replication-carry]{arbitrage and replication}; \hyperref[def:option-value-drivers]{value drivers}.

\end{qfresult}

\begin{qfmethod}[One-period binomial valuation: the riskless hedge, replication, the risk-neutral probability, and why real-world probabilities do not enter]\label{meth:one-period-binomial-valuation}
\qffield{Statement} Let the stock move from $S_0$ to $S_0u$ or $S_0d$, with option payoffs $f_u$ and $f_d$. A portfolio long $\Delta=(f_u-f_d)/(S_0u-S_0d)$ shares and short one option has the same value in both states, so it must earn the risk-free rate. This gives
\[ f=e^{-rT}[pf_u+(1-p)f_d],\qquad p=\frac{e^{rT}-d}{u-d} \]
\citep[chap.~13, sec.~13.1, eqs.~(13.1)--(13.3), pp.~288--292]{s7-hulloptions}. Equivalently, buying $\Delta$ shares and borrowing $(\Delta S_0d-f_d)e^{-rT}$ \emph{replicates} the option. The probabilities of up and down moves do not enter, because the option is priced relative to the stock. $p$ is the probability of an up move in a risk-neutral world, where every asset earns the risk-free rate. Pricing by expected payoff discounted at the risk-free rate in that world gives the right price in the real world as well (\emph{risk-neutral valuation}, sec.~13.2, pp.~292--294).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced} (Hull, Figure~13.1). The stock is at 20 and moves to 22 or 18 in three months; the call is struck at 21 and $r=4$ percent. $\Delta=0.25$ makes the portfolio worth 4.5 in both states, its present value is 4.455, and the call is worth $20\times0.25-4.455=0.545$. The risk-neutral probability is 0.5503, and $e^{-0.01}\times0.5503\times1=0.545$.

\emph{Original.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Real-world probabilities.} If the up move has a real-world probability of 0.7, the stock is expected to earn 4 percent a quarter, yet the call is still 0.545. Discounting the real-world expected payoff at the risk-free rate would give 0.693, which replication shows is wrong.
\item \emph{Arbitrage.} If the call is quoted at 0.60, write 400 calls for 240 and buy 100 shares for 2{,}000, borrowing 1{,}782.09. That leaves 22.09 today, and the position is worth zero in both states at expiry.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Two states and frictionless trading; with more states one stock and one bond cannot replicate an arbitrary payoff in a single period, which multi-period trees solve by rebalancing (\hyperref[meth:multi-period-binomial-valuation]{two-period trees}). No arbitrage requires $d<e^{rT}<u$.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Reading $p$ as a forecast. Discounting a real-world expected payoff at the risk-free rate. Forgetting that $\Delta$ changes as the stock moves.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:arbitrage-replication-carry]{arbitrage}; \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{BSM as the limit of many steps}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Two-period binomial valuation: European and American options by backward induction, option value as an expected discounted payoff, identifying option arbitrage, and an interest-rate option on a rate tree]\label{meth:multi-period-binomial-valuation}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Components.} A binomial model has an up factor $u$, a down factor $d$, a risk-free rate per step and the risk-neutral probability $p$. Over two steps of length $\Delta t$, $f=e^{-2r\Delta t}[p^2f_{uu}+2p(1-p)f_{ud}+(1-p)^2f_{dd}]$, the option's expected payoff in a risk-neutral world discounted at the risk-free rate \citep[chap.~13, sec.~13.3, eqs.~(13.5)--(13.10), pp.~294--297]{s7-hulloptions}. Matching volatility sets $u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d=1/u$ (secs.~13.7--13.8, pp.~300--303). \emph{American options} are valued by comparing, at each node, the value of continuing with the payoff from early exercise and keeping the larger (sec.~13.5, pp.~298--299). The hedge ratio changes from node to node, so the replicating portfolio must be rebalanced (sec.~13.6).

\emph{Known dividends.} A known dollar dividend makes a tree on the stock price non-recombining. Hull instead builds the tree on $S^*$, the stock price less the present value of dividends paid during the option's life, and adds that present value back at nodes before each ex-dividend date. The tree then recombines \citep[chap.~21, sec.~21.3, pp.~480--483]{s7-hulloptions}. An American call can be worth exercising only just before an ex-dividend date. Just before the last one, $t_n$, exercise cannot be optimal if $D_n\le K[1-e^{-r(T-t_n)}]$, and before an earlier date $t_i$ if $D_i\le K[1-e^{-r(t_{i+1}-t_i)}]$. When the inequality fails, exercise is optimal for a high enough stock price (chap.~15, sec.~15.12, pp.~361--362, eqs.~(15.23)--(15.25)).

\emph{Option arbitrage.} A quoted price that differs from the tree value under the same inputs is an arbitrage if the replicating portfolio can be traded and rebalanced: buy the cheap option and sell the replicating portfolio, rebalancing at each node. The same rule identifies the early-exercise premium of an American option.

\emph{Interest-rate options.} On a calibrated rate tree (\hyperref[meth:binomial-rate-tree-calibration]{rate tree}) the same induction values an option on a rate, with each node's rate as its discount rate.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced} (Hull, Figures~13.7--13.8). A two-year put has $S_0=50$, $K=52$, $u=1.2$, $d=0.8$ and $r=5$ percent, so $p=0.6282$. The European put is worth 4.1927; Hull's 4.1923 uses $p$ rounded to four places. As an American option, node C's continuation value of 9.4639 (Hull 9.4636) is below the exercise value of 12, so the put is exercised there and the value rises to 5.0896 (Hull 5.0894).

\emph{Arbitrage} (original). If that American put is quoted at 4.80, it is cheap by 0.29. Buy it and sell its replicating portfolio of stock and borrowing, rebalancing at node B and exercising at node C.

\emph{Dividends} (Hull's Example~21.5 reproduced: a five-month American put, $S_0=52$, $K=50$, 10 percent, 40 percent volatility, a \$2.06 dividend at 3.5 months). The tree gives 4.44 with five steps, and 4.208 and 4.214 with 50 and 100 steps (p.~483). Original call ($S_0=K=50$, 5 percent, 25 percent, six months, ex-dividend at 0.455 years): the bound $K[1-e^{-r(T-t_n)}]$ is 0.112. With a \$0.05 dividend no node exercises and the 1{,}000-step American call equals the European, 4.1009. With \$1.50 the American is worth 3.854 against 3.313, and every exercise falls on the last step before the ex-date.

\emph{Interest-rate option} (original, on the Handbook's tree with rates 3.5, then 4.4448 or 5.4289, then 4.6958, 5.7354 or 7.0053 percent). A caplet on 1 million fixes at year 2 on the one-year rate with a strike of 5 percent and pays at year 3. It pays 0, 7{,}354 or 20{,}053. Discounting each payoff at its node's year-2 rate and averaging back gives 7{,}495, the same as averaging the discounted payoffs over the four equally likely paths. The payoff is interpreted as the excess rate on the notional paid in arrears, the convention of \hyperref[meth:capped-floored-floater-valuation]{caps on floaters}.

\qffield{Conditions} A recombining tree with constant $u$, $d$ and $r$; trading at each node without costs. Two steps illustrate the method and do not give an accurate price; more steps converge to Black--Scholes for European options.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Testing early exercise only at the final nodes. Checking an American call for early exercise between ex-dividend dates, or not at all when a large dividend falls just before expiry. Mixing rounded intermediate values into later steps. Discounting a rate option's payoff from the fixing date instead of the payment date.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:one-period-binomial-valuation]{one-period model}; \hyperref[meth:callable-putable-bond-valuation]{embedded options on the rate tree}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[Black--Scholes--Merton in practice and Black's model: the call as a leveraged position in the underlying, currency options, options on futures, caps, floors and swaptions]\label{meth:black-model-rates-currencies}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Assumptions} are those of \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{the BSM model}: lognormal prices, constant volatility and rate, continuous frictionless trading and no arbitrage. \emph{Components.} In $c=S_0N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$, the first term is $N(d_1)$ shares, the hedge ratio, and the second is a loan of $Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$, where $N(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability of exercise. A call is therefore a leveraged long position in the stock, rebalanced continuously (Interpretation of Hull, chap.~15).

\emph{Currencies and equities.} A foreign currency is an asset paying a yield equal to the foreign risk-free rate $r_f$, so $c=S_0e^{-r_fT}N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$, the Garman--Kohlhagen formula. Using the forward $F_0=S_0e^{(r-r_f)T}$ gives the same value as Black's formula \citep[chap.~17, sec.~17.5, eqs.~(17.11)--(17.14), pp.~394--395]{s7-hulloptions}. Index options use the dividend yield in the same way (sec.~17.4).

\emph{Black's model for futures options.} $c=e^{-rT}[F_0N(d_1)-KN(d_2)]$ and $p=e^{-rT}[KN(-d_2)-F_0N(-d_1)]$, with $d_{1,2}=[\ln(F_0/K)\pm\sigma^2T/2]/(\sigma\sqrt T)$ (chap.~18, sec.~18.7, eqs.~(18.7)--(18.8), p.~408). Traders use it also for European spot options, because the futures or forward price carries the income or convenience yield (sec.~18.8, p.~409).

\emph{Interest-rate options.} A caplet on a forward rate $F_k$ for the period $t_k$ to $t_{k+1}$, with accrual $\delta_k$ and notional $L$, is worth $L\delta_kP(0,t_{k+1})[F_kN(d_1)-R_KN(d_2)]$, with volatility over $t_k$ \citep[chap.~29, sec.~29.2, eq.~(29.7), pp.~695--696]{s7-hulloptions}. Cap minus floor equals a swap (Business Snapshot~29.1, p.~695). A European payer swaption is $LA[s_FN(d_1)-s_KN(d_2)]$, with $A$ the annuity of the underlying swap and $s_F$ the forward swap rate; the receiver swaption, the right to receive $s_K$, is $LA[s_KN(-d_2)-s_FN(-d_1)]$ (sec.~29.3, pp.~699--702, eqs.~(29.10)--(29.11)). A payer swaption less a receiver swaption at the same strike is a forward swap paying $s_K$, worth $LA(s_F-s_K)$, so the two are equal when $s_K=s_F$ (Problem~29.18, p.~705). A receiver swaption is a call on a fixed-rate bond with strike equal to the principal (Business Snapshot~29.2, p.~700). Hence a bond callable at par on one date, with the swap's fixed rate as its coupon, is the straight bond less the receiver swaption (Interpretation).

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced} (Hull).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Equity} (Example~15.6). With $S_0=42$, $K=40$, 10 percent, 20 percent volatility and six months, $c=4.76$ and $p=0.81$.
\item \emph{Currency} (Example~17.2). A four-month sterling call at the money, with 8 percent dollar and 11 percent sterling rates and a price of 4.3 cents, implies 14.1 percent volatility. Black's model on the forward gives the same value.
\item \emph{Futures} (Examples~18.6--18.7). A four-month futures put with $F=K=20$, 9 percent and 25 percent volatility is worth 1.12. A six-month gold call with futures at 1{,}240, strike 1{,}200, 5 percent and 20 percent volatility is worth 88.37.
\item \emph{Caplet} (Example~29.3). On 10 million, with an 8 percent cap, a 7 percent forward, 20 percent volatility, fixing at one year and paying at 1.25 years with 6.5 percent zero rates, the caplet is worth 0.00519 million.
\item \emph{Swaption} (Example~29.4). The right to pay 6.2 percent in a three-year swap starting in five years, with a forward of 6.194 percent semiannual, 20 percent volatility, an annuity of 2.0035 and 100 million, is worth 2.19 million.
\item \emph{Receiver and parity} (checked, Example~29.4's inputs). The right to receive 6.2 percent is worth 2.2028 million, and payer minus receiver equals the forward swap, $-0.0120$ million. Across 1{,}000 random curves at the exercise date, an issuer's call at par on a 6.2 percent bond pays exactly what the receiver swaption pays. The callable bond is therefore worth the straight bond less 2.2028 million per 100 million under these inputs.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Leverage} (original, Example~15.6). The call equals 0.7791 shares (32.72) financed by borrowing 27.96. Its elasticity is $\Delta S/c=6.88$, so a 1 percent stock move changes the call by about 6.9 percent. Caplet minus floorlet equals the present value of $(F-K)$ on the accrual, as parity requires.

\qffield{Conditions} Lognormal underlying with constant volatility; Black's model for rates treats each forward or swap rate as lognormal under its own measure, an internally consistent market convention that cannot hold for all rates at once (Hull sec.~29.3) and fails with negative rates, where normal (Bachelier) or shifted models are used. The smile (\hyperref[def:implied-volatility-surface]{implied volatility surface}) means one volatility per strike.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Using the spot price and a dividend estimate when a liquid forward exists. Discounting a caplet from the fixing date. Quoting a swaption volatility without the model it belongs to.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{BSM}; \hyperref[meth:capped-floored-floater-valuation]{caps on floaters}; \hyperref[mod:sabr-smile]{SABR}.

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfmethod}[The Greeks in use: interpreting them, executing and rebalancing a delta hedge, gamma risk and gamma-neutral hedging, and implied volatility in trading]\label{meth:greeks-delta-gamma-hedging}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{Interpretation.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Delta is the change in option value per unit change in the underlying.
\item Gamma is the change in delta.
\item Theta is the change with the passage of time.
\item Vega is the change per unit of volatility.
\item Rho is the change per unit of interest rate.
\end{itemize}
Formulas are in \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{the BSM model}. A position with zero delta is delta neutral, but only for a short time, so it must be rebalanced: \emph{dynamic hedging} \citep[chap.~19, sec.~19.4, pp.~421--427]{s7-hulloptions}.

\emph{Executing a delta hedge.} A bank that sells calls buys $N(d_1)$ shares per call and adjusts the holding as delta changes. If the hedge worked perfectly, the discounted cost of writing and hedging the option would equal its BSM price on every path; weekly rebalancing leaves a dispersion that falls as rebalancing becomes more frequent (Tables~19.2--19.4, pp.~424--426).

\emph{Gamma risk.} For a delta-neutral portfolio $\Delta\Pi\approx\Theta\Delta t+\tfrac12\Gamma\Delta S^2$ (eq.~(19.3), p.~430). With negative gamma the portfolio gains if the price does not move and loses on large moves of either sign. The underlying has zero gamma, so a portfolio is made gamma neutral with $-\Gamma/\Gamma_T$ traded options and then delta neutral again with the underlying (pp.~430--431). Vega is hedged the same way, with options (sec.~19.8).

\emph{Second-order volatility Greeks.} Vanna, $\partial^2V/\partial S\partial\sigma$, is the change in delta per unit of volatility; volga, $\partial^2V/\partial\sigma^2$, is the change in vega per unit of volatility. Wilmott records them under the names ``kabanga'' and ``vomma'' and warns that vega itself is meaningful only for payoffs whose gamma has one sign \citep[chap.~7, pp.~127--128 and n.~3]{wide-wilmott-pwoqf}. For a European call, vanna is $-e^{-qT}N'(d_1)d_2/\sigma$ and volga is $\text{vega}\cdot d_1d_2/\sigma$.

\emph{Implied volatility in trading.} The implied volatility is the volatility that makes the model price equal the market price. Traders quote options in implied volatility, and the VIX indexes the 30-day implied volatility of S\&P~500 options (chap.~15, sec.~15.11, pp.~358--359). A delta-hedged long option earns, roughly, $\tfrac12\Gamma S^2(\sigma_{\text{realised}}^2-\sigma_{\text{implied}}^2)\Delta t$ each period. So buying options is a view that realised volatility will exceed implied: ``If implied volatility is low, such that options generally appear underpriced, look for spreads with a positive vega'' \citep[chap.~11, p.~199]{s7-natenberg}.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Reproduced} (Hull, Example~19.1 and sec.~19.6).
\begin{itemize}
\item A call with $S=49$, $K=50$, 5 percent, 20 percent volatility and 20 weeks has a delta of 0.522; 100{,}000 calls are worth about \$240{,}000.
\item A delta-neutral book with gamma $-3{,}000$ and a traded call with delta 0.62 and gamma 1.50 needs 2{,}000 calls bought and 1{,}240 shares sold.
\item A book with gamma $-10{,}000$ loses about 20{,}000 on a move of 2.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Checked} (original, the Example~19.1 call).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Rebalancing frequency.} Simulating 20{,}000 paths with a 13 percent drift, the ratio of the standard deviation of hedging cost to the BSM price is 0.415, 0.373, 0.270 and 0.192 for rebalancing every 5, 4, 2 and 1 weeks, within 0.03 of Hull's 0.42, 0.38, 0.28 and 0.21. The mean cost, 2.4035, is close to the 2.40 price.
\item \emph{Short intervals.} At half a week and a quarter of a week the simulation gives 0.136 and 0.098, falling by about $\sqrt2$ per halving as discrete-hedging theory predicts. Hull's Table~19.4 gives 0.16 and 0.13, ratios of 1.31 and 1.23; the cause is not stated, and the discrepancy is recorded.
\item \emph{Gamma--theta.} A short call hedged with delta shares over one day changes by $-0.1176$ for a move of $-2$ and $-0.1095$ for $+2$ on full revaluation, against $-0.1140$ from $\Theta\Delta t+\tfrac12\Gamma\Delta S^2$.
\item \emph{Implied against realised.} Buying the call at 20 percent implied and hedging daily at that volatility, while realised volatility is 25 percent, earns 0.62 on average per call (standard error 0.006).
\item \emph{Vega.} Vega is 12.1 per unit of volatility, so one point of implied volatility moves the call by about 0.12.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Conditions} Constant volatility, no transaction costs and continuous trading make the hedge perfect in theory. In practice traders rebalance to delta daily and manage gamma and vega by watching them, because options to neutralise them are expensive, and they use scenario tables of profit and loss across prices and volatilities (secs.~19.10--19.11, pp.~437--438). Greeks computed at one implied volatility per option ignore smile dynamics (\hyperref[def:implied-volatility-surface]{volatility surface}).

\qffield{Pitfalls} Treating a delta-neutral book as riskless. Hedging gamma with the underlying. Reading a profit on a delta-hedged option as alpha when it is the realised-minus-implied variance spread, earned at the cost of gamma risk.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{BSM}; \hyperref[meth:option-spreads-volatility-strategies]{volatility strategies}; \hyperref[def:vix-variance-dividend-swaps]{the VIX}; \hyperref[res:delta-hedging-volatility-mismatch]{hedging with the wrong volatility} (which delta to use when implied and actual volatility differ).

\end{qfmethod}

\begin{qfresult}[Delta hedging when implied and actual volatility differ: the locked-in profit of the actual-volatility hedge against the path-dependent profit of the implied-volatility hedge]\label{res:delta-hedging-volatility-mismatch}
\qffield{Statement} Suppose an option is bought at the price implied by $\tilde\sigma$ while the asset's actual volatility is $\sigma$, and the position is delta hedged to expiry. The question is which volatility goes into the delta \citep[chap.~12, pp.~197--199]{wide-wilmott-pwoqf}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Hedge with actual volatility.} This replicates a short position in a correctly priced option, so the profit is guaranteed to be $V(S,t;\sigma)-V(S,t;\tilde\sigma)$ in present value by expiry. The mark-to-market profit over each step is $\tfrac12(\sigma^2-\tilde\sigma^2)S^2\Gamma^i\,dt+(\Delta^i-\Delta^a)((\mu-r+D)S\,dt+\sigma S\,dX)$: it contains a $dX$ term and the real drift $\mu$, so ``you could lose before you gain'' (pp.~200--202).
\item \emph{Hedge with implied volatility.} The profit each step is $\tfrac12(\sigma^2-\tilde\sigma^2)S^2\Gamma^i\,dt$, eq.~(12.1), with no $dX$ term. Its present value over the life is $\tfrac12(\sigma^2-\tilde\sigma^2)\int_{t_0}^Te^{-r(t-t_0)}S^2\Gamma^i\,dt$, which is ``always positive, but highly path dependent''; the positivity needs $\Gamma^i\ge0$, as for a vanilla call or put. It depends on $\mu$, is largest if the asset ends near the strike, and needs no estimate of actual volatility beyond its sign relative to implied (pp.~202--203). For a payoff whose gamma changes sign the integrand changes sign too, so the total can be negative even with $\sigma>\tilde\sigma$.
\end{itemize}
Hedging with any volatility $\sigma_h$ gives $V(S,t;\sigma_h)-V(S,t;\tilde\sigma)+\tfrac12(\sigma^2-\sigma_h^2)\int e^{-r(t-t_0)}S^2\Gamma^h\,dt$, eq.~(12.2), after Carr and Henrard (p.~203).

\qffield{Notation} Superscripts $a$ and $i$ for values and Greeks at actual and implied volatility; $D$ dividend yield; profits in present value at $t_0$. Interpretation: ``zero diffusion'' describes the continuous-time increment of the implied-volatility hedge. With rebalancing at intervals $\delta t$ both hedges carry a hedging error, which Wilmott writes as $\tfrac12(\phi^2-1)\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\,\delta t$ per step: mean zero, chi-squared and skewed \citep[chap.~47, pp.~764--770]{wide-wilmott-pwoqf}. Hull's simulations show the dispersion of a discretely rebalanced hedge falling steadily with the rebalancing interval \citep[chap.~19, sec.~19.4, pp.~425--426]{s7-hulloptions}.

\qffield{Conditions} Geometric Brownian motion with constant, known actual volatility and constant implied volatility; the option is marked at implied volatility throughout; continuous rebalancing, no costs, borrowing and lending at $r$. The guaranteed profit needs the actual volatility to be known and constant, which it never is; the implied-volatility hedge has a positive total if $\sigma>\tilde\sigma$ throughout and $\Gamma^i\ge0$ along the path, as for a vanilla call or put (a convex payoff). Neither profit is positive merely because actual volatility exceeds implied: with negative gamma the integrand is negative, and where vega is negative $V^a-V^i<0$. With a smile, stochastic volatility or jumps, neither result holds exactly. The examples are synthetic.

\qffield{Practice} Example, synthetic: a one-year at-the-money call, $S_0=K=100$, $r=5$ percent, $q=0$, real drift 10 percent, actual volatility 30 percent, bought at implied 20 percent. Then $V^a-V^i=14.2313-10.4506=3.7807$. On 20{,}000 common paths:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Actual-volatility delta.} Mean profit 3.7770 (standard error 0.0044) with 252 rebalancings and 3.7805 (0.0014) with 2{,}520. The standard deviation falls from 0.627 to 0.200, a ratio of 3.14 against $\sqrt{10}=3.16$: the dispersion is discrete-hedging error.
\item \emph{Implied-volatility delta.} The root-mean-square gap between each path's profit and its pathwise integral falls from 0.797 to 0.256. The standard deviation across paths stays at 1.75, because the total itself depends on the path. The mean profit here is 3.746 (standard error 0.012); with another drift it would differ.
\item \emph{The gamma condition, in closed form.} A unit cash-or-nothing call (\hyperref[meth:digital-option-valuation]{digital options}) with $S_0=120$, $K=100$, $r=5$ percent, $q=0$ and $T=1$ is worth 0.8141 at 20 percent volatility and 0.6980 at 30 percent, so the actual-volatility hedge locks in $-0.1160$. Its gamma at implied volatility is $+0.00071$ at $S=80$ and $-0.00047$ at $S=120$; it is negative wherever $d_1>0$, so a path that stays above the strike makes the implied-volatility hedge lose.
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Expecting the implied-volatility hedge to earn $V^a-V^i$, or the actual-volatility hedge to make money smoothly. Calling the implied-volatility hedge riskless because each increment has no $dW$ term: the total is random, and finite rebalancing adds noise. Forgetting that the guaranteed profit depends on a volatility forecast that has to be right for the whole life. Expecting a positive variance spread to pay on a digital, barrier or spread position whose gamma changes sign.

\qffield{See also} \hyperref[meth:greeks-delta-gamma-hedging]{the Greeks in use}; \hyperref[mod:black-scholes-merton]{BSM}; \hyperref[def:vix-variance-dividend-swaps]{variance swaps} (trading the variance spread directly); \hyperref[meth:active-currency-strategies]{volatility trading in currencies}; \hyperref[meth:transaction-cost-hedging]{hedging with transaction costs}.

\end{qfresult}

\begin{qfmethod}[Hedging options with proportional transaction costs: Leland's adjusted volatility for long and short positions, the nonlinear portfolio equation, and fixed-interval against band rebalancing]\label{meth:transaction-cost-hedging}
\qffield{Statement} With a bid-offer spread on the underlying, continuous rebalancing would cost an unbounded amount, so a hedger rebalances at intervals and pays for each trade. The option then has no single value: costs are always a drain, so a hedger values a long position below the Black--Scholes value and a short position above it, and the amounts depend on the hedger's own costs. The modelling was initiated by Leland (1985). In Leland's model the portfolio is rebalanced to the delta every $\delta t$; buying or selling $\nu$ shares at $S$ costs $\kappa|\nu|S$; and the hedged portfolio's expected return is set equal to the risk-free rate. Long calls and puts are then valued with $\check\sigma^2=\sigma^2-2\kappa\sigma\sqrt{2/(\pi\delta t)}$ and short ones with $\hat\sigma^2=\sigma^2+2\kappa\sigma\sqrt{2/(\pi\delta t)}$, eqs.~(48.5)--(48.6) \citep[chap.~48, secs.~48.1--48.4, pp.~783--789]{wide-wilmott-pwoqf}.

The derivation explains the signs. Over one step the shares traded are, to leading order, $\nu\approx\Gamma\,\delta S=\Gamma\sigma S\phi\sqrt{\delta t}$, so the expected cost is $\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\kappa\sigma S^2|\Gamma|\sqrt{\delta t}$, eq.~(48.2). Subtracting it from the portfolio's expected return gives the Hoggard--Whalley--Wilmott equation $V_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}-\kappa\sigma S^2\sqrt{2/(\pi\delta t)}\,|V_{SS}|+rSV_S-rV=0$, eq.~(48.4). For a single long vanilla option $\Gamma>0$, the modulus drops and the equation is Black--Scholes at $\check\sigma$. For a short one every sign changes except the cost term's, giving $\hat\sigma$. A hedger selling after a rise sells at the bid, so the effective price moves less than the market: the long position sees a lower volatility (pp.~786--788; appendix, pp.~809--811).

The equation is nonlinear, so a portfolio's value is not the sum of its parts. Long and short the same call, hedged separately, lose on both hedges, while hedged together they never trade. For a portfolio whose gamma changes sign the modulus cannot be dropped and the equation must be solved numerically. The marginal effect of adding a contract depends on the sign of the existing portfolio's gamma: adding gamma of the opposite sign reduces costs (secs.~48.5--48.6 and 48.10.1, pp.~790--793 and 797).

\qffield{Notation} $\kappa$ one-way proportional cost; Interpretation: if trades fill at mid plus or minus half the spread, $\kappa$ is half the proportional bid-offer spread. $\delta t$ the rebalancing interval; Wilmott's non-dimensional cost $\mathcal K=\kappa/(\sigma\sqrt{\delta t})$, eq.~(48.7), written $K$ there. Large $\mathcal K$ means rehedging too often for the costs, small $\mathcal K$ too rarely (pp.~788--789). For small costs the long--short difference per side is about $2\kappa SN'(d_1)\sqrt{T-t}/\sqrt{2\pi\delta t}$, the volatility change times vega (p.~788). Interpretation: by It\^o's formula along paths of volatility $\sigma$, exactly $\hat V-V=E^Q\int_0^Te^{-rt}\kappa\sigma\sqrt{2/(\pi\delta t)}\,S_t^2\hat\Gamma_t\,dt$, where $\hat V$ and $\hat\Gamma$ are at $\hat\sigma$. The Leland premium is therefore the expected present value of the leading-order cost of hedging with the $\hat\sigma$ delta; the long discount $V-\check V$ is the same with $\check\Gamma$.

\qffield{Conditions} Leland's assumptions are rebalancing at a fixed, finite, small interval, costs proportional to the value traded, normal returns and valuation by the hedged portfolio's expected return. Choosing $\Delta=\partial V/\partial S$ minimises the leading-order variance but leaves a residual risk. The adjusted volatility applies only to positions whose gamma has one sign. The long formula needs $\check\sigma^2>0$, which by (48.5) means $\kappa<\sigma\sqrt{\pi\delta t/8}$, or $\mathcal K<\sqrt{\pi/8}\approx0.63$. Beyond that the equation is forward parabolic with final data and so ill-posed. Wilmott's p.~789 prints the threshold as $\kappa>\sigma/\sqrt{8\pi\delta t}$, a form that is dimensionally inconsistent with (48.5); the condition above follows from (48.5) directly. The formulas omit the trade that opens the hedge and any closing trade, and they are leading-order in $\sqrt{\delta t}$. For non-normal returns what matters is $E|\psi|$ of the standardised return; Wilmott's factors for selected assets are 0.50 to 0.96 against 1 for the normal (Table~48.1, p.~800). With fixed costs per trade the model can give negative values unless hedging stops below a boundary (sec.~48.10.2, p.~798). Wilmott's empirical comparisons use daily data for 1985--1996.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Fixed interval against a band.} Costs fall and hedging risk rises as the interval lengthens: expected costs scale like $1/\sqrt{\delta t}$ and the hedging error's standard deviation like $\sqrt{\delta t}$, so a confidence-level price has an optimal interval (sec.~48.13, pp.~807--808). A delta-tolerance rule watches the price continuously and rebalances to the delta only when $\sigma S|D-\partial V/\partial S|$ exceeds a bandwidth $H_0$, eq.~(48.9), giving a different nonlinear equation, (48.10). Utility-based models (Hodges--Neuberger; Davis--Panas--Zariphopoulou) find an optimal no-trade band around the delta and trade only to its edge. Whalley and Wilmott's small-cost asymptotics make the band symmetric, with a width proportional to the cube root of the cost coefficient (secs.~48.8--48.9, pp.~793--797).

In Jefferies' tests of an at-the-money one-year call (volatility 20 percent, rate 5 percent, $S=100$, each cost coefficient 0.01), the lowest 95th-percentile prices on simulated paths were 14.90 for Leland hedging every four days, 15.03 for the utility band and 15.15 for delta tolerance. On real daily data for nearly 40 stocks, delta tolerance gave the lowest cost on about 70 percent of paths, the utility band on 23 percent and Leland on 7. Wilmott concludes that it is ``not at all clear which is the best strategy'' (sec.~48.12, pp.~800--806).

Examples, synthetic: an at-the-money call with $S_0=K=100$, $\sigma=20$ percent, $r=5$ percent, $q=0$ and $T=1$. Risk-neutral paths, 100{,}000, physical settlement, opening trade excluded; standard errors of simulated costs 0.0007 to 0.005.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Leland premium against simulated cost.} At $\mathcal K=0.1$, $\hat\sigma=21.54$ and $\check\sigma=18.33$ percent. The short premium $\hat V-V$ is 0.5777 and the long discount $V-\check V$ 0.6233. Keeping $\mathcal K$ fixed with $\kappa=0.555$, 0.277 and 0.139 percent, the simulated present value of the $\hat\sigma$ hedge's costs over 13, 52 and 208 rebalances is 0.6177, 0.5902 and 0.5816. These exceed the premium by 6.9, 2.2 and 0.7 percent, a residual that shrinks about threefold per fourfold refinement. For the long position the residuals are 5.1, 1.6 and 0.5 percent; at $\mathcal K=0.4$, 9.5, 3.0 and 0.9 (short) and 1.9, 0.6 and 0.2 (long).
\item \emph{What the formula leaves out.} At the Leland price the writer's expected loss equals that residual. Opening the weekly hedge costs another 0.18, more than the residual. The settlement trade costs 0.017, against 0.18 for liquidating the hedge in a cash-settled contract.
\item \emph{Fixed cost, varying frequency.} At $\kappa=0.2$ percent with the Black--Scholes delta, 13, 52 and 208 rebalances cost 0.23, 0.44 and 0.87 and leave a profit-and-loss standard deviation of 1.77, 0.93 and 0.58. The premium that loses only 5 percent of the time is 13.7, 12.5 and 12.4, against a Black--Scholes value of 10.45.
\item \emph{Netting, a hedge-cost demonstration.} A bull spread, long the 95 call and short the 105 call, hedged weekly at $\kappa=0.2$ percent with Black--Scholes deltas: hedging the legs separately costs 0.854 in present value and trading only the net delta 0.247, a saving of 0.607 (standard error 0.0007). Netting never costs more on any step. Long and short the same call cost twice one hedge separately and nothing together. Valuing the legs separately with Leland's volatilities gives 4.49, against 5.33 without costs and 5.08 for the Black--Scholes spread less the netted cost. The last is an approximate value under a fixed delta rule, not a solution of (48.4).
\end{itemize}

\qffield{Pitfalls} Using the raised volatility for a long position, or the lowered one for a short. Applying the adjusted volatility to a spread or book whose gamma changes sign. Valuing a book as the sum of separately cost-adjusted options. Rehedging as often as possible, or using the long formula with $\mathcal K\ge\sqrt{\pi/8}$. Forgetting that the formula excludes the opening and closing trades.
