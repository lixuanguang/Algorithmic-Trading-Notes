\section{VIX, variance swaps and dividend swaps}

\begin{qfdefinition}[The VIX and its futures curve, variance swaps (strike, vega notional, variance units and payoff) and dividend swaps, valuation and settlement]\label{def:vix-variance-dividend-swaps}
\qffield{Statement} \emph{VIX.} Gliner describes the VIX as derived from S\&P 500 options ``one month out'', negatively correlated with equity returns, able to move ``from the mid-teens to 30 in a matter of days'' in sell-offs; VIX futures ``typically trade in contango'' and move to backwardation when spot spikes; VIX options are in effect options on the volatility of volatility; and other indices include V2X, VXN, VXD, RVX, GVZ and OVX \citep[chap.~8, sec.\ ``The Volatility Index (VIX)'', PDF pp.~222--225]{s7-gliner}.

\emph{Variance swaps} give ``pure variance exposure'' without delta hedging and without the interest-rate and dividend exposure of options; they are quoted by strike, vega notional, variance units and maturity (pp.~225--226). Variance units $=$ vega notional$/(2K)$ and payoff $=(\sigma_R^2-K^2)\times$ variance units: with $K=24$, vega notional \$1,000,000 and realised volatility 29, there are 20,833 units and a payoff of \$5,520,833 (p.~226).

\emph{Dividend swaps} let one take a view that an index's dividends will be higher or lower than a fixed amount, OTC for the S\&P 500 and listed for the Euro Stoxx 50 (sec.\ ``Dividend Swaps'', p.~227).

\emph{How the VIX is computed}. Cboe's methodology defines the index as $\text{VIX}=100\times\sigma$, where $\sigma^2$ is an annualised 30-day variance: for each of a near and a next expiry, $\sigma_j^2=\frac{2}{T_j}\sum_i\frac{\Delta K_i}{K_i^2}e^{R_jT_j}Q(K_i)-\frac{1}{T_j}\bigl(\frac{F_j}{K_0}-1\bigr)^2$ over out-of-the-money SPX and SPXW option mid-quotes $Q(K_i)$, the two variances are interpolated to a constant 30 days, and ``Then take the square root of that value and multiply by 100''; time is measured in minutes, with 43,200 minutes in 30 days and 525,600 in a year \citep[sec.~3.1, pp.~4--6, and app.~3, pp.~9--14]{gap-cboe-vix6}.

So the VIX is quoted in volatility points (percent per year), not variance: VIX~20 means $\sigma=0.20$ and an annualised variance of $0.20^2=0.04$, or $0.04\times30/365\approx0.0033$ over the 30 days. The index's first value date is January 1990 and the current methodology dates from 2003 (launch 22 September 2003), when the 1993 index on S\&P 100 at-the-money implied volatility was replaced (pp.~3 and~8).

\qffield{Notation} $K$ strike in volatility points; $\sigma_R$ realised volatility over the life, annualised from daily log returns, in points; vega notional is the approximate P\&L for a one-point move in realised volatility near the strike.

\qffield{Conditions} Gliner's examples as of 2013. \emph{The strike is fixed at inception from option prices.} Gliner writes that variance swaps ``are priced with realized volatility, which gives the buyer slightly more edge'' (p.~225); the payoff depends on realised variance, but the strike is set at the start from the market's implied variance and is usually above subsequent realised variance (the variance risk premium), so the long typically pays: with strike 24 and realised 20 the long loses \$3,666,667 (\nolinkurl{notes/artefacts/multi-asset-systematic-macro/macro-pricing-risk-checks.py}, section~13). The payoff is convex in volatility, linear in variance.

\qffield{Practice} \emph{Daily moves.} With $\sqrt{252}=15.87$, a volatility of 16 corresponds to a daily standard deviation of about 1 percent and 24 to about 1.5 percent (Gliner, p.~226; checks, section~13); that is a standard deviation, not the ``expected daily move'', whose mean absolute size under normality is about 0.8 of it. \emph{Dividend swap example} (p.~227): with the 2014 S\&P 500 dividend quoted at \$31 against an index of 1,350 (an implied yield of 2.3 percent), a buyer of 500,000 units gains $(36-31)\times500{,}000=\$2.5$ million if the market moves to \$36; Gliner's ``baskets of 100,000'' sits uneasily with that arithmetic, which counts one index unit per basket.

\qffield{Pitfalls} Gliner's description, that the VIX ``is extrapolated by using various strikes of implied volatility'' (p.~222), does not match Cboe's methodology above, which aggregates option \emph{prices} across strikes into a variance, the formula of the variance-swap replication literature (it cites Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal and Zou), and takes its square root; implied volatilities are not inputs. Squaring a VIX level gives a variance; adding or averaging VIX levels across maturities is not the same as averaging variances. The rule ``daily move $\approx$ VIX$/\sqrt{252}$'' mixes Cboe's calendar-time annualisation with a 252-trading-day year and treats all variance as falling on trading days; it is an approximation. Gliner's ``V2X Index---Euro Stoxx 500'' should read Euro Stoxx 50.

``VIX options provide asymmetric hedges'' omits what they settle on: VIX futures and options settle to a Special Opening Quotation computed on expiration morning from a single SPX or SPXW expiry 30 calendar days later, without the near/next interpolation of the spot index (sec.~3.4, p.~7), so an option's payoff is not tied to the spot VIX on the day it is bought.
