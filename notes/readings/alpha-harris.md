## Key results

- Market value against fundamental value, the expected present value of holding the instrument given everything known; noise is their difference; fundamental-value changes unpredictable (chap. 10, pp. 222–223). Stale information and pseudo-informed traders (p. 229).
- Informed traders move prices towards their estimates; algebraic box: the price is the average forecast under linear demands and zero net supply, its error vanishing with independent errors (p. 225); weighted averages and the statistical calculator (p. 226); orthogonal estimates (p. 237).
- Market paradox (pp. 238–239); weak, semistrong and strong forms and the cost-sensitive definition (p. 240); the trade-off between liquidity and informative prices, with publication of information as the exception (pp. 241–242).
- Value traders profit only from uninformed liquidity demand; news traders profit from information changes (chap. 16, pp. 338–339); "If you are not a buyer, should you be a seller?" (box, p. 343).
- Inside information: material information not available to public traders; the SEC's 2002 summary (box, p. 585); insiders, tippees, misappropriation (pp. 584–587); employee pre-approval and trading windows (p. 588); detection (pp. 588–590); restrictions narrow spreads and make prices less informative in the short run (pp. 597–598).
- Informed-trader types: value, news, information-oriented technical, arbitrageurs (sec. 10.5, Table 10-3, p. 235); aggressive against stealth trading (sec. 10.4, pp. 225–226).
- Order anticipators: front runners (quote matchers against passive traders), sentiment-oriented technical traders, squeezers; gunning stop orders; time precedence with a meaningful tick protects liquidity suppliers (chap. 11, pp. 245–257).
- Bluffers: rumourmongers and price manipulators (wash trades a technique); profitable when buy and sell impacts differ, with the worked example on p. 270; value traders call bluffs; manipulation hard to prove (chap. 12, pp. 259–274).
- Arbitrage: hedge portfolio, basis, fair value, arbitrage spread, bounds (p. 349); pure (shipping, delivery, conversion, cyclic) against speculative (spreads, pairs, statistical, merger) (pp. 350–363); four risks and staying power (pp. 363–373); slow adjustment against uninformed demand (pp. 373–375); price and quantity characterisations (pp. 375–376).
- Winner's curse: bid lower with more bidders and harder valuation; market power with very few (pp. 341–343).
- Specialist privileges as of 2002: order-flow speculation, quote matching, cream skimming, stop-order anticipation, stopping stock (sec. 24.5, pp. 501–508).
- Insider trading: detection by surveillance and list matching (pp. 588–590); deterrence by probability times penalty (p. 590); competition among insiders (p. 592).
- Inventory imbalance, and the two inventory risks: diversifiable (independent of the imbalance) and adverse selection (inversely correlated with it) (pp. 283–286).
- Dealers seek market values that produce two-sided flow, and discover fundamental values as a by-product (pp. 284, 286–287).
- Tactics: Table 13-2 for inventory (p. 285), Table 13-3 after a suspected informed trade (p. 288), Table 13-4 for consequences when trading with informed and uninformed buyers (p. 292).
- Adverse-selection spread component: quotes conditional on the side of the next trade (pp. 289–290); the information and accounting views agree, shown in a simple example based on Glosten–Milgrom, $a-b=2PE$ (pp. 300–302, 320–321). P. 321 misprints the informed-buyer profit as $A-(V-E)$.
- Spread determinants: asymmetric information, volatility, utilitarian trading interest (pp. 311–313); secondary proxies (pp. 314–317); market failure and private financing (p. 317).
- Total spread: ask $=V^B+C/2$, bid $=V^S-C/2$ (Figure 14-1, p. 301); both quotes rise by half the adverse-selection component after a buy (Figure 14-2, pp. 301–302). Two components only; the inventory premium sits in the transaction-cost component (pp. 298–299).
- Uninformed traders lose whichever order type they use (sec. 14.3, pp. 303–304, Table 14-1).
- Price-benchmark cost estimators: decision-time midpoint (Perold's implementation shortfall; Treynor's paper portfolios), VWAP, open, close and the average of open, high, low and close; consultants' benchmarks as of 2002 (pp. 424–426). Implementation shortfall as paper minus actual portfolio, with the filled and missed-trade parts (p. 426). Noise, trade timing, split-order, momentum, informed-trader and gaming properties (sec. 21.4, pp. 427–432, Table 21-1, p. 433); worked split-order example recomputed (\$400 against decision midpoint, zero against VWAP, −\$200 against the close). Missed-trade opportunity cost, its horizon and realistic order sizes; the DINE example recomputed (\$12,600 and \$714,600) (sec. 21.6, pp. 434–436). Plexus components and their 2002 basis-point estimates (box, p. 437).
- Buy-side order submission: display questions for large traders (p. 380); reactive against proactive traders and the separate aggressive–passive continuum (sec. 18.3, pp. 383–384); evasive, deceptive and offensive strategies (sec. 18.5, pp. 386–389).
- Program trades (15 or more coordinated transactions worth \$1 million, about 27 percent of NYSE volume as of 2002, p. 489); the firm bid/offer (package, basket, portfolio) market, its characterisation and close-relative pricing (sec. 23.4.1, pp. 490–491); index-fund rebalancing and trading tactics that raise tracking error (sec. 23.2, pp. 486–487).
- Portfolio insurance as a dynamic replicated put that trades like stop orders; the overhang box (about \$100 billion insured, delta about 0.4, 10 million shares per 1 percent fall against 160 million ADV) and the ratcheting box (sec. 28.2.2, pp. 560–561). The box sizes rebalancing by delta rather than by the change in hedge ratio (own check).
- Order-driven equilibrium spread: indifference between limit and market orders; Table 14-2 (p. 311); management cost plus twice the timing option (p. 307).
- Outside spread of value traders and why it exceeds dealer spreads (chap. 16, pp. 340–345).
- Roll's estimator, $S=2\sqrt{-\mathrm{SCov}}$ (box, p. 415, page image); typically biased down by violations, use only without quotes (p. 434).
- Fundamental volatility from unexpected value changes, transitory volatility from impatient uninformed trading, which reverts; total = sum; negative serial correlation a strong indicator; perishable spot prices an exception; regulators can affect only the transitory part (chap. 20, pp. 410–416).
- Bubbles and crashes: five-phase dynamics, price accelerator (box after Treynor, p. 556), most crashes correct earlier mispricing (box p. 559); the 1987 crash (pp. 559–563); circuit breakers' net effect on transitory volatility indeterminate (secs. 28.3.1–28.3.4, pp. 572–577).
- Effective spread = twice the signed difference from the time-of-trade midpoint; realised spread from a later midpoint (pp. 424–425); quoted − effective = price improvement, effective − realised = losses to informed traders (pp. 425–426). "Realised spread" also means a dealer's round-trip outcome (p. 280).

## Open questions

- The box on p. 225 says the market's forecast error is below the average individual error whenever the individual errors are not identical; that holds for squared errors but not, in general, for absolute ones (own check, slice 5). Is a mean-squared sense intended? Update (slice 6, page image checked): realisation by realisation only the squared comparison is strict whenever errors differ; in expectation, with independent nondegenerate errors, the absolute one is strict too. The closing claim that the price beats any individual trader needs equal accuracies.
