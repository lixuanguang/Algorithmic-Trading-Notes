## Key results

- Four liquidity variables: volume, spread, volatility, book size; their intraday seasonality, high after the open (Figure 2.12, pp. 145–148); at a one-tick spread, book size adjusts instead of the spread (p. 148).
- Cross-sectional spread relation $\psi_i=a+b\sigma_i/\sqrt{N_i}$ for small-tick instruments; for large-tick ones $\psi_i=a+(b_1\sigma_i+b_2\eta\,\mathrm{tick})/\sqrt{N_i}$, both terms over $\sqrt{N_i}$, with bounce parameter $\eta=N^c/(2N^a)$ (eq. (2.1), pp. 144–145, page image of p. 145 checked in the slice-19 follow-up).
- Daily spread rises with daily volatility (Figure 2.13, pp. 150–151); spread on volatility in levels and logs, with the log-normal back-transformation (app. A.10.2, pp. 295–298; Rio Tinto, $R^2$ 0.24, 0.16, 0.11).
- Garman–Klass volatility normalised by the average price (p. 172, page image); used for seasonality and for the FTSE 100 / IBEX 35 comparison, which found no significant effect of UK fragmentation on intraday volatility (pp. 146–147, 172–174).
- Intraday impact in spreads from the arrival price, concave during execution and reverting after (Figure 3.3, pp. 204–206, CAC 40, 2010); impact $\propto$ Duration$^\alpha\times$Participation$^\gamma$, $\gamma\approx0.5$ (eq. (3.1), pp. 207–209); drivers and timing (pp. 203–206); restatement of Bacry et al.'s post-execution study with home indexes and least-squares beta (eq. (3.2), pp. 209–215).
- Optimal trade scheduling (app. A.7, pp. 273–281): temporary impact $\kappa\sigma_n(v/V_n)^\gamma$ (A.17), expectation-only schedule $\propto V_n/\sigma_n^{1/\gamma}$ (A.22), variance-only (A.25), mean–variance (A.26) and the $\gamma=1$ recurrence (A.28); two layers of a trading algorithm (p. 274); Cartea–Jaimungal stochastic control (A.7.3, pp. 281–284; see the next item). The appendix mixes timing conventions ((A.16) against (A.27)) and mislabels Proposition 5 (own check).
- Cartea–Jaimungal framework as presented in app. A.7.3, pp. 281–284 (page images; slice 16): objective, dynamics with exogenous flow, Proposition 6 and its quadratic ansatz. Own checks: $\min$ printed for $\sup$; half-spread in the terminal condition but not the objective; $-\partial_q$ printed for $-\nu\partial_q$; "bid–ask spread $\phi$" for $\psi$; the closed form for $h_2$ gives $h_2(T)=A-b$ (sign of $\xi$ reversed); the boxed $h_1$ omits the factor $b$. Follow-up (own checks, same day): with the corrected terminal payoff the reward is finite under, for example, $\kappa>0$, $\phi>0$, $A>b/2$, but the appendix's unconstrained problem can have infinite value (constant buying with $b/2>\kappa+A+\phi/3$); $\phi\int Q^2$ matches only the Brownian part of shortfall variance when the flow is stochastic. AC for a 5–30-minute scale, CJ for continuous-time flow dynamics (p. 284).
- Benchmarks of trading algorithms by region (US stealth, Asia cross-market comparison, Europe detection and routing), "on paper" IS, VWAP or PoV and liquidity seekers, adjusted by order size; Table 3.1 (order size, market context, hedged risk, p. 229) and Table 3.2 (stock, trade and features, p. 230); the risk–reward reading of the hedged-risk column (sec. 3.4.1, pp. 227–228).
- Components of a trading algorithm: execution parameters, risk-control layer producing a trading curve and envelope, trading robots and liquidity-seeking tactics (sec. 3.1.2, pp. 197–198); parameters estimated daily, variables in real time; dark orders as probes; recorded messages (pp. 196–197); pre-trade analytics, monitoring and performance indicators, the average-price decomposition and what-if scenarios (sec. 3.1.3, pp. 198–201). Combining benchmarks by constraints as bang-bang control, trading envelopes and two-step customisation (sec. 3.4.1, pp. 231–233). The trading-curve reference to app. A.6 should be A.7; Figure 3.14's caption lacks its second algorithm name (own checks). Compatible pre-trade, monitoring and post-trade indicators; post-trade (one day) against TCA (months), routing flow by market context, liquidity breakdowns (pp. 202–203). The average-price decomposition (p. 201) cites (A.26), which is an ex-ante objective (p. 279); its realised counterpart was checked by simulation (own check).
- Trading architecture (Figure 3.1, p. 194) and the inputs of trading tools (sec. 3.1.1, pp. 194–197): consolidated market data with analytics computed as soon as raw data arrive; two-way venue connections, faster than and not synchronised with market data, returning acknowledgements, rejections, cancellations and partial fills; dark fills as probes; historical data with recorded messages and context; models as formula, estimation and accuracy computation, with deactivation. Algorithms as indicator publishers, called "stateless agents" with introspection (p. 202, page image; slice 17).
- Smart order routing (sec. 3.4.2): venue-specific marketability; U.S. trade-through rule against European best-execution policies as of 2018 (p. 234); three-broker example, Table 3.3 (pp. 235–236; B1 101.15 and B3 100.75 recomputed, B2 printed 102.50 for 102.05); fee against tick threshold and Figure 3.16 (pp. 236–238); latency and mirrored liquidity (pp. 238–239); passive split, Tables 3.4–3.6, eq. (3.4) printed with $D-\sum Q_n$ for $D+\sum Q_n$ (pp. 239–242); liquidity seekers and fill-rate curves (pp. 242–244).
- High-frequency market makers as cross-venue arbitrageurs; low latency lowers their inventory risk; a venue's low "flow reactivity" read as liquidity withdrawn when activity rises (p. 158). Coverage of less liquid stocks rose 2009–2012 as competition forced high-frequency market makers to diversify; hedging and short-selling costs set a threshold (pp. 161–162). The SEC's five HFT characteristics, quoted from its 2010 concept release; HFT shares of 80, 40 and 35 percent "said to be" (p. 163).
- Stylised model of a fast trader quoting inside the book: the effective spread narrows but other traders' cost per trade stays at or above its earlier level (Figure 2.21, pp. 164–165); $\mathbb E[C]=(\psi_B+r\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}})/2$ and its variance (Figure 2.22, p. 166); calibration $\psi_B=5.41$, $\psi_N=4.73$ bp, CAC 40, Euronext Paris, 2007 and 2011, $q=40\%$ assumed, implying $\psi_{\mathrm{HFT}}=3.71$ bp; the text's "approximately 0.5bp" rise from $r=30\%$ to $50\%$ is 0.37 bp by its formula and Figure 2.24 (own check, slice 15).
- Number of trades per day as a proxy for HFT activity (Figure 2.25, p. 171).
- Price formation process: the impact of each trade plus the variation of the fair price (after Bouchaud et al. 2002); immediate impact, then decay or resilience (Figure 8, pp. 27–29); temporary impact when pressure contradicts consensus, permanent when it agrees (pp. 3, 28); mainly driven by information (p. 7); more information should improve it, but traders fear leakage (pp. 19–20).
- Extraday and intraday volatility as two scales (sec. 2.5, p. 169); intraday and overnight returns and correlations, eqs. (2.2)–(2.3) (p. 175); correlation spikes mainly overnight, intraday correlation a baseline, highest since 1991 in October 2010; the opening fixing less noisy than the closing one (pp. 174–177).
- Euronext outage of 13 October 2010: MTFs pegged to the primary kept trading, mid-points stopped, deviations of about 2 percent, "as if two price formation processes took place in parallel"; the figure captions date it 10 October (pp. 183–186).
- Flash Crash account: volume-following sell algorithm, hot-potato trading between futures and equities, paused orders, internalised retail flow routed to the market, NYSE LRPs, post-crash rules (sec. 2.5.2, pp. 177–183).
- Toy model of the Flash Crash: overflow $r_t=F_t-\bar F$, impact $I=1/D$ with $dI_t/I_t=\sigma_IdW_t+r_tdt$ (A.12), price $dP=\sigma_PdW^P+I^+dR^+-I^-dR^-$ (A.13), hot-potato rate $r_t\simeq(1-\rho^t)r_0/(1-\rho)$; the explicit solution (A.14) has a $t^2$ exponent that does not follow from $R_t\simeq tr_0/(1-\rho)$ (own check, open) (app. A.5, pp. 261–266).

## Open questions

- Is (A.14) a misprint for an exponent linear in $t$, or does the model intend $dI_t/I_t$ to depend on $R_t$ rather than $r_t$? Answered by derivation in slice 36: the $t^2$ exponent is $\int_0^tR_s\,ds$, so (A.14) solves the equation with the overflow $R$, as the gloss on p. 262 suggests, not (A.12) with the rate; and the hot-potato $t$ counts rounds, not clock time (`mod:depth-overflow-flash-crash`).
- Which date is right for the Euronext outage, the text's 13 October 2010 or the captions' 10 October 2010 (a Sunday)? Slice 36 takes the text's date (a Wednesday) and records the captions.
- What does "efficient" mean in eq. (A.4)? The text does not say; slice 36 reproduces it as the trade-maximising (and venue-revenue-maximising) split of a fixed total fee, which Foucault, Kadan and Kandel's paper should confirm.
