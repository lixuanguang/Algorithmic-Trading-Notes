Bessent (chap. 13 in 2006, chap. 12 "The Stock Operator" in the 2013 EPUB), read in context in both editions on 2026-10-18:

- Bottom-up research: Soros Fund Management "did a lot of bottom-up research"; "Stan and George believed that if you can find out what's going on at a micro level, you can figure out the big picture" (2006 p. 274; 2013, answer to "So you were running the Soros London office when the pound fell out of the ERM. Can you talk a bit about what happened on that famous trade?").
- Entry discipline: "I don't usually have stop-losses. One of the things that I learned from Stan Druckenmiller is how to enter a trade"; he tries to make his entry point "good enough that I rarely stop myself out of a trade" (2006 p. 285; 2013, answer to "When you initiate a trade, do you have profit targets and stop-loss levels?"). His own practice and a lesson he attributes to Druckenmiller; no results are given.
- Paradigm shifts: "Soros used to say, 'Don't try to play the game better, try to figure out when the game has changed'"; "Catching a paradigm shift is where you make a lot of money because everyone is still focusing on what mattered" (2006 p. 285; 2013, answer to "Do interest rates matter once again for currencies? What the market focuses on in foreign exchange seems to be constantly changing."). The advice is Soros's as Bessent reports it.
- Also in both: 50/30/20 (2006 p. 280; 2013 after the interviewer's remark beginning "I find it difficult to foresee the day when global macro won't matter") and 8 to 14 positions (2006 p. 283; 2013, answer to "Let's talk about portfolio construction. How do you structure your portfolio in normal times?").
- Differences found: the 2006 question "What is your favorite trade at the moment?" reads "What is your best trade at the moment?" in 2013; the quoted answers are unchanged.

## Limits

Survivorship and selection: successful managers describing their own rules. Attributions: the "75 percent of price movement" remark, long-gamma leverage, never being a forced seller and buying options when an outcome is "inevitable" are the anonymous currency specialist's (pp. 331-336), not Siva-Jothy's; 4:1 risk/reward and the stop rules are Andres Drobny's (pp. 131-132), not Wadhwani's; his cooling-off of about a month applies after a portfolio-wide stop-out and big losses ("On a specific trade, no"), while a single stopped trade may be re-entered with a very tight stop and a worse risk/reward (4:1 becoming 4:3 after two stops); three or four major opportunities a year, overtrading and the 50-60 percent options share ("I always structure my portfolio to be 50 to 60 percent options", no denominator stated) are Siva-Jothy's (p. 85), the three-or-four-trades framing at p. 125 is the interviewer's, which Andres Drobny accepts; Wadhwani's re-entry rule is a 24-hour break (p. 177); time-horizon arbitrage is Porter's (p. 135); front-page and magazine-cover contrarianism is Rogers's (pp. 217, 230) and Porter's (p. 152), not Anderson's. Porter's "the yield curve is 90 percent explained by the direction of interest rates" (pp. 147-148); it does not make a steepener 90 percent bullish. The anonymous manager's three blocs are Europe, Asia and Latin America (p. 328).
