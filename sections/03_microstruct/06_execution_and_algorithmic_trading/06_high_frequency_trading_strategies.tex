\subsubsection{High-Frequency Trading Strategies}

\begin{taxonomy} \hlt{HFT Strategy Segmentation}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \hlt{passive market making}: quote both sides, earn spread and rebates, manage inventory;
\item \hlt{statistical arbitrage}: trade short-horizon relative value or lead-lag signals;
\item \hlt{latency arbitrage}: react faster to stale quotes or delayed cross-venue price updates;
\item \hlt{order anticipation}: infer large parent orders from child-order footprints;
\item \hlt{liquidity detection}: probe venues and dark pools for hidden or fleeting interest.
\end{enumerate}
The same firm may run several of these, but the risk and social value differ by strategy.
\end{taxonomy}

\begin{principle} \hlt{HFT Edge Decays With Diffusion}\\
Speed advantages are valuable only while competitors, venues, and clients cannot replicate them.\\
Once reaction speed and routing logic diffuse, durable profits must come from inventory control, better models, lower costs, or privileged order flow.
\end{principle}

\begin{caveat} \hlt{Message Profit Is Not Market Quality}\\
High message rates can improve displayed prices, but they can also increase cancellation risk and stale-book uncertainty. Evaluate HFT by spreads, depth, realised costs, adverse selection, and stress behaviour.
\end{caveat}
